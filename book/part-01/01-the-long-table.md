# Chapter One — The Long Table

*Merrow, Republic of Liora. Late summer. Six years before the dissolution.*

The first thing to disappear was the long table.

It was made from six folding tables, and nobody could remember whose they were. Every August they emerged from garages along Rowan Way and were carried into the street, where they were covered with mismatched cloths and loaded with food. There was always too much potato salad. Someone always burned the onions. Every year, someone said they should buy a proper table; every year, nobody did.

On the last Saturday of August, when the air was warm enough to make the evening seem endless, Mara Arden stood in her kitchen with a bowl balanced against her hip and watched her husband try to explain an electrical problem to their son.

“Electricity doesn't come out of the wall because it's angry,” Tomas said.

“I didn't say angry. I said it's trying to escape.”

“From where?”

“From being electricity.”

Tomas considered this. “Fair point.”

Ivo was eleven, old enough to recognize when adults had stopped answering a question, but not yet old enough to mind. He liked the way his father made the ordinary world sound complicated and his mother made complicated things sound manageable.

“Your uncle Dain is coming,” Mara said. “Ask him. He knows everything about wires.”

“Dain knows everything about telling people he knows everything about wires,” Tomas replied.

The brothers worked on opposite sides of the same river. Tomas maintained signaling equipment for the southern railway. Dain repaired substations for the municipal power company. One believed most large public services ought to remain in public hands. The other believed the country could use more private businesses and fewer men in offices deciding what a wrench should cost. They had been arguing about it since their first jobs, and it had never prevented them from borrowing one another's tools.

By seven, Rowan Way belonged to its residents rather than its cars.

Sela Orin came with three loaves of dark bread and a jar of pickles that no one would admit to liking but that always vanished. Her wife Lev, who worked at the district clinic, carried a small folding chair because her back was bad. The Kellers arrived with their elderly dog. Young Fen from number sixteen brought a battered speaker, and Mrs. Kade from number four immediately asked him to turn it down.

He did. Mrs. Kade was ninety and had once taught half the street to read.

On the far end of the table stood the town's mayor, who was only there because his sister lived on Rowan Way. Everyone ignored him except to complain about the pothole near the bus stop.

“It's scheduled,” the mayor said.

“It was scheduled when my knees still worked,” Mrs. Kade told him.

The table laughed.

This was how politics usually appeared in Liora: as a practical inconvenience, an argument over the distribution of money, a complaint addressed to someone who would still be expected to pass the bread.

There were serious divisions in the country. Housing had become expensive. A factory outside Merrow had announced another round of layoffs. The schools were being asked to do more with less. People could be selfish, prejudiced, vain, cruel. Some newspapers lied. Some politicians were thieves. Liora was not innocent; it was simply accustomed to the idea that its troubles belonged to everyone who lived there.

In the middle of the meal, the power went out.

The speaker stopped. Porch lamps blinked away. Twenty-two houses vanished into a soft, blue-black evening.

“Dain!” someone called.

“I don't work for free after seven,” Dain said.

A moment later he was on his feet, Tomas beside him, both searching the row of houses to see whether the outage extended beyond the street. Children produced little circles of light from their phones. Lev went inside to check on a neighbor who used an oxygen concentrator, though the neighbor's backup battery was charged. Someone brought a lantern. Then another.

The street did not become frightened or even particularly quiet. They kept eating.

Dain came back first.

“Substation fault,” he said. “They'll have it in an hour.”

“Who is *they*?” Tomas asked.

“People who know more than you.”

The brothers clinked their bottles together.

Mara watched them and felt a happiness so unremarkable she did not think to name it. The smell of warm bread; Ivo leaning against her arm; Tomas in the darkness trying to steal the last grilled pepper; Sela crossing the road to bring Mrs. Kade a blanket. None of it seemed historical. Nothing worth protecting ever does while it is still intact.

Later, when the lights came back, Fen turned on the speaker. The old song that came out was one almost everyone knew, although half the street insisted they hated it. People sang anyway. A few danced. Dain took Mara's hand and spun her once, badly, so that she almost knocked over the mayonnaise.

“You're supposed to warn me,” she said.

“Then it wouldn't count.”

Tomas watched from his chair with the easy possessiveness of a man who had never worried that his brother and wife might stop being friends.

At midnight they took the table apart. The Kellers carried two sections home. Tomas and Dain carried the third and fourth; they argued about whether one of the hinges had been bent last year. The remainder was stacked inside Mara's garage. Nobody wrote down who owed what to whom.

On her way inside, Sela found a photograph on her phone. She had taken it earlier, when everyone was leaning across the food and laughing at something Mrs. Kade had said. The photograph caught twenty-six faces, some hidden, some blurred. It was not a good photograph. Someone had moved while it was taken. A child had both fingers in his nose.

Sela sent it to the neighborhood group on a social platform called Lantern.

**Same time next year?** she wrote.

There were thirty-one little hearts by breakfast.

Far to the north, beyond the mountains and the sea, an army was shelling a country called Tarven.

In Liora, the war was on the evening news. The children collected money for displaced families, parliament condemned the invasion, and people argued about whether sanctions were enough. For a while there had been demonstrations. Then life, as life does, resumed around the horror on the other side of the map.

At the breakfast table, Mara read the latest reports while Tomas sliced bread.

“It's getting worse,” she said.

“I know.”

“What if they come farther south?”

Tomas glanced at Ivo, who was constructing a fortress out of cereal pieces.

“That's a long way,” he said.

He didn't know whether it was true. He only knew what a father was supposed to say.

Mara put the phone down. She did not see the new notification waiting beneath the war report. It was an invitation to follow a page about the future of Lioran schools, suggested by a service she had used for years and generally trusted.

She had never heard of the page.

She did not open it that morning.

Outside, somebody was returning an empty dish to the wrong house. Somebody else was watering the flowers of a neighbor who had gone away for the weekend. Ivo finished his cereal fortress and knocked it down with a spoon.

Six folding tables slept in six garages.

The country still existed.

# Chapter Five — The Shape of a Missing Person

*Merrow and Veyr. The first winter.*

Sela Orin noticed the empty spaces before she noticed the arguments.

At the library where she worked, the Thursday reading group had once been a reliable collection of people who disagreed about every book and brought cake anyway. Now two members came only if a third stayed away. A retired teacher stopped volunteering after a dispute over a history exhibition. The mother who organized winter clothing for newly arrived families resigned because anonymous strangers had published her address, and the people who normally defended her were arguing about whether she had brought it on herself.

At the library desk the losses arrived disguised as administrative tasks.

**Cancel room reservation.**

**Remove my name from mailing list.**

**Do not contact me again.**

Sela had kept attendance records for eleven years. Looking through them in December, she realized that most departures followed no official closure, no reduction of opening hours, no change in library policy. People were simply withdrawing from places where they might encounter someone who thought differently.

She showed the figures to Lev one evening.

Her wife had just returned from the clinic, where a patient had refused treatment from a doctor because of something the doctor had said at a public meeting.

“Maybe everyone is exhausted,” Lev suggested.

“They were exhausted last year too.”

“Maybe there are more places to spend time online.”

“Then why does every argument end with someone leaving?”

Lev took the notebook and looked at the rows of names. She was a doctor, and the records stirred an instinct she trusted: when a pattern is unusual, check the records before deciding why.

“Ask the other libraries,” she said. “Find out whether it's just us.”

Sela wrote to colleagues in five towns. Three replied quickly with similar stories. One said the local community garden had split into rival groups that now refused to share the greenhouse. The fifth asked Sela not to contact her through work email.

That was what unsettled her most.

Not the argument. The caution.

A week later, she met Eva Maren, a regional journalist, in a café near the station. Eva was twenty-nine, perpetually short of sleep, and a believer in the idea that every assertion should survive being written on a piece of paper.

Sela showed her the attendance sheets.

“People stop joining things,” Eva said. “That happens.”

“Not like this. And not everywhere at once.”

“Do you have proof it's everywhere?”

“Not yet.”

“Then that's what we need.”

Eva had been investigating a collection of newly influential media organizations whose funding was difficult to trace. She had also found local political figures gaining prominence far beyond what their earlier audiences would have predicted. None of it proved foreign direction. People were perfectly capable of manufacturing a national crisis for money or attention without help from across a border.

But there were anomalies.

The same exaggerated claims appeared in unrelated communities. Small organizations suddenly had unusually sophisticated publicity campaigns. A company with no discernible history was paying for political advertisements. The source of the money disappeared behind several layers of ownership.

Eva laid a folder on the table.

“I don't have a story yet,” she said. “I have a lot of questions.”

“Do you think it's Veyr?”

Eva shook her head.

“I think you don't get to name your suspect before you've established a crime.”

Sela smiled, relieved by the rebuke.

For a moment it felt like an ordinary day in an ordinary republic: two people with different knowledge trying to establish what was true.

That afternoon, Sela walked home past the Arden house. Tomas stood on a ladder taking down a strand of dead Christmas lights. He had always called Dain for electrical jobs, although most of them were simple enough for him to do himself.

“Where's your assistant?” Sela asked.

“Busy.”

She knew he wasn't. She said nothing.

At number fourteen, Mara's car was parked crookedly on the drive. Through the window Sela saw Mara at the kitchen table, face blue with light, a hand pressed to her forehead.

Sela considered knocking. They had once visited each other without invitations. Now every conversation felt as though it might contain a hidden tripwire. She kept walking.

In Veyr, Niko Rahl had reached a similar conclusion from a very different collection of numbers.

The first reports had measured attitudes: what people believed, how they voted, how intensely they expressed their opinions. Then the department had begun measuring participation. Attendance, membership, voluntary cooperation, the quiet infrastructure through which a country solves problems without waiting for its government.

The results frightened him.

He presented them to Irena late on a Friday.

“Some of the ideological separation is temporary,” he said. “People change their minds. But the civic withdrawal may persist even when opinions change.”

“Explain.”

“If someone leaves a group because they hate another member, the group loses the ability to solve a problem. Even if they later discover the argument was foolish, the meeting might never resume.”

Irena took the papers.

“And does that help our objective?”

“Is that what we are calling it?”

She gave him a long look.

Niko stared at the sheet. In the margin he had written: *Absence is not neutral.*

“People can still disagree and live together,” he said.

“Yes,” Irena replied.

“Then this is something else.”

“I know.”

It was the first time she had said it without wrapping the answer in professional language.

That evening she left work after dark. The northern capital was preparing for the Victory Observance, another public ceremony commemorating a military advance nobody could find on an ordinary map. Banners hung between government buildings. On a large screen, the Leader promised that Veyr would never yield to its enemies.

Irena stopped beneath the screen because a girl was crying nearby.

The child's mother had bought her a pastry, which had fallen onto the wet pavement. The child was perhaps five and looked at the ruined food with the complete despair only small children are allowed to show.

A stranger picked up the paper bag and offered the mother an unopened sweet from his own shopping. The child accepted it reluctantly. A moment later she smiled.

Irena watched the exchange, then boarded the tram.

At home there was a message from Director Kest.

**The Leader has reviewed the winter assessment. Excellent progress. The next phase is authorized.**

She did not answer.

Back in Merrow, the first snow began to fall across Rowan Way. In earlier years, the neighbors had borrowed snow shovels, cleared the pavements together and taken turns making hot drinks. That winter the sidewalks were cleared at different hours. People glanced from their windows before stepping outside.

It was nothing a statistician would have called a national emergency.

Sela, standing at her front door, looked down the quiet street and tried to remember when being a neighbor had become something people did in private.

Inside the Arden house, Ivo found a photograph from the summer meal. He carried it to the kitchen, where Mara was working on her phone and Tomas was sorting unpaid bills.

“Can we do this again?” he asked.

“Do what?”

He held up the photograph.

Mara looked at the faces around the table. There was Dain, laughing with Tomas. Mrs. Kade, who had since gone into care. Lev with her little chair. Sela leaning over the bread. And Mara herself, caught in the impossible act of being happy without needing to prove that she was right.

“Of course,” she said.

“When?”

“Next summer.”

Ivo nodded. He was old enough to hear an answer and young enough to believe it.

Mara returned to her phone.

Tomas returned to the bills.

Between them, the photograph lay face-up on the table.

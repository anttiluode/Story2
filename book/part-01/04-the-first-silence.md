# Chapter Four — The First Silence

*Merrow. November.*

Dain and Tomas had a ritual for bad weather.

When the wind rose above the rooflines and the railway wires began to sing, Dain would send his brother a photograph of whatever he was repairing, usually taken from an angle that made the job look more dangerous than it was. Tomas would send back a picture of a cup of coffee.

**Expertise looks different in our professions**, he would write.

They had been doing it for fifteen years.

In November the wind tore branches from the trees along the river and knocked out power across the western district. Dain spent sixteen hours working at a substation in freezing rain. He came home hungry, with blood dried across a knuckle, to find that a video of the blackout had circulated online beneath a title suggesting that the outages were deliberate.

Someone accused the utility crews of refusing to repair neighborhoods whose residents supported the wrong political faction.

Dain sat in his kitchen staring at the claim.

He could have ignored it. He could have posted the maintenance records he was legally allowed to share, or asked a reporter to explain the fault. Instead he wrote a furious paragraph about people who knew nothing of work and had turned inconvenience into a national disease.

By morning, strangers were praising his courage.

Others called him a liar.

He stayed home from work because his wrist had swollen. For the first time in his adult life, he spent an entire day responding to people he had never met.

When Tomas called that evening, Dain did not answer. It wasn't an important refusal. He was tired, and he wanted to finish one more message. Tomorrow, he thought. The brothers could speak tomorrow.

A week later, the government announced an emergency budget. The railway was to lose several maintenance contracts. Dain said private firms could do some of the work more efficiently. Tomas said cutting preventive maintenance would cost more later, and perhaps cost lives.

They had held versions of this argument for years.

But the words arrived differently now. Tomas had been reading stories about the collapse of public services. Dain had spent days being congratulated by people who regarded public-sector unions as parasites. Each brother came into the conversation accompanied by an invisible crowd.

“You always think you're the only one who works,” Tomas said.

“I was out there repairing the lines while you were talking about them.”

“I repair signals. You know that.”

“I know you get paid to stand behind a union whenever somebody asks you to improve something.”

Tomas looked up from his plate.

There was a time when the insult would have ended with laughter. Dain would have made a face, Tomas would have thrown a roll, and their mother—if she had been alive—would have told them both to stop being idiots.

Their mother was dead. The room was quiet. And Dain had spent so long defending an argument in public that retreating from it in private felt like humiliation.

“Get out,” Tomas said.

Mara, who had been washing a dish, turned around.

Neither brother moved.

“I mean it.”

Dain stood. The chair scraped the floor. He looked at Mara as if asking her to explain what had happened, but she was angry at him too, for reasons that had begun with the sports hall and accumulated through a hundred smaller disagreements.

“Maybe you should go,” she said.

Dain nodded. He took his coat from the hook where it had hung every Sunday for years.

At the front door, Ivo appeared from the hallway.

“Are we going fishing next weekend?”

Dain looked at his nephew.

“Not this weekend, little man.”

“Next weekend?”

“We'll see.”

The phrase hung between them. Ivo understood it perfectly. It was what adults said when they didn't want to tell you no.

Dain left.

For a while, nothing happened.

Tomas rinsed his plate. Mara turned off the kitchen lights. Ivo sat on the stairs and waited for the sound of his uncle's car to return, because there had obviously been a misunderstanding and someone would come back to correct it.

Nobody did.

At eleven, Tomas opened his phone and found the old message thread with his brother. Photographs of wind-damaged roofs, coffee cups, tools borrowed and returned, bad jokes, a picture of their mother on her last birthday. The recent messages were fewer. Most concerned politics. Almost all had ended without a reply.

He typed: **I shouldn't have said that.**

He watched the words on the screen.

Then he erased them. Dain ought to apologize first.

Across town, Dain opened the same conversation and typed: **We got carried away.**

He erased that too. Tomas had thrown him out of the house. An apology without an acknowledgment would make him look weak.

They both slept badly.

The next morning Ivo asked Mara whether Dain was still his uncle.

“Of course he is.”

“Even if Daddy doesn't like him?”

“Daddy likes him.”

“Then why did he tell him to go away?”

Mara opened her mouth and discovered that she had no answer a child would believe.

In the northern capital, three floors below Irena Solvik's office, a presentation displayed a graph of declining cross-group contact in selected southern online communities. Some lines were drawn from reliable data. Others were projections, their uncertainty hidden beneath clean colors.

Niko Rahl watched his supervisor circle a small downward movement.

“This is interesting,” the supervisor said.

Niko remembered the photograph of the neighborhood supper. He imagined the two men carrying the table.

He did not know they had stopped speaking. The department could not see inside the Arden kitchen. The graph was not proof of the cause of any single quarrel. But its abstractions were beginning to resemble something he feared.

“You have to remember,” the supervisor said, misreading his expression, “these figures represent millions of people.”

Niko wanted to ask whether that made each person matter more or less.

Instead he made a note.

On Rowan Way, the year continued. Cars were repaired, meals were cooked, children went to school. The family catastrophe did not announce itself as a catastrophe. It looked like one missed Sunday, then another.

By December, Tomas and Dain had not spoken for thirty-seven days.

Nobody had decided that they never would again.

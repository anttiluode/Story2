# Chapter Three — A Very Ordinary Notification

*Merrow. September.*

Mara did not think of herself as political.

This did not mean she lacked opinions. She had strong opinions about school lunches, public transport, the price of football boots, and the particular cruelty of scheduling children's matches at eight in the morning. She had spent six years managing the junior football club, which required a level of diplomacy normally associated with international treaties.

She knew which parents couldn't afford the annual fee and which ones pretended they could. She knew who was recently divorced, who needed a lift, which child was frightened to play in front of his father, and why the grandmother of the smallest goalkeeper always sat behind the far goal instead of with the other spectators.

Politics was something she dealt with when it touched those facts.

The municipality planned to close one of Merrow's two indoor sports halls for repairs. Nobody disputed the roof was dangerous. The problem was that the hall served the poorer side of town, and the proposed temporary replacement required two buses to reach. The town offered reimbursement to low-income families, but the application process was complicated and the reimbursement came afterward.

“Afterward,” Mara said at a parents' meeting, “isn't much use when you don't have the money beforehand.”

Everyone agreed. Even the mayor agreed, which made Mara suspicious.

She wrote a short note about the problem on Lantern. She expected perhaps twenty replies, a council member's promise to investigate, and a dozen photographs of leaking ceilings.

Instead, her phone stayed awake through dinner.

There were hundreds of replies. People from towns she had never visited shared their own stories. Some were useful. A woman explained how another municipality had arranged transport. A retired builder offered to inspect the roof report. Others accused the council of hating children, wasting money, favoring wealthy parents, or deliberately destroying community sport. Their confidence seemed inversely related to their knowledge of Merrow.

A page called **Fair Liora** reposted Mara's note.

Another page, **The People's Ledger**, presented the hall closure as evidence of everything wrong with public spending.

Mara stared at the number beneath her post. Twelve thousand people had seen it.

At breakfast Tomas asked her whether she had finished the club accounts.

“Not yet.”

“You promised.”

“I've been trying to get someone to listen.”

“Someone listened. That's good. Can you put the receipts in the folder?”

“Could you be happy about something I did for once?”

Tomas stopped buttering the bread.

He was happy. He was also worried about the receipts because the club's treasurer was ill and there was a deadline. He had no idea that Mara had spent three hours the previous night reading strangers congratulating her on being brave enough to speak.

“Of course I'm happy,” he said.

But it sounded like surrender instead of admiration, which was not what she had wanted.

Later, after she dropped Ivo at school, Mara returned to Lantern. In a few weeks her daily feed had changed from photographs of the football team and the bakery's lunch menu to an endless procession of arguments about the kind of country Liora was becoming.

Some of the arguments concerned genuine injustice. She learned things she had not known. She also encountered stories she could not verify, presented by people who seemed to have no doubts at all. The platform, which had always rewarded material that kept users looking, gave her more of the subjects to which she returned. No northern officer needed to choose each post. The machine was already an attentive servant of her attention.

Mara noticed that, for the first time, the people on her screen seemed to know what she was angry about before she did.

That feeling was intoxicating.

She began to suspect that people who didn't share her anger had simply never looked closely enough.

On a Friday evening, Dain came to help Tomas replace a cracked pipe under the sink.

“The entire football thing is being made into a campaign,” Dain said, crouching on the kitchen floor. “Some of the numbers going around aren't even right.”

Mara folded her arms.

“Which numbers?”

“The budget figures. Someone's comparing the repairs to the whole education budget. Different years, different categories. It's nonsense.”

“Is the roof safe?”

“No.”

“Can children get there?”

“Not easily.”

“Then what exactly are you defending?”

“I'm not defending the closure. I'm telling you that people are using it.”

“People like me?”

Dain looked up. He had the wrench in his hand and a pale line across his forehead from where the cupboard frame had pressed against it.

“Come on, Mara. That's not what I said.”

She knew it wasn't. Later she would remember this moment and be unable to explain why she had answered as though he had accused her of being stupid. At the time it seemed important not to let him get away with the implication.

Tomas moved between them with a towel.

“Can we at least finish flooding the kitchen before starting a civil war?”

Dain laughed.

Mara did not.

That night she lay in bed reading until Tomas turned over and asked whether she could lower the brightness.

“Sorry,” she whispered.

After a while, she held the phone against her chest and looked at the ceiling. Tomas slept beside her. They had been together seventeen years. He had cared for her when she had pneumonia. He knew she hated coriander. She knew the smell of the oil on his railway jacket better than she knew the smell of her own perfume.

Yet, somehow, the people on Lantern had begun to feel like the people who understood her best.

She turned the screen back on.

In Veyr, a routine report recorded increased engagement with contentious political material in several southern districts. The report did not mention Mara by name. It could not know what she had said to Dain, or why she had stayed awake.

Analysts would debate how much of the shift belonged to deliberate interference and how much to the platform's ordinary appetite for attention. Their superiors cared less about the distinction than the direction.

At home, Tomas reached across the bed in his sleep and put a hand on Mara's shoulder.

She lifted it gently and kept reading.

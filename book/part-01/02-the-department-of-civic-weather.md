# Chapter Two — The Department of Civic Weather

*Veyr, the Northern Directorate. The following Monday.*

The windows in the Ministry of Continuity did not open.

They were enormous, floor-to-ceiling panes looking over a capital of wide stone avenues and dark, orderly traffic. In winter, ice gathered on their outer surfaces. In summer, the glass became warm to the touch, although the building's air-conditioning held every room at nineteen degrees.

Dr. Irena Solvik preferred the old rooms before the renovation. They had been unpleasant, but at least a person could tell whether the sun was shining.

She was fifty-two, a clinical psychologist by training, a civil servant by temperament, and a woman whose work had been praised three times by the Directorate without ever being described publicly. Her mother had taught school. Her father had served in the army. Irena had spent the first part of her career treating soldiers who came home from the northern border unable to sleep.

Now she led a department whose name did not appear on the building directory.

Her office was on the seventh floor, between Strategic Demography and Public Resilience. On the frosted glass of her door, someone had printed:

**CIVIC WEATHER — RESEARCH DIRECTORATE**

It was an absurd name. She had disliked it at first. Over the years she had come to understand its usefulness. Weather happened to people. It was a condition to be measured, not a crime to be confessed.

At nine, her staff assembled around a table polished enough to reflect the ceiling lights.

Niko Rahl was the youngest in the room. He wore the same gray jacket every day and carried his notes in a school exercise book because the secure tablets made him nervous. On one page he had written a sentence from an old psychology textbook: *A person does not experience society. A person experiences other people.*

He had underlined it twice.

At the head of the table sat Director Kest, an administrator from the Office of State Stability. Nobody knew what he had studied, only that the Directorate trusted him and that he remembered every appointment he had ever granted.

“Tarven is taking longer than planned,” Kest said.

Nobody asked which part of the war he meant. The government had declared victory nine times in eighteen months. Military funerals continued. The newspapers had learned to print the dates of victories without printing dates of the dead.

“Our southern problem becomes more urgent,” he continued.

A large map lit the wall.

To the north, Veyr had swallowed a strip of disputed territory and was fighting over what remained of Tarven. To the south, separated from Veyr by a mountain corridor and a gulf, lay Liora: small, wealthy enough to be inconvenient, democratic enough to speak plainly about Veyr's war. It exported medicines, hosted regional negotiations, and gave sanctuary to people who crossed the border.

Liora was not a military threat. That was precisely why the Directorate disliked it.

“Can the southern state be neutralized?” Kest asked.

The question was not directed to the room. It was directed to Irena.

She had been trained to dislike vague nouns. Neutralized could mean so many things. A child sedated before surgery was neutralized. A factory whose generator had failed was neutralized. A country rendered incapable of acting was neutralized. Her department existed to make the third meaning possible without appearing in the first two.

“We can measure pressure,” she said. “We cannot promise an outcome.”

Kest looked disappointed. Senior men generally were when science refused to speak in the future perfect.

Irena brought up a chart: trust in neighbors; participation in local associations; belief that an election loss could be accepted; willingness to cooperate with a stranger. The numbers had been assembled from public research, private data purchases and estimates. Some were reliable. Some were not. None could tell the room what a particular family would do next Tuesday.

“Liora's institutions are strong,” she told them. “But strength is not uniform. There are economic frustrations. Regional resentments. Political organizations that would welcome foreign approval as long as they could pretend it wasn't foreign.”

“And the citizens?” Kest asked.

“They're citizens. They aren't laboratory animals.”

“Are you refusing the assignment?”

“No. I'm telling you the difference between a model and a person.”

Kest folded his hands.

“Doctor, the northern soldiers do not have the luxury of that distinction.”

The room remained still. Somewhere beneath them, a printer was producing a report no one would be allowed to take home.

The project had no dramatic name. There would be no declaration of war against Liora, no anthem, no troops at the border. It would be administered through budgets, subcontractors, research summaries, sympathetic intermediaries, and a continual argument over whether anything was working. The public platforms used by Liorans were not controlled by Veyr. They had their own commercial motives, their own defects and owners. Veyr's officers considered those ordinary incentives a favorable climate.

Niko was asked to present his preliminary study.

He stood at the wall screen and showed the room a series of photographs drawn from Lioran public pages: a village festival, a nurses' fundraiser, a railway workers' gathering, a school football team, a neighborhood supper.

There was a photograph from Rowan Way.

Mara was leaning toward Sela, laughing. Behind them, Tomas and Dain carried a folding table across the road. Ivo had raised his hands as though surrendering to the camera.

“They have high local participation,” Niko said. “Very low expressed hostility. Their disagreements seem to be managed socially rather than politically.”

“You make them sound sick,” said a senior analyst.

“I didn't mean—”

“What are they disagreeing about?” Kest asked.

“Rail funding. A school levy. The town budget. Quite ordinary things.”

“And can those disagreements change?”

“All disagreements can change.”

Kest nodded, satisfied. Niko sat down, dissatisfied with himself.

The meeting lasted another fifty minutes. Its language remained bloodless. Nothing in the minutes could be read aloud at a tribunal and sound like an instruction to harm a marriage. The officials discussed legitimacy, confidence, institutional resilience, information environments. When the presentation ended, Kest authorized the first expansion of the department's budget.

No one applauded. They had work to do.

Afterward, Niko stayed behind to collect his notes. Irena was looking at the photograph of Rowan Way, still projected on the wall.

“Why that one?” she asked.

“It was in the sample.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

Niko hesitated.

“My family used to have a table like that,” he said. “Before my father was transferred.”

Irena turned off the screen.

“You've been here three weeks. It's natural to confuse curiosity with conscience. Try not to let either one interfere with your measurements.”

“Do you believe this will work?”

“Which part?”

“Making people stop trusting each other.”

Irena considered him with more care than she had shown Kest.

“I believe human beings are capable of many things when they are afraid,” she said. “I also believe officials are capable of claiming responsibility for things they didn't cause.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“No,” she said. “It isn't.”

At six-thirty, she collected her coat and walked through the security doors. On the tram she watched schoolchildren fight over a seat, laugh, reconcile, and share a packet of sweets before the next stop.

Her daughter sent a photograph of a cake she had baked for the grandchild's birthday.

**Will you be here on Sunday?**

Irena typed **Of course**.

Then she put the phone away and thought about the neighbors who appeared in Niko's photograph. She remembered the woman leaning across the table, the boy with his arms raised, the two men carrying its wooden surface between them.

By the end of the year, she would know their names.

She did not yet know that one of them would die.

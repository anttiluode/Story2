# Chapter Nine — The Ledger

*Merrow and the Northern Directorate. November.*

Eva Maren's editor had three rules for stories involving money.

The first was that everybody had a name. A company had a legal name. An officer signing a document had a name. A sum of money had an origin and a destination, even when the path between them was difficult to establish.

The second rule was that the missing part of a record was not evidence of whatever a reporter happened to suspect.

The third was never to publish a number you had not checked twice.

Samuel Rees had taught her these rules when she was twenty-four and believed that a sufficiently angry article could make the world behave. He had spent thirty years in local journalism, most of them in a building where the heating never quite worked. His newspaper, the *Merrow Chronicle*, was not particularly influential. The national networks borrowed its stories when they were interesting and forgot to credit it when they were not.

On the morning of the eleventh of November he called Eva into his office.

There was a letter on his desk.

The Public Voice Foundation's lawyers accused the newspaper of conducting a campaign of harassment against an independent civic organization. They demanded that Eva cease approaching the foundation's contractors and implied that the paper's questions were motivated by political prejudice.

“Did you harass anyone?” Samuel asked.

“No.”

“Did you threaten to publish their addresses?”

“No.”

“Did you ask the caterer who paid for an event?”

“Yes.”

“Did you ask more than once?”

“Twice. The first time he thought I wanted to book a wedding.”

Samuel almost smiled.

He had a photograph of his late wife on the desk. Eva had known him only as a widower. He was one of those men whose grief had settled into the furniture: not loud, not hidden, simply always there.

“Do we have anything?” he asked.

“We have invoices, contracts, public filings, and an explanation from a financial expert who says the filings don't match the foundation's public claims.”

“Do we have a crime?”

“Not yet.”

“Do we have the north?”

“No.”

“Then we have a story about a foundation that won't tell the public where its money comes from.”

“Two foundations.”

Samuel looked at her.

“Explain.”

Eva opened her folder.

***

She had spent ten weeks doing the least romantic work in journalism.

She requested records, checked dates, called accountants, waited in municipal offices, and learned far more than she wanted about the reporting rules for charities. She ate sandwiches beside photocopiers. She discovered that organizations could be careless, opaque, or dishonest without being agents of a foreign power. She had to follow the evidence even when it led to something disappointingly ordinary.

The Public Voice Foundation had paid for Joren Fenn's programme and public events. That much was established by contracts and the foundation's own acknowledgement.

A second nonprofit, **Tomorrow's Neighbors**, supported civic education events and public discussions. One of its grants had gone to a Common Future-affiliated local group. That was also lawful in principle, and publicly disclosed.

What troubled Eva was a shared outside contractor named **Harborbridge Civic Services**. Its name appeared in invoices from both organizations. The descriptions differed. For Public Voice, the invoices concerned media production and outreach. For Tomorrow's Neighbors, they referred to community research and event administration.

A firm was entitled to serve clients on both sides of politics. Lawyers did it. Printers did it. Venues did it. Even newspapers sold advertising to parties that disagreed with their editorials.

So Eva did not write **foreign conspiracy** at the top of her notes.

She wrote **why this firm?**

Its headquarters was a quiet office above a shop selling hearing aids. Nobody answered when she called. A recorded voice directed her to an address for written inquiries, and the address returned a polite note saying Harborbridge had no comment on client relationships.

A retired accountant helped Eva examine several receipts. The accountant had no information about Veyr, and did not pretend otherwise.

“There's nothing inherently criminal about shared suppliers,” she said. “What interests me is that one of these expense descriptions appears inconsistent with the way the payment was reported publicly.”

“Could it be an error?”

“Of course.”

“Could it be deliberate?”

“Of course.”

Eva wrote both possibilities in her notebook.

That evening she put them side by side and circled neither.

***

Sela Orin arrived at the newspaper carrying a cardboard box.

Eva opened the door to find her standing in the rain with water dripping from her hair onto her collar.

“This isn't a bomb,” Sela said.

“I wasn't worried until you mentioned it.”

“It's attendance records.”

Eva let her in.

Sela spread the notebooks across the long table in the paper's conference room. They were not glamorous documents. The covers were bent; some had coffee stains; one contained a recipe for pear cake. The handwriting changed over the years as volunteers took turns keeping the records.

“What am I looking at?” Eva asked.

“The Thursday reading group. Volunteer deliveries. The community repair club. The winter clothing committee. Eleven years where I could find them.”

“And?”

“An ordinary decline during the pandemic, then a recovery. Then something else. People who'd been attending for years stopped coming. Groups didn't just lose members; they stopped working together. In two cases they divided into rival committees and kept the same name.”

Eva studied the dates.

“How do you know they stopped because of politics?”

“I don't. Some moved. Some died. Some got old or ill. I called people who left, where it was appropriate to call. I asked what changed.”

“And they told you?”

“Some did. Some wouldn't talk.”

Sela opened the last notebook. Under each date she had written a short explanation when a volunteer had given one: *Uncomfortable with comments at meeting. Not willing to work alongside X. Feels unsafe after argument. Doesn't want trouble with family.*

“This isn't a national sample,” she said before Eva could object. “It's our town. It's not proof of a coordinated attack. But something has changed here, and people keep pretending they're imagining it.”

Eva ran a finger down the pages.

“Do you have permission to use the names?”

“Not all of them. I thought you could speak to people yourself.”

“Good.”

Sela looked relieved.

They worked for two hours, one examining vanished participation, the other following money that might or might not be connected to the disappearance.

The coincidence seemed almost too neat for a novel. In life it felt like two incompatible ways of saying the same thing.

At three, Eva ordered coffee. At four, Sela went home to cook supper for Lev.

Before leaving, she asked Eva what the paper would publish.

“I don't know.”

“What do you believe?”

“That people are being encouraged to distrust one another. That some organizations are getting money without explaining enough about it. That your town is losing something.”

“And the foreign operation?”

Eva looked down at the receipts.

“Possibly. But I won't put Veyr's name in an article just because it would make the pieces fit.”

“Even if that's the truth?”

“Especially then.”

Sela nodded slowly. She did not like the answer. She trusted it.

***

At the Arden house, Ivo had become skilled at listening for weather that did not exist.

A drawer closing too hard meant his mother had read something upsetting. A long silence before supper meant his father had disagreed with her but did not want to start a fight. If they spoke about the railway, Ivo could safely ask whether the trains were late. If they spoke about the government, he went upstairs.

That evening Mara was planning a winter fundraiser for the football club.

“Joren says he'll do a benefit episode,” she said.

Tomas looked up from his tea.

“Joren Fenn?”

“Yes.”

“That's not a good idea.”

“He can raise money.”

“He can make a fight out of a lost dog.”

“Could you stop dismissing people just because you don't like their politics?”

“I don't mind his politics. I mind him lying about the railway.”

“He didn't lie. He quoted a report.”

“Wrong year. Wrong line. I've shown you.”

Mara sighed. “Why does every conversation turn into this?”

Tomas wanted to say that she was the one who had mentioned Joren. Instead he asked whether the fundraiser could be run through the club without a broadcaster.

“Alia says Common Future can help with the hall,” Mara said.

Tomas set down his cup.

“You're having Common Future run the fundraiser?”

“No. I said they can help.”

“Then why involve Joren?”

“Because the point is to get help from everyone.”

It was the right answer. Mara believed it. She had tried to hold a door open between two groups and found them both suspicious of the doorway.

“Fine,” Tomas said.

“I don't want another 'fine.' I want you to understand.”

“I'm trying.”

“You say that as if I'm the problem.”

He looked at her. The exhaustion in his face made him seem suddenly older than he was.

“I didn't say you were.”

Mara opened her mouth, then shut it.

In the hall Ivo waited with his school bag. He had a form requiring both parents' signatures for a trip to the capital. He stood listening until the conversation seemed finished.

Then he walked into the kitchen.

“Can I go to the museum?” he asked.

Both parents looked at him, grateful for a question that had an answer.

“Yes,” they said together.

He smiled. They smiled. For a moment they were a family solving a small problem.

Mara signed the form. Tomas signed beneath her.

Ivo took it upstairs before they could disagree about the train fare.

***

The winter fundraiser was canceled.

It never reached the stage of an argument about catering, or a hall booking, or the price of tickets. One sponsor threatened to withdraw if Joren appeared. Another said its contribution depended on Common Future having no involvement. Mara tried to negotiate. People began copying her private messages into public arguments. The football club committee voted to postpone the event.

Postponed, in this case, meant dead.

Mara sat in the clubhouse after the meeting, staring at the sum they had hoped to raise. It had been enough to pay for transport for the whole winter.

The treasurer, an elderly man named Cor, began stacking plastic cups.

“People want us to choose,” Mara said.

“Choose what?”

“Who we're allowed to take help from.”

Cor stopped stacking.

“In my day the worst thing a sponsor could do was make us put its name on the shirt.”

“You had your day, then.”

He did not answer. Mara regretted the words immediately.

“Sorry.”

Cor shrugged, but the old man looked tired.

After he left, she found herself alone in the clubhouse with the children's photographs on the wall. There was Tessa Fenn in a goalkeeper's shirt. Ivo grinning with a broken tooth. A girl whose parents had moved away after her father lost his job. Two brothers who were still on the team but no longer arrived in the same car because their parents would not speak to one another.

Mara took down the folder of unpaid transport claims and began putting them in date order.

This was how the work had started. Not with a movement or a stage, but with children who needed to get somewhere and adults who had promised to help.

Her phone lit up with a message from Alia.

**You're doing important work. Don't let the enemies of public services bully you.**

A second message arrived from Joren.

**I said I'd help. I'm still ready. Don't let their lot shut you down.**

Both were trying to comfort her.

Both had included an enemy.

Mara turned the phone face down and kept sorting.

***

The missing entry appeared on a Thursday.

Eva had gone back through an annual disclosure statement from Tomorrow's Neighbors because a date troubled her. She compared it with a payment confirmation supplied by a person who had worked briefly for the organization's bookkeeper. The source feared retaliation, and Eva agreed to protect their identity while independently verifying what she could.

The confirmation documented a payment that had been omitted from the public account of a particular event.

It was not a spectacular sum. It would barely pay for one of the *Chronicle*'s printing runs. What mattered was where it had come from.

The payor was Harborbridge Civic Services.

The recipient was a local organization associated with Common Future.

Eva returned to the Honest Hour papers. A second confirmation, from an independent supplier, showed Harborbridge arranging payment for one of Joren's events as well.

She laid the documents beside each other.

It was not conclusive proof of a foreign intelligence service. Nor was it proof that the two political organizations had cooperated with each other, or even known that a supplier served both.

It was proof that public descriptions of at least one payment were incomplete, and that a supposedly neutral service company had financed political activity on opposing sides of a civic divide.

She called Samuel.

“Come downstairs.”

“I'm in the middle of something.”

“Please.”

He arrived carrying a half-eaten sandwich.

Eva showed him the documents and explained how each had been verified. She showed him what remained uncertain. Samuel read the dates, then read them again. He asked for the original confirmations and the source protection notes.

“This is better,” he said.

“It is.”

“Do you know who owns Harborbridge?”

“Not completely. There's a registered local director and an overseas investor named in older filings. The investor's identity is not established in the records I can access.”

“Where overseas?”

“North of the gulf. That doesn't mean Veyr.”

“I didn't say it did.”

They sat without speaking.

Samuel's telephone rang. He let it ring out.

“Do we print?” Eva asked.

“Maybe. We need legal to look at it.”

“We can say what these papers establish.”

“We can. And then two political movements will accuse us of serving the other one.”

“They can't both be right.”

“They don't have to be right to hurt us.”

Eva looked at the photograph of his wife.

“Since when has that stopped you?”

Samuel folded his sandwich wrapper and placed it in the bin.

“Since advertising revenue dropped forty percent and we couldn't afford to replace the heating system.”

The answer was not noble. It was also not a lie.

Eva knew the newspaper employed fourteen people. A legal fight would consume money that paid their wages. The *Chronicle* could win a case and still cease to exist.

Samuel agreed to have the documents reviewed.

“Until then,” he said, “you don't publish any of it.”

Eva wanted to argue. She remembered his three rules and said nothing.

***

In the Northern Directorate, Dr. Irena Solvik was preparing a report for the Office of State Stability.

She had been told that the Leader would receive a private briefing on Liora within the month. Kest wanted a presentation with fewer uncertainties and a clearer account of progress. There were twenty pages of statistics, interviews, and assessments; the final version would contain six.

Niko Rahl entered her office carrying a sheet marked with red corrections.

“Harborbridge is in the file,” he said.

Irena looked up.

“Which file?”

“The southern press monitoring.”

“Why are you reading that?”

“Because the research assessment says the program's public intermediaries remain secure.”

He placed the sheet on her desk.

A small local newspaper in Merrow had requested comment from several organizations associated with public communications spending. The department's liaison office had received word of the inquiries. The report did not specify exactly what the journalist had found or whether she understood the broader program.

“It's a newspaper inquiry,” Irena said.

“It's an inquiry into financial relationships.”

“Those happen.”

“The report says none are active.”

“Then the report should be corrected.”

Niko waited.

Irena saw the longer question in his face.

“Have you done your work?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Then send the correction through the usual channels.”

He did not move.

“Doctor, at what point does a study become an operation?”

She put down her pen.

He had asked it plainly, the way young people sometimes did before learning that plain questions could end careers.

“I don't think you are confused about that,” she said.

“I think we've stopped using the correct names for what we're doing.”

Irena looked past him to the glass. Beyond the seventh-floor windows, traffic moved in neat lines through the northern capital.

“Names aren't the problem,” she replied.

“Then what is?”

“Consequences.”

“Whose?”

She did not answer.

Niko took the paper and left.

For the first time she considered dismissing him. He was an excellent analyst, but excellence was becoming inconvenient. She told herself he needed maturity, that no government department could be run by people who treated every policy choice as a moral emergency.

Then she thought of the girl on the tram who had lost her pastry. A small stranger had offered a sweet, and the problem had been solved before anyone had time to calculate whether it served the interests of the state.

She opened Kest's report.

On the page describing southern political fragmentation, an indicator of declining trust appeared beside a green arrow.

She left it there.

***

Eva worked late.

The legal review would not be complete until the following week. Samuel had gone home. The newsroom smelled of cold coffee and old carpet. Rain pushed against the windows.

She opened the evidence again and created a careful summary.

**Established:** two receipts showing Harborbridge's role in payments connected to opposing political organizations; inaccurate or incomplete public accounting for at least one payment; a refusal by the shared contractor to discuss its client relationships.

**Not established:** who ultimately directed Harborbridge; whether recipients knew the complete source of the money; whether any northern intelligence agency was involved; what effect, if any, those payments had on public attitudes.

She printed the page.

Then she printed Sela's attendance records, with private names removed.

They looked like two kinds of accounting. One recorded the money entering public life. The other recorded people leaving it.

Eva could not prove they belonged to the same story. She could no longer comfortably believe they did not.

At half past eleven she received an email from an unknown address.

There was no attachment, no threat, no promise of a revelation. Just a sentence:

**You are asking the right question about the wrong level of the building.**

She reread it.

The message might have come from a prankster, an employee with knowledge, or someone trying to lure her away from evidence she could verify. She did not reply. She printed the message and noted the time. It was a lead, nothing more.

At midnight she turned off the office lights and locked the door.

Across the street a café worker was stacking chairs. A bus went past with three passengers. Above the town, the first snow clouds of winter moved slowly across the sky.

Eva walked to the station with the documents in her bag.

Behind her, in a street she could not see, Sela Orin was taking another name off the reading group's list. At the Arden house, Ivo was asleep while his parents lay awake on opposite sides of the same bed. At Joren Fenn's garage, a producer had left an urgent message promising an extraordinary audience for the next episode.

In the northern capital, Irena Solvik would receive the revised report in the morning.

On its final page, beneath a table of figures, someone had written a sentence that sounded like a scientific conclusion:

**The system now produces its own separation pressures.**

No one had written down who would be responsible for stopping them.

Eva reached the station just as the last train arrived. Its doors opened. She stepped inside.

For one brief moment, before the train moved, she saw her reflection in the dark window, superimposed upon the empty platform.

A woman with a folder.

A country with a name.

Neither of them yet knew how little time was left.

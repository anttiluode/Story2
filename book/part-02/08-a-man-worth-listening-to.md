# Chapter Eight — A Man Worth Listening To

*Merrow. September.*

Joren Fenn had never wanted to be famous.

He had wanted a larger workshop, an apprentice, and a roof that did not leak onto the diagnostic equipment when it rained. He had wanted his daughter Tessa to have a new pair of football boots before she outgrew the old ones, and he had wanted the municipal parking officer to stop issuing tickets outside the garage when customers left their cars for repairs.

He had worked for twenty-one years on engines other men could afford to neglect. He knew the smell of a failed clutch before opening the bonnet. He knew which customers would pay him on Friday, and which ones meant the following Friday, and which ones would never pay him at all. He was the man neighbors called when their cars refused to start on the morning of a funeral.

In his own mind, these were respectable achievements.

They were not achievements anyone outside Merrow cared about.

Then somebody clipped forty seconds from a town meeting in which Joren had told the mayor that an expensive new municipal office was no use to a man whose shop was closing because the road outside it had been shut for eight months. The crowd had cheered. The clip appeared on Lantern with a caption calling Joren *the only honest man in the room*.

By evening it had been seen across the district.

The following day a caller from a small online channel asked whether he wanted to repeat what he had said.

“I haven't got anything new,” Joren replied.

“That's fine,” the caller said. “We want you to say it again.”

So he did.

He spoke from his garage beside a car with its engine exposed, wearing a work shirt with an oil stain on the collar. His voice was rough and he did not sound as though he had rehearsed. He told the truth about the road closure. He told the truth about a procurement contract that had left independent traders waiting for an answer. He called the mayor an upholstered nuisance.

The short interview became another popular clip.

After that came the invitations.

A Renewal council candidate asked him to appear at a meeting. A regional station wanted a comment. A man in a dark coat introduced himself as a producer from an organization called the Public Voice Foundation and said there could be funding for a weekly programme about the concerns of ordinary working people.

Joren did not ask much about the foundation. He was forty-seven, frightened about losing his business, and unaccustomed to people with business cards treating his opinions as something worth buying.

His wife, Mira, asked whether he had signed anything.

“Not yet.”

“Show me before you do.”

“It's a radio programme, not a mortgage.”

“You haven't read the mortgage we *have*.”

He laughed because he thought she was being funny.

She was only half joking.

***

The first episode was recorded on a Wednesday evening in a room above an office that sold insurance policies.

The programme was called **The Honest Hour**. Its logo showed a clenched hand holding a wrench, although Joren had never held a wrench in that particular way and pointed out that it would be useless for repairing anything.

The producer said the logo wasn't meant to be literal.

“Neither is half of politics,” Joren replied.

The producer laughed too loudly.

Joren sat before a microphone while a technician adjusted the level. Through the glass he could see a cheap clock and a coffee cup with somebody else's lipstick on it. It was nothing like the polished studios he had watched on television. That pleased him. The programme felt homemade and independent.

They asked him to discuss the decline of small businesses in Merrow.

He spoke about the workshop, the blocked road, his customers, and the price of parts. There were things he got wrong. He confused two municipal budgets and misunderstood the rules governing a national tax. The producer did not correct him. At the end of the recording Joren said he hoped the council could find a solution.

The finished episode left that part out.

Instead it ended with his furious question about why honest workers were expected to suffer while important people were never inconvenienced.

The producer sent him the link.

“That's not quite what I said,” Joren replied.

“It's exactly what you said,” the producer answered.

Both statements were true, in different ways.

Within hours people were congratulating him for finally refusing to compromise. They said the country needed a man who would speak plainly. Several offered to send money. Someone described him as a future minister.

Joren read every message.

At the next recording, when he considered ending a story with a practical suggestion, he remembered which part of the first episode had received applause.

He did not need to be ordered to become angrier.

***

Mara encountered Joren at a junior football match.

He was standing beside the touchline with Tessa, carrying a paper cup of coffee and a bag of oranges for the team. It was a pleasant autumn morning. Wet grass clung to the children's socks. A goalkeeper was complaining that his gloves smelled like cheese.

For a while Mara forgot the national arguments. She checked the team list, tied a shoelace for a child whose hands were shaking, and reminded a father not to shout instructions over the coach.

Joren came to her when the match ended.

“Heard the sports hall's still shut,” he said.

“It is.”

“That's rotten.”

“Yes.”

“Funny, isn't it? We can spend a fortune on an administration building, but not get kids to a match.”

Mara looked at him.

“That's not the same budget.”

“Don't you start.”

“What?”

“Everybody says it isn't the same budget. It's always a different pot when the pot's empty.”

She had heard the sentence before, in the comments beneath her own posts. She had admired it at first because it sounded like the simple truth of a complicated situation.

But she now knew enough of the accounts to understand why the distinction mattered.

“Joren, if they take the money from the building budget, they still have to cancel a contract and pay penalties. The problem is that the transport scheme was promised without a realistic funding plan.”

“Listen to you,” he said affectionately. “You sound like the mayor.”

The words were meant as a tease.

They hurt more than an accusation would have.

Tessa emerged from the changing rooms holding a football boot by its loose sole.

“Dad, can we get this fixed?”

Joren took it. “Bring it to the workshop. We'll sort it.”

Mara watched him press the sole back into place with a mechanic's practiced hand. He was not a villain standing over a frightened child. He was a father with a failing business trying to keep his daughter in the game she loved.

“You coming next week?” Mara asked Tessa.

“Depends,” the girl said.

“On what?”

Tessa glanced at her father.

“Some of the girls say my dad hates their parents.”

Joren's face tightened.

“Who said that?”

Tessa looked down.

“I don't want to say.”

Mara felt a fierce, protective anger. She wanted to tell the girl that no parent ought to have their child's life spoiled by an argument about politics. She almost did.

Then she remembered that Alia Venn had asked her to share a statement condemning people who appeared on the Honest Hour. She had shared it without thinking much about who else might see it.

“I'll speak to the coach,” Mara said.

Tessa nodded, though it was not clear whether that was what she needed.

When they had gone, Mara opened her phone and searched for the statement. Thousands of people had already reacted to it. Some were arguing, some praising her for taking a stand, some calling Joren a parasite.

She read the words she had shared.

**No platform for those who poison our communities.**

She had liked the sentence when she first saw it. It sounded principled.

Standing beside the empty pitch, she began to wonder who counted as a community once everyone had agreed on the people who could not be part of it.

***

A producer from the Honest Hour came to Joren's workshop on Monday and offered to pay for new equipment.

The arrangement would be described as a sponsorship. Joren would retain the appearance of independence and remain free, he was assured, to speak his mind. The foundation wanted only a regular programme and the chance to help a working man reach an audience.

The money was not extraordinary by the standards of national media. By the standards of Joren's garage it was enough to pay three months of rent and perhaps keep his apprentice.

Mira read the papers at the kitchen table.

“Who's paying?”

“The foundation.”

“Who pays the foundation?”

“I suppose donors.”

“Which donors?”

“Why would it matter if they're not telling me what to say?”

She put down the agreement.

“Because somebody is paying you to keep talking.”

“People pay journalists.”

“They pay journalists to find things out.”

“Is that what they do?”

Mira could not answer without admitting that some journalists were just as uncareful as Joren's new producers.

“I'm asking you to know who you're working for,” she said.

He heard: *I don't believe anyone could value what you have to say.*

It was not what she meant, but it arrived in him as an old grievance. His father had called him a fool for leaving school. His first employer had withheld wages. An official had once smiled politely while refusing to explain why Joren's application had been rejected. He had spent his life fighting to be treated as competent. Now people who had never met him were listening.

“Maybe you just don't like seeing me do well,” he said.

Mira looked at him as if he had spoken in a language she did not understand.

She left the agreement where it was and took Tessa to the dentist.

Joren signed the following day.

***

Three weeks later, the Honest Hour devoted an episode to a patient at the Merrow clinic who had waited all night to see a physician.

The patient's account was real. She had been frightened and badly treated by a failing scheduling system. Her interview was painful to hear.

Joren began with sympathy. Then he expanded the story into a general indictment of the clinic and the people who defended its funding. He said the staff had become untouchable because of the political coalition that protected them.

He never mentioned Lev Orin by name.

He did not know that the clip would be sent to a large group accusing Lev's wife, Sela, of helping cover up the clinic's failures because Sela supported public services.

That afternoon a man came to the reception desk and demanded to know which doctors were members of Common Future.

The receptionist said that staff voting preferences were private.

The man recorded her refusal. He returned with two other people the next morning. They were loud but not violent. They knew enough about their rights to remain just inside the boundary where security could act.

Lev arrived to find a young nurse crying in the storeroom because her photograph had appeared on Lantern beneath an allegation that she would refuse to treat Renewal voters.

“It's not true,” the nurse said.

“I know.”

“Then tell them.”

Lev made a statement through the clinic. She was exact, calm, and factual. The statement circulated less widely than the accusation.

By Friday the nurse had asked for a transfer.

Another name disappeared from a rota.

At home Lev told Sela that the clinic was becoming a place where staff discussed patients only behind closed doors, not because of confidentiality, which had always been essential, but because someone might mistake an ordinary conversation for evidence of a political conspiracy.

Sela reached for her notebook.

Lev stopped her.

“Don't write it down yet. Just listen to me.”

Sela put down the pen.

“I have been listening.”

“I know. But lately everything I tell you becomes part of a pattern.”

Sela felt the accusation. Lev saw it and continued more gently.

“I'm your wife. Tonight I need to be your wife.”

Sela closed the notebook.

They sat together without trying to solve the country.

It was the most peaceful hour either of them had had in weeks.

***

Eva Maren attended the Honest Hour's first public event because she wanted to ask about its funding.

It was held in a converted warehouse beside the river. Two hundred people came, more than enough to surprise Joren. The foundation had supplied lights, a stage, and a camera crew. The banners were new. The volunteers wore matching shirts. A catering company had arrived with food.

Eva had covered local elections for four years. She knew how much an event like this usually cost. A failing garage mechanic had acquired professional media production in six weeks.

The audience was not composed of monsters.

A woman sitting beside Eva was caring for an elderly father and said the clinic had repeatedly lost his appointments. A man from the factory feared he would lose his pension. Another woman had been ignored by the council after dangerous mold appeared in her flat. They had come to be heard, and some were crying before the event began.

Joren took the stage and told a story about paying his apprentice when there was barely enough money left to pay himself. He was funny and direct. The audience loved him.

Then he spoke about politicians who no longer believed ordinary people deserved a country of their own.

“Who are the ordinary people?” Eva wrote in her notebook.

The room had an answer. People began chanting a word she had heard only a few times a year before.

**Renewal.**

The chant grew. The feeling of belonging was palpable, not manufactured in the narrow sense of being false. Many people genuinely felt it. What they did with it would matter.

After the event Eva waited by the loading bay while the stagehands dismantled the lights.

Joren emerged carrying a box of unsold merchandise.

“Mr. Fenn?”

“It's Joren.”

“Eva Maren. I sent questions about the Public Voice Foundation.”

“Oh, the paper.”

“That's me.”

“Look, I'm just a mechanic. I didn't ask to become a politician.”

“But you're running political events.”

“I'm talking to people about their problems.”

“Who paid for tonight?”

“Ask the foundation.”

“I did. They say the donors prefer anonymity.”

He shifted the box against his hip.

“Maybe they don't want you harassing them.”

“Then it should be easy for the foundation to show that it operates according to the rules.”

“Are you saying I'm doing something illegal?”

“No. I'm asking about money.”

He stared at her. A few months earlier, he would have answered a reporter by offering a cigarette and complaining about invoices. Now the question seemed to arrive already sharpened by a hundred arguments he had survived online.

“You've written your story already, haven't you?” he said.

“I haven't.”

“Then you'll be the first.”

He walked away.

Eva watched him disappear around the corner of the building. She made a note of the event's production company, the name on the catering vans, and the foundation's registered address. She would check each through lawful public records and ordinary interviews. None of those details, alone, would establish foreign involvement.

The evidence she wanted was a connection that survived scrutiny.

The story she feared was the one that would be published without one.

***

Niko Rahl listened to the recording of the Honest Hour inside a secure room in Veyr.

He had been asked to review a selection of southern media events, including those involving organizations that the department considered useful. The particular relationship between the department and any named event was not documented in the material before him.

He watched Joren speak. He watched the audience rise to its feet.

“Remarkable,” said his supervisor.

“Which part?”

“The growth.”

“The foundation is spending money. That explains some of it.”

“Not the enthusiasm.”

“No.”

Niko did not want to deny what he saw. The emotion in the room was real. But the event had been lit, recorded, promoted and distributed by people whose interests were not visible to its audience. That distinction mattered even if no single sentence had been scripted.

“Does he know?” he asked.

“Know what?”

“Who may ultimately benefit from his success.”

His supervisor folded the laptop shut.

“You're asking questions above your classification.”

That evening Niko was required to attend a birthday dinner for his sister's son. The boy was twelve and wanted a bicycle. The family argued about whether he was old enough to ride across the city. Niko took the boy's side. His sister called him irresponsible.

Everyone laughed, and the argument ended with a promise that the boy could ride to the park but not to the railway station.

It was the sort of disagreement that once would have meant nothing to Niko. Now he found himself studying it as though it were a specimen.

His sister noticed.

“Stop working,” she said.

“I'm not.”

“You are looking at us like we're a problem.”

He put down his glass.

“Sorry.”

The boy brought out a cake with an uneven candle in the middle. Niko sang with everyone else. When the boy blew out the candle, he made a wish he refused to share.

On his way home Niko passed the Ministry of Continuity. Its windows were lit. People were working late on his reports.

He wondered whether his country still possessed a room in which people could disagree about a bicycle without the argument eventually becoming useful to somebody.

***

The following Sunday, Joren found an envelope pushed under his garage door.

It contained photocopies of his new sponsorship agreement with three sections marked in red. Someone had written **ASK WHO OWNS THEM** across the top.

He stood in the workshop for a long time.

The simplest explanation was that a competitor wanted to embarrass him. The second simplest was that someone from the foundation had made a mistake. He did not yet have a reason to believe a foreign state was involved, nor a reason to dismiss the possibility entirely.

He took out his phone and called Mira.

“Can you come down?” he asked.

“Is everything all right?”

“I'm not sure.”

It was the first time in months he had admitted uncertainty before someone forced it out of him.

By the time she arrived, he had read the papers twice.

They sat on overturned paint buckets and examined every page.

“This doesn't say who they are,” Mira said.

“No.”

“Then you have to find out.”

He wanted to tell her she had been right. The words stood in his throat and would not move.

His phone vibrated. The producer wanted a comment on a confrontation outside the clinic. Thousands of people were waiting for his opinion, and somebody was offering to pay him to give it.

Joren looked at the contract. Then at the photograph of Tessa taped to the wall above his bench.

He let the call ring.

Once.

Twice.

On the third ring he answered.

“Joren,” said the producer, “we need you tonight.”

Joren closed his eyes.

“What happened?” he asked.

The producer began to explain. Before he had finished, Joren was already thinking of the words that would bring the applause back.

Mira watched him listening, and knew what the answer would be.

He had wanted to be heard.

He had not understood that, after a while, being heard could become a job he was afraid to lose.

# Chapter Seven — The Second Table

*Rowan Way, Merrow. The following August.*

The first invitation received nineteen replies.

Sela had posted it in the old neighborhood group on Lantern, beneath the photograph of the previous summer's supper.

**The table is back. Saturday the twenty-third, six o'clock. Bring whatever you've got. We can argue about the weather in person.**

Six people clicked a heart. Eight clicked that they were interested. Five said they were coming. By the next morning three of the five had changed their minds.

Somebody asked whether the gathering would be political.

Sela wrote: **It's supper.**

A man she barely knew responded: **Everything's political now.**

The sentence was awarded twelve little hearts. It seemed to settle the matter more effectively than any decision Sela might have made.

She went to see Lev in the kitchen.

“Am I being foolish?”

Lev was peeling potatoes. She had worked an eleven-hour shift and was still wearing the soft shoes she used at the clinic.

“About the supper?”

“About pretending we can have one.”

“Trying to feed people isn't pretending.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It is an answer. It just doesn't have a guarantee attached.”

Lev held out a peeled potato. Sela took it, kissed her cheek, and went to knock on doors.

Mrs. Kade was in the care home now. She had broken her hip in January and never quite trusted her legs again. Her house stood empty, curtains drawn, with an estate agent's sign at the front. Sela paused beside the gate. Mrs. Kade had never permitted the supper to begin without her terrible pickled onions. There would be no pickled onions this year.

At the Kellers' house, Jonas Keller said he might come, depending on who else was coming. His wife, Ada, emerged behind him and said they would definitely come; then they quarreled about that after Sela had left the step.

At number sixteen, Fen offered to bring the speaker.

“Please don't bring the one with the broken bass,” Sela said.

“That's all of them.”

“Then bring the least broken.”

At the Ardens' house, Mara opened the door with a telephone pressed to her ear. She covered the microphone.

“We'll try,” she said.

“Try?” Sela asked.

“There's a football meeting on Saturday.”

“After six?”

“Maybe.”

Sela saw Tomas in the hallway. He had come to hear who was at the door, but he remained behind Mara, one hand resting on the frame. They looked like strangers waiting for the same bus.

“Tell him I'll save a place,” Sela said.

Mara smiled, though not quite at her.

Outside, Sela marked the response in her notebook. Beside the Arden house she wrote **uncertain**.

By nightfall she had written it seven times.

***

The trouble started with the tables.

In the previous year the six sections had arrived without discussion. Everyone knew, or thought they knew, where the folding tables lived. One belonged to the Kellers, one to the Ardens, one to the house with the blue door; their ownership was less important than the habit of carrying them.

Now nobody could find the two supposedly belonging to Mrs. Kade. Her nephew had cleared the garage when she moved into care and sold the contents. Jonas Keller was unwilling to lend the two in his garage because he had just painted them and did not want the legs scraped on asphalt.

“You've always lent them,” Sela said.

“They aren't community property.”

“No one's saying they are.”

“That's what people say before they decide they are.”

Sela stared at him.

They had reached the point at which a request to borrow a folding table could become a referendum on property rights. She thanked him, walked away, and bought a cheap plastic one from the hardware shop.

It was white, hollow, and so light the wind shifted it across her drive.

Lev came out, caught the edge, and laughed.

“Perfect.”

“It's horrible.”

“So is the weather.”

For a moment they laughed as they once had, before every small difficulty acquired a theory about why it had occurred.

They had three tables, not six. Sela put them end to end in the street anyway.

***

Dain saw the invitation at work.

A colleague had printed a picture of Sela's post and pinned it to the locker room noticeboard with the words **COMMUNITY HARMONY INITIATIVE** scrawled across the top. There was a drawing of a green Common Future placard beside it.

“Your brother going?” someone asked.

“Don't know.”

“Bet he'll bring the union.”

Dain removed the paper and put it in the bin.

It made him angry, not because the joke was especially cruel, but because these men now presumed to know his brother from an argument Dain himself had not finished having. Tomas had repaired their father's old radio when Dain was fourteen. He had helped Dain pass an important technical examination, and had stayed all night at the hospital when Dain's wife miscarried, years before the marriage ended.

None of that was visible to the people in the locker room.

“I'm going,” Dain said.

His colleague looked surprised.

“You're serious?”

“It's dinner.”

“Right. Enjoy the politics.”

Dain almost told him to get lost.

Instead he folded his work jacket and thought about his nephew.

***

At six on Saturday, the sky was clear and the air smelled of dust after a week without rain.

Fen brought the speaker. Lev brought potato salad. Sela put out a tray of roasted vegetables, two loaves, and a bowl of fruit. A few children arrived and immediately began arguing over the chalk used to draw a football pitch on the pavement.

There were eleven adults.

The previous year's photograph showed twenty-six people, not counting the child who had taken the picture.

Sela counted the plates, then counted them again as though she might have missed a stack.

Jonas Keller came without Ada. He brought sausages in a paper bag and said he could stay for half an hour. The man from number eight arrived late, glanced at the people present, and went home to fetch a jumper. He never came back.

Mara arrived with Ivo. Tomas was not with them.

“He's on a late shift,” she said.

Sela knew the railway timetable had changed, and it might have been true. She also knew a person could make an excuse out of a true fact.

Ivo ran toward Fen and the other children. He had grown taller since the photograph and had begun holding his arms close to his body, self-conscious about how he looked. He was wearing the same old football shirt, though the sleeves ended above his wrists.

At half past six Dain appeared at the corner carrying a dish covered in foil.

Mara looked up.

For one moment she saw the man who had taught her to dance badly, the brother who had brought tools for jobs that didn't need them, and the person Tomas had now been avoiding for nine months. The history was so large that the politics ought to have been small beside it.

“Peppers,” Dain said.

“What?”

“I made peppers. You always said I burned them.”

“You always do.”

“Not these.”

She lifted a corner of the foil. They were burned.

She laughed. Dain laughed. Then both remembered that they were not supposed to be so easy with one another.

“Is Tomas coming?” he asked.

“He's working.”

“Right.”

There was a terrible awkwardness in the word. Dain set the dish on the table.

Ivo appeared beside him.

“You said we'd go fishing.”

Dain's smile stopped.

“I did.”

“You said next weekend.”

“That was a long time ago.”

“Every weekend is next weekend until you go.”

Dain looked down at the boy. For the first time all evening he seemed to have no opinion ready.

“You're right,” he said.

“When?”

“Tomorrow, if you like. I can get the rods.”

Mara opened her mouth. She thought of Tomas, of the way he went quiet when anyone mentioned Dain. She thought of the messages from her Common Future acquaintances, some of whom had begun warning one another not to confuse family loyalty with political conscience. She also thought of an eleven-year-old boy who had once sat on his uncle's shoulders to watch a match.

“Tomorrow is fine,” she said.

It was a small rebellion against a set of expectations no one had actually imposed on her in person.

Ivo grinned so widely that he forgot the dignity of being almost twelve.

For an hour the supper worked.

There were too few people and too many empty spaces, but they ate, and Fen played a song that Mrs. Kade would have hated. The children drew a goal on the pavement and kicked a ball between two overturned bins. Lev spoke to Ada Keller, who had eventually come after all. Somebody complained about the mayor's pothole.

The complaint traveled harmlessly down the table. Nobody asked whether the pothole was a Renewal pothole or a Common Future pothole.

Sela felt herself relax.

Then Jonas Keller asked Dain about the electrical outages.

“I saw your interview,” Jonas said.

Dain had given an interview to a small local channel about maintenance staffing and the waste of purchasing decisions. It was a good interview. He had been articulate and funny. The channel had posted a shortened version in which he seemed to blame public-sector workers for every failure in the system.

“I didn't recognize half of it,” Dain said.

Jonas shrugged. “They always edit.”

“Not like that.”

“It's what your Renewal people have been saying.”

Dain put down his fork.

“I don't have people.”

“That's not what the badge says.”

“I stopped wearing the badge.”

“Still a member.”

“Are you checking?”

Jonas laughed. It was a laugh of disbelief, not amusement.

Mara could have interrupted. Sela could have asked for the bread. Lev could have announced that the next person to discuss politics was doing the washing-up.

But they had become used to waiting for an argument to declare its allegiance. The interval before anyone acted grew long enough for other people to step in.

Ada said she had seen the full interview and Dain was right about the editing.

Jonas said there was no point defending a man whose movement wanted to cut the clinic.

Lev said nobody had proposed cutting the clinic at that meeting.

Someone said the clinic was already being cut.

Someone said that was because money was being wasted on outsiders.

Mara said the real waste was paying for an empty sports hall while children traveled across town.

It should have been possible to talk about all those things. They were real questions with real answers, though none of the answers would have been simple.

Instead each statement arrived wearing the uniform of its supposed owner.

A man from number twelve said, “We know which side you're on, Mara.”

She turned to him.

“I don't remember declaring a side.”

“Your photographs are all over Common Future.”

“They used a photograph from the sports campaign.”

“Same difference.”

The words struck her with unexpected force.

She had spent a year taking calls from parents who couldn't pay bus fares. She had sat through meetings and learned to understand municipal finance, and now the work had been reduced to a badge she had not chosen to wear.

Dain said, “She's trying to help children.”

Jonas replied, “Convenient that you defend her now.”

“She's my family.”

“Then explain why your brother won't sit at the same table.”

The sound afterward was not silence, exactly. It was the background noise of children still playing, the speaker playing softly, a plate scraping on plastic. But among the adults something stopped.

Dain stood.

“I'm not doing this.”

He took one step toward the pavement.

Sela said, “Please don't leave.”

“Then tell him to stop talking about my brother.”

Jonas folded his arms. “He doesn't need me to make him look bad.”

Dain picked up his dish, the burnt peppers almost untouched.

Mara looked at her son. Ivo was watching from beside the bins. He had stopped playing.

“Dain,” she said.

He paused.

“Tomorrow?”

He looked at Ivo, then back at Mara.

“Tomorrow.”

He left.

Ten minutes later Jonas and Ada went home separately. The man from number twelve said the atmosphere had been spoiled and there was no point staying. Fen turned off the speaker. The children started calling for their parents.

Sela carried the bowls inside. Lev followed her with the potato salad.

“Don't,” Lev said.

Sela had not yet started crying, but Lev knew her well enough to recognize the shape of it.

“I thought if they could just be in one place,” Sela said.

“I know.”

“We did this every year.”

“I know.”

“I don't understand how it happened.”

Lev set down the bowl.

“I think we understand parts of it. That's what makes it so awful.”

***

At eight-thirty the street was empty except for the plastic table.

Sela stood at the window and saw Mara and Ivo walking home. Ivo was saying something with both hands. Mara nodded, then took his hand. Their figures passed beneath the streetlight and vanished behind the Arden hedge.

A little later Tomas arrived home from the railway.

He had genuinely been working late.

Mara told him that Dain had come.

“He did?”

“He brought peppers.”

“Burned?”

“Completely.”

Tomas smiled in spite of himself.

“Ivo's going fishing with him tomorrow.”

The smile disappeared.

“You decided that without asking me?”

“He's his uncle.”

“I know who he is.”

“Then why are you acting like he isn't?”

Tomas unbuttoned his jacket slowly. He was exhausted from work and had arrived expecting supper, not an argument about a brother whose absence had become easier than his presence.

“I wish you'd told me.”

“I am telling you.”

“You know what I mean.”

Mara did know. She also knew that if she gave in, she would be telling her son that adult pride was more important than a promised fishing trip.

“He's going,” she said.

Tomas looked down at his hands.

“Fine.”

He meant it as a concession. It sounded like a verdict.

Ivo listened from the stairs. The fishing trip, which had seemed like the first good thing in months, had already become something his parents could use to hurt one another.

He went to his room without saying good night.

***

Across the sea, the Ministry of Continuity issued a private memorandum praising the summer's developments in Liora.

The language was technical: lower inter-group trust, diminished willingness to participate in shared associations, increased perception of irreconcilable civic identities. Several indicators were based on small and uncertain samples. Others were reliable enough to alarm the people who understood them.

On a chart prepared for Director Kest, the drop in participation was marked with a green arrow.

Niko Rahl pointed to it.

“Why green?”

“Because it's movement in the desired direction,” his supervisor said.

“But it's people stopping doing things together.”

“Yes.”

“They may be withdrawing because they are frightened, not because they agree with either coalition.”

“It doesn't matter to the project.”

Niko studied the arrow. He had previously assumed that his job was to distinguish outcomes. The department was teaching him that outcomes could be flattened into one word so long as the word sounded successful.

He found a public photograph from Merrow's neighborhood supper. There was a long gap between the people, and the table was short. He could not know the circumstances. The photograph had been posted by a young man whose speaker had been used at the event.

The caption read:

**Different crowd this year. Guess that's how things are now.**

Niko placed it beside the previous year's photograph in a folder marked **illustrative material**.

In the older picture there had been six tables, too many dishes, a woman laughing, and two brothers carrying furniture side by side.

In the new picture there were three tables, several people looking away, and a space at one end of the street where the chairs should have been.

The department's report did not mention the pictures. Kest preferred a graph.

***

The fishing trip took place on Sunday.

Dain arrived early, sober-faced, carrying two rods and a thermos. Tomas met him at the door. It was the first time the brothers had stood face to face since the night Dain had been told to leave.

“Morning,” Dain said.

“Morning.”

“I'll have him back by three.”

“Don't take him past the old weir. The banks are slippery.”

“I know.”

“You haven't been there in years.”

“Neither have you.”

The old shape of their argument stood up between them and waited to see whether it would be fed.

Dain looked at Ivo, who was already wearing his boots.

“Would you mind coming?” he asked Tomas.

The question startled all three of them.

Tomas saw what the day could be. Three people walking down to the river, thermos between them, the boy learning how to cast. He could almost feel the damp stones beneath his shoes. Then he remembered the things they had called one another, the months spent rehearsing a defense of his own pride.

“Can't,” he said. “I've got jobs.”

Dain nodded. Ivo's shoulders dropped a little, but he said nothing.

At the river, Ivo caught nothing.

Dain caught a small fish and let it go. They ate sandwiches on a concrete wall and talked about the insects on the water. Dain did not mention the supper. Ivo did not ask whether he and Tomas were friends again.

On the drive home, Ivo fell asleep with his head against the window.

Dain slowed near Rowan Way.

He could see the Arden house ahead. The garage door was open. Tomas was inside, working at an old wooden table. For a wild instant Dain thought of stopping, getting out, and carrying the broken hinge into the light with his brother.

He kept driving.

That evening the white plastic table Sela had bought was still standing on the pavement. Nobody had agreed where to store it.

A storm came through after midnight and tipped it onto its side.

By breakfast, one leg had snapped.

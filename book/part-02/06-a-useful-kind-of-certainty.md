# Chapter Six — A Useful Kind of Certainty

*Merrow, Republic of Liora. March. Five and a half years before the dissolution.*

The first person to call Tomas Arden a traitor was a man who had once borrowed his ladder.

It happened in the supermarket car park on a Tuesday, beneath a sky so low that the streetlights were on at three in the afternoon. Tomas was putting milk into the boot when a voice said, “Still stealing from the people, then?”

He turned.

Joren Fenn stood two spaces away, holding a bag of apples and wearing the expression of a man beginning a joke.

Tomas knew him well enough to wave at weddings. Joren fixed cars, played darts on Fridays, and had helped the street clear snow after the power failure two winters ago. His oldest daughter had attended football practice with Ivo.

“What?”

“The railway. Saw what your union was demanding. Nice work, if you can get it.”

“We're demanding that the signaling inspections don't get cut.”

“That's what you call it.”

“What would you call it?”

Joren moved the apples from one hand to the other.

“Look, mate, I don't want an argument.”

“Neither do I. You started one.”

The joke had gone from Joren's face. “That's the trouble with your lot. Can't even ask a question.”

He walked away. Tomas remained by the boot with a carton of milk freezing his fingers.

*Your lot.*

It was a small phrase, the kind people threw around without thinking. By the time Tomas reached home it had grown into a declaration: Joren no longer regarded him as an ordinary person doing a job. He regarded him as a member of a hostile species.

Tomas told Mara what had happened.

She was at the kitchen table with a letter from the municipality. The sports hall repairs were postponed again. The children's temporary transport scheme was being extended on paper, but the reimbursement funds had run out.

“Joren's an idiot,” she said without looking up.

“He didn't used to be.”

“People change.”

“I suppose.”

She glanced at his face and put down the letter.

“What happened?”

He told her. He expected that she would find it ridiculous, and perhaps she did, but when he finished she said, “You do sometimes talk as if everyone who wants changes to public services is trying to destroy them.”

Tomas looked at her.

“I'm talking about signaling inspections.”

“I know. I'm talking about the way you talk.”

There had been a time when he would have said that this was exactly the sort of thing she might say. They would have rolled their eyes and gone on with the evening. Now he heard the hidden accusation before he heard the concern.

“You sound like Dain.”

She flinched.

It was a cruel thing to say, precisely because he knew it would hurt.

“Maybe that's not always a bad thing,” she answered.

Then Ivo came in asking where the football boots were, and the argument dissolved into the more urgent work of finding two objects that ought to have been in one place.

The boots were in the airing cupboard.

Neither parent remembered putting them there.

***

The town meeting took place in a former cinema with worn velvet seats and a stage designed for a screen that had been removed in the previous century.

The municipality had invited residents to discuss the next budget. The factory outside Merrow was reducing its workforce again. The clinic had a six-week backlog. The roof of the sports hall still leaked. The mayor stood beside a projector displaying columns of numbers that were accurate, uncomforting, and impossible to read from the back.

Mara came with a folder of receipts. She had taken time off work and prepared three questions.

By the time she reached the microphone, the meeting had ceased to be about budgets.

A man wearing a white badge with a blue circle announced that the council was proof that Liora had been stolen from its proper owners. Several people cheered. On the other side of the aisle a woman held up a green placard calling for the defense of every public institution. Her neighbors cheered in return.

Both groups had come with printed signs.

Mara had come with receipts.

The white badges belonged to **Renewal**, a national movement that promised to free honest workers from a bloated and contemptuous state. Its meetings attracted shopkeepers facing high rents, tradespeople crushed by paperwork, pensioners who had watched their districts decline, and people who liked the simple relief of being told they were not responsible for anything that had gone wrong.

The green placards belonged to **Common Future**, a coalition that defended public services, collective bargaining, and the rights of people who had the least power to defend themselves. Its meetings attracted nurses, teachers, young tenants, railway workers, and people who had seen austerity arrive at their front doors with the manners of an accountant.

Neither movement had invented the underlying troubles. The rent really was high. Public services really were strained. Employers really did treat some workers badly. The difference was that, increasingly, each movement asked its supporters to interpret every ordinary problem as proof that the other movement was unfit to share a country with.

Mara stood beside the microphone while the mayor pleaded for quiet.

“I have the transport claims from the last three months,” she said.

A man shouted, “Are you with Renewal or not?”

She blinked. “I'm with the football club.”

Laughter came from scattered seats.

A woman in the third row called, “The mayor needs to answer her question.”

Then another voice: “You people ask for money for everything.”

Mara wanted to explain that some children had missed practice because their parents could not pay a bus fare in advance. She wanted to point out that the council had already promised the money and was now pretending a promise was a budget.

Instead she found herself defending the idea that children were worth transporting.

When she finally sat down, her hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the humiliating sensation of having brought a specific problem into a room that had become incapable of seeing it.

Afterward, in the aisle, a woman called Alia Venn handed her a business card.

Alia was local director of Common Future. She wore an expensive coat and spoke with the confidence of someone who could make an institution answer a telephone.

“You were the only person on that stage who knew the actual figures,” Alia said. “I thought you were magnificent.”

“I didn't get an answer.”

“You got their attention.”

“I needed the buses.”

“Come to our gathering on Thursday. You don't have to join anything. You should meet people who'll back you.”

Mara kept the card.

Outside, Dain was standing with three men from the power company, each wearing a white Renewal badge. He looked embarrassed to see her.

“Your numbers were good,” he said.

“Thank you.”

“The mayor's still an idiot.”

“I know.”

For several seconds they were merely a sister-in-law and a man who had once taught her son to ride a bicycle. Dain opened his mouth, perhaps to ask about Tomas.

One of the men called from the pavement, “Come on, Dain. The enemy's getting away.”

Dain grinned because his friends were watching.

“See you,” he said.

Mara watched them leave and wondered when an accounting problem had acquired an enemy.

***

Two days later, Tomas found a message from his union representative warning members not to speak publicly without authorization.

**We cannot afford to give Renewal ammunition**, it said.

The message irritated him. He had joined a union to protect his work, not to receive instructions about who he could talk to. He replied asking why union representatives were using the same language as the people they complained about.

The representative wrote back: **This is not the time for nuance, Tomas.**

Tomas stared at the sentence.

Nuance was what his father had called an excuse. His father had been a good man, though often wrong. He had said nuance was what educated people demanded just before taking something from somebody else.

Tomas had spent his adulthood believing the opposite: most things worth protecting were complicated, and understanding the complication was a form of respect.

Now the men who agreed with him were asking him to give that up.

He closed the message without answering.

That evening he found Ivo sitting on the stairs, trying to fix a broken wheel on a toy bus. Tomas knelt beside him.

“Want help?”

“I can do it.”

“Fair enough.”

Ivo examined the little plastic axle.

“Is Uncle Dain bad?” he asked.

Tomas felt the old anger come up, then another feeling behind it.

“No.”

“Joren's daughter says he is.”

“People say all sorts of things.”

“Is he bad at politics?”

“Your uncle's wrong about plenty of things.”

“But he's still good?”

Tomas took the toy bus and held the axle while Ivo pressed the wheel into place.

“People aren't one thing,” he said.

It was a sentence he believed.

The trouble was that, lately, he had been living as though he didn't.

***

In the northern capital, Dr. Irena Solvik received a briefing on the southern movements.

The report described them in compressed language that made their voters appear like weather patterns: areas of rising confidence, zones of grievance, projected response to elite statements, opportunities for institutional distrust. The authors had appended a diagram of the Republic of Liora in two colors.

Irena hated the diagram.

“What is this supposed to show?” she asked Niko.

“Political alignment.”

“The country is not two colors.”

“It's a simplification.”

“Of what?”

He pointed to a footnote. The classification excluded undecided citizens, local parties, mixed coalitions, and a large number of respondents who rejected the choices entirely.

“Then why is it in the executive summary?”

“Because Director Kest likes maps.”

She laughed despite herself.

Kest arrived ten minutes later, carrying the newest instructions from the Ministry of Continuity. The Directorate had approved another budget expansion. Several intermediary organizations were already spending funds in the south. Their activities varied, and not every organization had the same degree of knowledge about where support originated. The bureaucracy took care not to provide one neat line connecting every transaction to the seventh floor.

“You have an excellent opportunity,” Kest said, examining the two-color map.

“To study?” Irena asked.

“To assist history.”

He seemed to think this was a modest phrase.

Niko displayed a second page: a measure of whether citizens still trusted someone who held an opposing political view to perform an ordinary duty. The line was declining.

“We should be cautious about attributing that to us,” he said. “The economic shocks are real. The platform changes are independent. Some domestic organizations have substantial funding of their own.”

Kest gave him the patient smile of a man observing a child who had misunderstood an exercise.

“Nobody has asked for a confession.”

“I'm asking for a distinction.”

“Then make it in the appendix.”

After the meeting Irena read Niko's notes. He had included the number of Liorans who still identified with neither camp. It was larger than either directorate estimate.

He had also written a sentence on a separate sheet.

*The risk is not that people disagree. The risk is that every institution becomes a test of belonging.*

Irena crossed it out.

“Too literary,” she said.

“Is it wrong?”

“No. It isn't measurable as you've written it.”

“I can define the variables.”

“I know you can.”

She put the page in a confidential drawer instead of the wastebasket.

Outside, the great avenue filled with buses carrying workers to their homes. The city looked peaceful from the seventh floor. Irena had learned not to ask whether peace and obedience meant the same thing.

***

On Thursday Mara went to the Common Future gathering.

It was held in a warm meeting room above a bakery. Someone made tea. People introduced themselves by name, offered chairs, asked about her son. The room contained retired nurses, young parents, a disabled student, two men recently laid off from the factory, and an older woman who wanted the town to stop wasting money on decorative paving while the clinic struggled.

For the first forty minutes, Mara felt she had discovered the Merrow she remembered. They were serious. They were kind. They listened when she spoke about bus fares.

Alia introduced her to a lawyer who volunteered to help clubs appeal municipal decisions. Mara wrote down his number. The meeting had accomplished more for her football players than six weeks of angry posts.

Then the conversation turned toward the upcoming regional election.

Alia said there were people who wanted to dismantle the social fabric of Liora. A young man at the window said Renewal supporters ought to be exposed wherever they worked. Someone else said that was wrong.

“It's wrong,” Alia agreed. “We're not here to threaten people.”

But she did not object when the same young man said anyone still defending Renewal was choosing to hurt children.

Mara thought of Dain. She thought of him changing Ivo's bicycle tire without charge, spending an afternoon helping an elderly neighbor carry a refrigerator, and shouting at a public meeting about waste.

She might have said, *It's possible for someone to be wrong about spending and still care about children.*

She did not.

The group was full of people who had recognized her competence. She did not want to be the person who spoiled the first pleasant evening she had enjoyed in months.

On the train home, she received a message from Alia.

**You belong with us.**

Mara read it twice.

At home Tomas was asleep on the sofa with the television on. There was a plate in the oven and a note beside it.

**Saved you supper. Love you.**

Mara warmed the plate, ate standing at the counter, and looked again at Alia's message.

She was grateful for the supper.

She was grateful for the invitation.

The second feeling was louder.

***

In Merrow's library, Sela Orin opened a new notebook.

On the first page she drew two columns, then crossed out the headings. She did not want a list of good people and bad people. She wanted a record of what the town still did together.

She wrote down the reading group. The football club. The winter clothing drive. The volunteer fire fund. The annual street supper. The elderly neighbors who needed somebody to notice if their curtains stayed closed.

Then she began to write down the absences.

She did not know what the list would prove. It might prove nothing but that one town was going through a bitter year.

She kept it anyway.

On the opposite page she wrote a question.

*When did people begin to need permission from their own side to be kind to someone on the other?*

She underlined it once, and left enough space beneath it for an answer.

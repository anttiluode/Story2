# Chapter Twenty-Four — The Empty Table

*Rowan Way, Southern Administrative Territory. Late summer.*

There were six empty spaces in the garage where the folding tables had been.

Ivo remembered their shape better than he remembered their owners. Two had gone to the Kellers years ago, before the couple moved north to live with Ada's sister. One had been sold when the house with the blue door changed hands. One had broken beyond repair. A fifth had disappeared into the municipal storage system during an event nobody could now name.

The last had been kept against the garage wall until Tomas needed the space for a replacement motor.

Ivo did not know where it was.

He had come looking for a bicycle pump.

The garage smelled of dust, oil, and old paint. On a shelf above the workbench sat the can of yellow paint Tomas had bought seventeen years before. Its lid was rusted into place. The label had faded, but the color sample remained visible: a pale, hopeful shade that nobody in the family had ever wanted on a wall.

He shook the can.

Nothing moved.

He set it down and found the pump beneath a sack of cables.

Outside, the street was quiet.

Not the quiet of a sleeping neighborhood. It was the quiet of doors closed by people who no longer expected anyone to knock.

***

Mara did not live on Rowan Way anymore.

She had moved to a small flat near the center of Merrow the previous year, after months of conversations with Tomas that made no progress and then, unexpectedly, one that did. They had agreed that continuing to share a house was hurting them both. The separation was not a legal divorce; the new administrative system had delayed several family-law processes during the transfer of authority.

They had moved her books in cardboard boxes.

Tomas helped carry the heavy ones.

On the last afternoon, Mara found the photograph of the day they had collected the keys. She stood in the hall with the picture in her hand, younger versions of herself and Tomas smiling outside an empty house.

"I don't know when we stopped being those people," she said.

Tomas set down a box.

"We were never only those people."

She nodded.

It was one of the kindest things he had said to her in years.

For a moment they looked at each other as though a different future might still be possible.

Then the removal van arrived.

There was no dramatic final argument. No betrayal to simplify the story. They had simply spent too long making each other pay for things neither could fully explain.

Mara's new flat had two rooms and a view of the bus station. She continued working with children's sport, though the clubs had been reorganized under the Southern Youth Service and several of the people she had once worked alongside would no longer speak to her.

Some believed she had betrayed Common Future by opposing blanket restrictions on political opponents. Others believed she remained a loyal servant of a movement that had helped destroy the republic.

She was none of those things as fully as the messages claimed.

She was a woman trying to find enough money for children to get to a football pitch.

When Ivo visited, she made his favorite stew.

She always asked about Tomas.

He always said his father was fine.

They both knew he was simplifying.

***

At seventeen, Ivo had learned how to fill in the new forms.

The country field no longer offered Liora.

Instead it offered **Southern Administrative Territory**, followed by a number denoting the region. The form required him to acknowledge the authority of the Joint Continuity Council and confirm his eligibility for a new identity document.

Without the document, he could not enroll in several higher training programs, cross the northern-controlled border checkpoints, or obtain a number required by employers under the new system.

Some people refused to apply.

They kept the old Lioran passports in drawers and argued that the compact had been unconstitutional. They were not wrong about the serious legal objections. Several foreign governments still regarded the old republic as unlawfully dissolved.

But the local registry office no longer recognized the old passport as sufficient identification for many ordinary purposes.

Ivo sat at the kitchen table with the form.

Tomas had gone to work.

The question was simple on the page.

**Do you accept the terms of identification issued by the Southern Administrative Authority?**

Ivo stared at the box.

He thought of Sami, who had come from Tarven and knew what it meant to have a house, a school, and a country whose official name changed while the memories remained. Sami was applying for a technical apprenticeship. His family had already lived with the practical cruelty of documents that no longer matched places they called home.

Ivo had asked what he would do.

"Apply," Sami said.

"Even though you hate the new government?"

"I don't have to love a number to use it."

"Isn't that agreeing?"

"To the number? Yes."

Ivo laughed.

Sami did not.

"Don't make your whole future into a protest only the clerk can see," he said.

Ivo remembered the conversation while holding the form.

Then he thought of Sela, whose refusal to deny evidence had been written down as a symptom. He did not want to mistake survival for agreement. He also did not want the state to decide the terms on which his life could begin.

He checked the box.

The pen moved across the paper.

He felt no relief.

He felt as though he had performed an ordinary administrative task on the grave of something nobody had bothered to bury.

***

On the afternoon of the anniversary, Ivo visited Lev Orin.

She still lived across the street.

The crooked coat hook remained beside the door. Sela's scarf hung from it, washed and folded over the wood. The little plant Lev had carried home from the clinic had grown tall enough to lean toward the window.

Lev looked older. She still worked as a physician, though not in the supervisory position she had lost. Her review had eventually ended without the worst threatened sanction, but the letter of reprimand remained in her file. She had been offered a route back into management if she would sign a statement accepting the regional assessment system's legitimacy without reservation.

She had not signed.

"Hello, Ivo," she said.

He handed her a paper bag.

"Mum made bread."

Lev looked inside.

"Tell her thank you."

"She said it was better than last time."

"Then she's learning."

They smiled.

On the table were two plates.

Ivo had been here many times since Sela was taken away. At first the second plate had upset him. He thought it was a sign Lev could not accept what had happened. Now he understood it differently. She accepted the fact of her wife's absence every morning. She simply refused to organize the house as though the absence were permanent or deserved.

"Any news?" he asked.

"Another review next month."

"Do you think this one will work?"

Lev sat down.

"Aron thinks we've got a good argument."

"He's thought that before."

"Yes."

Ivo looked at the scarf.

"Does she know the country is gone?"

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

Lev rested her hands on the table.

"She asked whether the library had been given a new name."

"Has it?"

"It's the Merrow Community Information Centre now."

"Same books?"

"Most of them."

"Same building?"

"Yes."

Ivo considered this.

"She'll hate that."

"She laughed when I told her."

"Why?"

"Because the new name is longer and less useful."

He laughed too.

Then Lev's face changed.

"She asked whether the records were still there."

"Are they?"

"Some. The older material is being transferred to a regional archive. We have copies of the most important things."

Ivo nodded.

He had always associated Sela with books, not with the possibility of someone needing permission to read them.

"Can I visit her?"

"She can request it. The approval isn't automatic."

"Why not?"

Lev looked at him.

He was seventeen. He had asked variations of that question for years. The answer remained as ugly as it had been when he was thirteen.

"Because the authority holding her decides who can come," she said.

He looked down at the bread.

***

Dain lived three streets away in a small rented house.

He had lost his job with the municipal power service during the transfer to the Southern Energy Coordination Office and then been hired by a private contractor to do almost the same work for less security. He could still fix anything that had a wire, a motor, or a hinge. He had become less willing to discuss what he believed about the country.

On weekends he sometimes went fishing alone.

Ivo visited him on Sunday.

Dain was cleaning a reel at the kitchen table. Beside it lay Mrs. Kade's old book about machines.

"You still have that?" Ivo asked.

"Of course."

"Didn't know you kept it out."

"Reminds me to read."

Ivo picked up the book.

Inside the cover, the teacher's handwriting had faded.

*A difficult word is only a word you haven't learned yet.*

"Did you and Dad ever go fishing together after that funeral?"

Dain paused.

"No."

"Why?"

"We were busy."

Ivo gave him a look.

Dain put down the reel.

"We were angry."

"About what?"

"Everything."

"Still?"

Dain rubbed his hands.

"Some of it."

Ivo was nearly as tall as he was now. Dain could no longer answer him with the easy half-truths adults used for children.

"I should have called him," Dain said.

"You did sometimes."

"Not enough."

"Why didn't you?"

Dain looked toward the window.

"After a while, admitting I missed him felt like letting him win."

Ivo sat down opposite him.

"Win what?"

Dain shook his head.

"Exactly."

They were quiet for a while.

Then Ivo asked whether he still had the spare fishing rod.

"Of course."

"Can I borrow it?"

"Whenever you like."

He almost asked whether his father could come with them.

He did not.

The old failure lived in the family now, passed from one generation to another as a method for keeping the peace.

***

Tessa Fenn worked part-time in a workshop that repaired bicycles and small electrical devices.

Joren had sold the garage after a sequence of financial disputes involving the Honest Hour. He had managed to close the programme formally, but recordings of his old broadcasts continued circulating, detached from their dates and contexts. For some people he remained a courageous public figure. For others he was a paid instrument of the northern operation.

The documented evidence had never established that he knew the ultimate source of every payment to his media foundation.

He had nevertheless been responsible for many things he said.

He no longer argued with strangers about the distinction.

He worked at a repair depot outside town and came home too tired to open social media. Mira stayed with him. Their marriage had survived, though not untouched.

Tessa was seventeen too.

She had become good at small machines. When a motor failed, she listened to it before taking it apart. When a bearing seized, she checked the alignment. She had learned from her father that repairs began with attention, and from his public life that confidence could make a person overlook the obvious.

Ivo saw her outside the bicycle shop in September.

She was carrying a box of bearings.

He almost walked past.

"Tessa," he said.

She stopped.

"Ivo."

For a moment they were two children in a workshop again, arguing about whether steel or wood would make a better hinge.

He pointed to the box.

"Still fixing things?"

"Somebody has to."

"Good."

"How's Sami?"

"Fine. Apprenticeship started."

"That's good."

There was a pause.

"I kept the bridge," she said.

He looked at her.

"The old drawbridge. School let me take it when they cleared the workshop."

"Does it work?"

"Yes."

"Even the hinge?"

She smiled, barely.

"Especially the hinge."

He wanted to ask why she had never answered his message. She wanted to tell him that she had read it a hundred times. The years had made the old silence less urgent and more difficult to undo.

Instead she asked whether he was studying engineering.

"Trying to."

"You'll be good at it."

"Maybe."

She shifted the box against her hip.

"Take care, Ivo."

"You too."

She went inside.

He stood outside the shop.

They had not reconciled their friendship. They had not become enemies either. They had exchanged a few ordinary words without making the encounter prove anything larger.

It was both less and more than either had once imagined possible.

***

That evening Ivo returned to Rowan Way with the fishing rod.

The air smelled of rain and distant wood smoke. The new administrative flag hung from the school building at the end of the street. Its colors were different from those he remembered, but the building itself was unchanged.

His father was working in the garage.

"Borrowed a rod from Dain," Ivo said.

Tomas looked up.

"Good."

"He said you can come."

Ivo had not heard Dain say that.

He had grown tired of the years in which every invitation traveled through him like a message between hostile governments. For once he wanted to place a possibility in the room and see whether either man would refuse it.

Tomas put down the tool.

"Did he?"

Ivo looked at the rod.

"He didn't say you couldn't."

Tomas laughed softly.

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

"Maybe I'll ask him."

"Will you?"

Tomas was quiet.

The boy's voice had changed, but the question was the same one he had asked when he was eleven.

*Are you still brothers?*

Tomas looked toward the shelf where the yellow paint stood.

"I should," he said.

Ivo waited.

His father picked up the tool again.

The moment passed.

***

The photograph was in a box beneath the stairs.

Ivo found it while searching for the old passports. He had wanted to compare the symbols, the dates, the language on the cover, and the places where a country described its right to issue a document. His old passport had expired. The new one bore the emblem of the Southern Administrative Territory.

The box contained school certificates, a broken watch, an old football shirt, and several photographs.

One showed Mara and Tomas outside the house before they married.

One showed Ivo as a baby.

The last was the neighborhood supper.

Six tables. Too much food. A child with fingers in his nose. Mrs. Kade leaning forward as though she were about to tell the mayor something he would dislike. Dain and Tomas smiling near the middle. Mara carrying a bowl. Sela standing beside Lev, both looking toward someone outside the frame.

Ivo held the picture.

He remembered the power going out that evening. He remembered the lanterns and the feeling of sitting beneath the dark sky while adults continued laughing as though darkness were only a small change in the arrangements.

He could not remember what anyone had been laughing about.

For years he had tried to determine the exact moment his country went wrong.

Was it the first argument between Tomas and Dain? Mara's first widely shared post? The election? The law? The northern file? The day Sela was taken away? The flag lowering?

He knew now that there was no single moment.

There had been a series of choices, some made far away by people who wanted harm, others made close to home by people who wanted safety, belonging, vindication, or simply an end to exhausting arguments.

The choices had accumulated.

So had the absences.

He took the photograph outside.

***

Sela's old white plastic table was in Lev's shed.

The broken leg had been braced years before, and a corner was chipped. It was not one of the six wooden folding tables from the first supper. It was the replacement Sela had bought when people stopped lending theirs.

Ivo had helped Lev clear the shed once and knew where it stood.

He knocked at the door.

Lev opened it.

"Can I borrow the table?"

She looked surprised.

"What for?"

"Nothing special."

She studied him.

"Then take it."

He carried it across Rowan Way and set it in front of the Arden house.

It was shorter than he remembered.

The legs were stiff. One did not lock properly. He adjusted the brace, pressed the hinge, and managed to make the table stand level.

Then he brought out the photograph and placed it in the middle.

For a moment he considered setting six plates around it.

He did not.

He had no reason to expect six people to come. Mara was at her flat. Dain was at home. Sela was at Eastmere. Mrs. Kade was dead. The Kellers lived elsewhere. The old neighborhood had been rearranged by choices and losses that could not be reversed by a sentimental gesture.

Tomas came out of the house.

"What are you doing?"

"Looking at the photograph."

"Why outside?"

"Thought this was where it belonged."

Tomas stood beside him.

He looked at the picture for a long time.

"That's the night the power went out," he said.

"I remember."

"Dain thought he'd fixed it himself."

"He hadn't?"

"No. He just knew it would come back."

Tomas smiled.

He reached toward the photograph, then drew his hand away.

"We ought to put it somewhere safe."

"I know."

Neither moved.

A few people passed along the street. One nodded. Another looked at the table and hurried on. There was no music. No one brought potato salad. No one called from a doorway asking whether more chairs were needed.

A light came on in Lev's house.

Tomas went inside to make tea.

Ivo remained beside the table until the evening grew cold.

***

At the end of the street, the new flag moved in the wind.

The school bell rang.

A bus stopped at the corner and let off three people carrying groceries. The road workers' repaired drain lay beneath smooth asphalt. The same rain fell on the same roofs that had sheltered generations of Liorans.

The buildings remained.

The people remained.

The old country existed in photographs, memories, legal arguments, and the stubborn language of people who refused to call it something else. But it no longer possessed the institutions through which it could decide its own future. It could not issue its own passports, appoint its own ambassadors, control its own borders, or command the authority that governed its territory.

The Republic of Liora was gone.

Ivo folded the table.

The repaired leg caught against the frame. He adjusted it once, then again, until it closed.

He carried the table back to Lev's shed and left it where she could find it.

When he returned, the photograph lay on the doorstep.

He picked it up.

Inside the house, Tomas had placed two mugs on the kitchen counter without thinking. One was for himself and one for the son who had grown old enough to leave.

Ivo put the photograph beside them.

"Tea?" Tomas asked.

"Yeah."

They sat at the table.

There was room for more people.

Nobody else came.

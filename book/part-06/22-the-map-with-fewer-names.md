# Chapter Twenty-Two — The Map With Fewer Names

*The Republic of Liora. Three years later.*

On the morning Liora's last sovereign parliament met, the road outside the Arden house was being resurfaced.

The council had arranged it before the winter, when Merrow still had an elected administration with enough authority to approve a road contract. The workers had arrived in two orange vehicles, put cones across Rowan Way, and begun cutting a rectangle around a drain that had collapsed beneath the weight of a delivery truck.

The work made a horrible noise.

Tomas Arden watched from the upstairs window while he tied his boots.

He was fifty now. His back hurt when he bent to repair low-mounted signaling cabinets. The railway had changed employers twice in three years, first through an emergency maintenance contract and then through a transfer of responsibility to the National Continuity Authority. His pay arrived on time, although the amount had become difficult to predict. He still knew where every dangerous crossing on his route stood.

Downstairs, Ivo was making toast.

At seventeen he was taller than his father and had acquired the habit of checking a room before entering it, as though every doorway might contain an argument he would be expected to settle. He had an examination the following week. The school had told students the examination would still count under the new qualification framework, although nobody could say which authority would award the certificates.

"Can you turn it down?" Ivo called.

Tomas looked toward the machines.

"The road?"

"Yes."

"Not unless you want to go ask them."

"Could try."

"They'd tell you to wait."

"That's what everybody tells me."

Tomas smiled. He had learned to accept his son's jokes as jokes, even when they arrived with an edge.

There was a new radio on the kitchen counter. It was cheap, plastic, and made in a country neither of them could locate easily on a map. The public broadcaster had changed its name twice. The presenters still sounded Lioran.

At eight, the newsreader announced that parliament would convene for an extraordinary session at noon to consider the **Compact of Civic Administration**.

Tomas put down his toast.

"That's today?"

"Apparently," Ivo said.

"Thought they'd delayed it."

"They delayed the vote. Not the country."

The boy had acquired a dry way of speaking that Tomas sometimes mistook for maturity. It was a defense. There were too many subjects on which sincerity invited a lecture.

Ivo looked at the clock.

"Do we get new passports if they pass it?"

Tomas stared at him.

"We still have passports."

"Old ones."

"Yes."

"The school says we'll need new numbers for the examination register."

"Then the school can wait."

Ivo took another bite.

"That's what everybody tells me," he said again.

***

For nearly three years the government had tried to govern a country whose essential institutions no longer trusted one another.

The Northern Directorate of Veyr had not invaded Liora with the same open force it had used in Tarven. Its soldiers had remained on the far side of the gulf, and its officials continued to deny that the southern republic was the object of a covert campaign.

But Veyr controlled important commercial routes, supplied fuel through companies that had survived the sanctions, and had acquired influence over creditors willing to lend to Liora's failing agencies. A succession of economic emergencies had given the northern government leverage that a military force might not have achieved without a war.

Liora's troubles were not all imported.

The factories had been closing for reasons that began long before the Directorate's operation. Housing costs were high. The emergency government had made poor decisions. Some private contractors had behaved dishonestly. Rival movements had learned to punish compromise. Civil servants had been driven from their posts by threats, exhaustion, and the knowledge that any decision might be interpreted as a political allegiance.

Nera Valen's coalition had collapsed eighteen months earlier after disputes over emergency financing and the continued use of the Public Disorder Act. Renewal, led by Garran Holt, had helped block several budgets while demanding a fresh election. The caretaker administration remained in office through a series of extensions contested in court.

An election had eventually been held.

Turnout was low. The counting procedures functioned in most districts, but entire regions refused to recognize the elected national authority. The result produced another parliament without a workable majority. Constitutional appeals moved through courts whose orders were increasingly ignored by agencies claiming that compliance would endanger essential services.

A country could survive one government failing to form.

It could survive an unfair contract, an angry protest, an incompetent minister, or an election that left people bitter.

Liora was becoming unable to survive the absence of any agreed institution capable of resolving those failures.

When emergency fuel shipments stopped arriving at the ports, rival councils accused one another of sabotage. When the railway lost power at a central junction, one regional authority refused to release technicians employed under the other authority's contract. When a high court ordered the release of several people detained without adequate clinical grounds, the regional administrative office responsible for their custody said the order required clarification under the emergency legislation.

The law existed.

Its reach depended on who still agreed to obey it.

***

At the beginning of the final winter, the Northern Directorate offered a proposal.

Veyr would guarantee fuel shipments, stabilize the southern currency, assume several outstanding public obligations, and provide security coordination for critical infrastructure.

In exchange, Liora would transfer control of its foreign policy, border administration, monetary authority, and national security institutions to a new supranational body.

The proposal was called a compact because it sounded like a promise between equals.

The compact contained no clause requiring the Northern Directorate to accept direction from Liora's parliament.

It placed the governing authority of the south under a **Joint Continuity Council** on which Veyr appointed the chair and held a permanent security veto.

It allowed local municipal administrations to continue providing services under regional commissioners.

It required the Republic of Liora to relinquish its separate sovereign legal personality when the compact entered into force.

The last clause was on page forty-seven.

A legal adviser named Taren Esh found it immediately.

She had worked in the Justice Ministry for twenty years, and had spent the preceding five keeping track of emergency legislation that began with a promise to be temporary. She read the clause aloud to the people sitting beside her.

"This dissolves the state," she said.

The official opposite her replied that it reorganized governance.

"Those are different things."

"People will still live here."

"Of course."

"Schools will still operate."

"Schools operate in territories that are not sovereign countries."

The official looked tired.

"People need fuel."

"I know."

"Then we have to be practical."

Taren put down the compact.

"This is not an amendment to a ministry's powers. This is the end of the republic's authority to decide what it is."

Her objection entered the official minutes.

In the version prepared for public release, it appeared as **a concern regarding the distribution of competencies**.

***

Prime Minister Valen, no longer able to command a majority, attended the final negotiating session as a caretaker leader.

She had aged visibly. The woman who once explained the finer points of labor law on television now spoke in shorter sentences, as though every word carried a cost.

Garran Holt sat across from her.

They had spent years calling one another threats to the country. Now neither commanded enough trust to govern it.

"The public won't accept this," Valen said.

Holt looked at the draft.

"The public won't accept you telling them what to accept."

"Is that really what you want to argue about today?"

He met her eyes.

For a moment the old reflex left him.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

They examined the compact together.

Veyr's representatives had offered guarantees that sounded generous in the short term: funding for hospitals, protection of pension payments, reopening of trade, a timetable for infrastructure investment. There were clauses requiring administrative transparency and the protection of ordinary civil rights. Several of the guarantees had no independent enforcement mechanism.

Holt pointed to the section establishing regional commissioners.

"We could keep our local elections."

"Only for bodies whose powers the Council permits," Valen said.

"People care about local services."

"They care about whether someone else can overrule them."

He looked at her.

"Are we supposed to let the hospitals close while we defend a principle?"

She heard the question the way she had heard it throughout the emergency years. The choice was presented as if it were between medicine and a constitution, bread and a parliament, a warm house and the right to govern oneself.

She had once been able to explain why those were not always separate things.

Now she could no longer guarantee that the republic would supply the bread or heat.

"I don't know," she said.

They sat in silence.

It was the first honest conversation either had allowed themselves with the other in years.

It was far too late to serve as the beginning of a government.

***

The caretaker parliament met under heavy security.

Its members were not all present. Several regions had ceased sending representatives. Some members boycotted the session because they regarded it as unlawful. Others attended because they believed that refusing to vote would leave no record of objection.

The speaker read the compact's title.

The galleries were partly empty. The public had been told the session might attract violence. Buses had been rerouted. The square outside parliament was divided by barriers to keep rival demonstrations apart.

The arguments lasted all afternoon.

A deputy from the coastal region called the compact the only way to prevent the collapse of essential services.

A former judge said that no emergency legislature could surrender the people's sovereignty without a constitutional mandate.

A Renewal representative insisted the old republic had failed ordinary citizens and that the new council would finally make infrastructure work.

A Common Future representative replied that replacing elected government with a northern veto was not repair but submission.

Several members who had spent years insulting one another agreed with the strongest parts of both speeches. They did not know how to vote.

A motion to refer the compact to a referendum failed. Officials argued that a national referendum could not be safely organized or independently recognized in every region within the deadline set by the agreement.

The court had not certified such a deadline as constitutionally necessary.

The deadline nevertheless governed the afternoon.

At seven forty-six, the speaker called the final vote.

The compact passed by a narrow majority of those present.

The constitutional validity of the vote was disputed immediately. The highest court received an urgent petition from members of several parties. A panel later ruled that the caretaker parliament had exceeded its powers in approving the surrender of sovereignty without the required constitutional process.

By then the institutions that might have enforced the ruling were already being transferred.

The Northern Directorate recognized the compact as valid.

Several foreign governments refused to recognize it. Others opened relations with the new administration to protect trade and the safety of citizens. Diplomatic recognition became another argument about names.

The facts on the ground moved faster than the arguments.

***

On the morning the compact took effect, the national flag was lowered from the government building in the capital.

It was not torn down by a mob.

A ceremonial officer loosened the rope. The cloth descended slowly, its colors bright against a gray sky. A small crowd stood behind barriers. Some were crying. Others applauded. Several had come with no sign or political emblem because they wanted to witness what happened.

The flag was folded according to the old protocol.

The ceremonial officer, who had served three governments and believed fiercely in doing his work properly, placed it in a case.

A new banner was raised: the emblem of the Joint Continuity Council, combining the northern star with a design intended to represent the southern regions.

It had been prepared by a design office that had never held a public competition.

The music played.

A government broadcaster announced the **commencement of the Southern Administrative Territory**.

In the same hour, the Republic of Liora's foreign ministry ceased issuing diplomatic instructions. The consulates were directed to transfer their records. The central bank's authority passed to a financial office established by the Council. Border officers received new badges and instructions from a northern-appointed commissioner.

The Lioran parliament was suspended as a sovereign legislature. Its chambers were assigned to a transitional advisory assembly with no independent authority over foreign affairs, security, or currency.

The republic was not merely under a different government.

Its legal power to act as a country had been surrendered and replaced.

That morning, the Republic of Liora ceased to exist as a sovereign state in the territory its new administrators controlled.

***

In Veyr, Director Kest watched the ceremony from an office with a view over the river.

The broadcast was delayed by three seconds.

Dr. Irena Solvik stood beside him. She had been invited because her department's work was listed among the contributing factors in the Directorate's strategic assessment.

The room contained officials who understood the end of Liora as a success in different ways.

A trade official welcomed the new access to ports. A security adviser praised the removal of a government that had supplied Tarven. A finance officer discussed repayment schedules for southern obligations. Kest seemed particularly satisfied by the lack of military photographs.

"Not a single northern tank in their capital," he said.

Irena watched the flag descend.

"Not yet," she replied.

He looked at her.

"Don't spoil the occasion."

She said nothing.

The report on her desk still contained uncertainty estimates showing that much of Liora's political deterioration could not be attributed cleanly to the northern program. There had been domestic economic failures, political ambition, commercial incentives, and choices made by people who had never received money from Veyr.

Kest did not dispute that in private. He simply did not consider it relevant to the victory.

"A country with an army can be occupied," he said. "A country that can no longer agree who commands it is something else."

Irena looked again at the empty flagpole.

There were a thousand ways she might have refused to help make this day possible. She had chosen not to use any of them.

A secretary entered with a list of officials proposed for national honors.

Her name was near the top.

***

At Eastmere, the change of state appeared as a new form.

Sela Orin had been confined for more than three years. Her lawyer had filed appeals, received partial rulings, and challenged repeated extensions of administrative detention. Dr. Oren Pell's original assessment remained in the record. The second opinion had been cited more frequently. Later reviews described her continuing insistence on the documented northern program as evidence of inadequate progress.

During the transition, Eastmere was transferred from a Lioran regional authority to the Southern Health Coordination Service.

The nameplate on the door changed.

The people who worked there were largely the same.

Ines had stayed. Lina, whose illness had improved with care, had been discharged long ago and had sent a card through Lev. A few patients with genuine treatment needs came and went. The emergency wing remained occupied.

Sela was shown a form asking her to acknowledge the new administrative authority.

"Does this affect my appeal?" she asked.

The clerk said all pending cases would be reviewed for conformity with the continuity framework.

"Does that mean the old court order still stands?"

"We'll have to check."

She asked for a copy.

"Of course."

The copy did not arrive that week.

In her room, she took out the sheets of paper she had begun writing on the first morning. There were more of them now, folded, annotated, and worn soft at the corners. Ines had helped her obtain paper when the property system delayed requests. Sela had kept a private chronology of hearings, letters, visits, and the facts behind the investigation.

She added a date.

**The republic ended today.**

For several minutes she stared at the words.

The country was not a person. It had no heart to stop or lungs to fail. Its roads, buildings, and rivers remained. Its people still spoke the same language and cooked the same food.

But the institution through which they had once been able to call themselves sovereign no longer existed.

She had warned about the loss of the habits that made collective life possible.

Now the authority that confined her belonged to the people whose operation she had helped expose.

***

In Merrow, the road workers finished at four.

The cones were removed. Fresh asphalt lay around the repaired drain. A supervisor photographed the work and submitted the completion record to an administrative system whose logo had changed that morning.

Tomas stood at the gate.

"Good job," he told the nearest worker.

The man looked up, surprised.

"Thanks."

He climbed into the truck.

Ivo came outside carrying his school bag.

"Did they pass it?"

"Yes."

"The compact?"

"Yes."

"What happens now?"

Tomas looked along Rowan Way.

The houses were still there. The Kellers' hedge was overgrown. The Orins' curtains were closed at one window. Mrs. Kade's house had been sold to a couple who kept their distance from the neighbors. The streetlights would come on at dusk, if the new service contract held.

"I don't know," he said.

Ivo nodded.

For once he did not complain about the answer.

The radio in the kitchen began playing the new Council anthem.

Somebody in a house farther down the street turned it off.

Tomas went inside.

The road outside was smooth.

There was nowhere in the country he could drive to find the Republic of Liora.

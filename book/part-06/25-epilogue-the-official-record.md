# Epilogue — The Official Record

*Eastmere. The following winter.*

The award citation occupied one page.

It had been printed on thick paper bearing the seal of the Northern Directorate. The lettering was dark blue. Beneath the name **DR. IRENA SOLVIK** stood the phrase **EXCEPTIONAL SERVICE TO NATIONAL CONTINUITY**.

A copy had been placed in the public archive of the new Southern Administrative Territory as part of a collection documenting the political reorganization of the former Republic of Liora.

The accompanying official history described the transition as a peaceful settlement achieved after a prolonged period of internal instability. It credited Veyr's patient efforts with reducing conflict and enabling the restoration of essential services.

There were photographs.

Fuel trucks at a port.

Children receiving new textbooks.

A refurbished clinic.

The ceremonial lowering of the Lioran flag.

A smiling psychologist receiving a medal.

The archive listed these materials under the heading **Civic Stabilization**.

***

In another part of the same administrative system, Sela Orin's file contained a different history.

It began with a complaint from a municipal office and a referral under the Public Disorder Act. It described her as persistently preoccupied with a hostile external narrative concerning the institutions of her community.

The clinical record included Dr. Oren Pell's assessment that there was no psychiatric basis for involuntary detention.

It included Dr. Marek Voss's later recommendation for secure evaluation.

It included reviews that had failed to identify a threat, then continued to justify confinement because Sela would not withdraw the claims that had brought her there.

The file had grown thicker over the years.

Some pages were accurate. They recorded visits, dates, medication offered and refused, conversations with staff, and requests for books. Some pages were carelessly written. Some repeated claims that had never been substantiated. Others were designed to make an administrative decision appear inevitable by presenting its earlier consequences as reasons to continue it.

The original referral had treated her discussion of the northern program as evidence of impaired judgment.

The public archive now celebrated that program's contribution to the new political order.

Those two records were held by departments whose computers had been connected under the same continuity framework.

They were never compared.

***

At Eastmere, the radiator made a knocking sound when it warmed.

Sela had complained about it once. A maintenance worker had examined the pipe, tightened a fitting, and said the noise was probably harmless. It continued.

In the mornings she woke before the first staff round and listened to the building gather itself into the day: doors opening, shoes moving along the corridor, a trolley arriving from the kitchen.

The sounds had become familiar.

They did not mean she belonged there.

She was older now. Her hair had grown white at the temples. Lev brought her clean clothes whenever the visiting rules allowed it, and sometimes a book that had to be inspected before it could pass from one side of the reception desk to the other.

Aron Vale continued to work on her case.

His appeals had become complicated by the change of sovereignty. The old constitutional protections still existed in legal arguments, but the authority enforcing the new administrative rules did not accept the old courts' jurisdiction over every decision. A legal challenge could win a carefully reasoned judgment and still fail to open a door.

Aron had become an expert in explaining why a victory on paper did not always become a victory in life.

He never described the work as hopeless.

He had also stopped promising dates.

***

Ines arrived one morning carrying an envelope.

"From Lev," she said.

Sela took it.

The envelope contained a photograph.

It showed Rowan Way in late summer, the new flag visible at the end of the street. In the foreground was Ivo, now seventeen, standing beside a small white table. He was holding the old supper photograph in one hand.

The image was not dramatic.

There were no crowds.

There was no announcement.

It had been taken by Lev through her front window, the way people sometimes photograph the weather or a child growing too fast.

On the back she had written:

**He found the table.**

Sela sat on her bed and looked at it.

She remembered the night she bought the white table because the others would not lend theirs. She remembered the broken leg and the repair. She remembered the first summer's six tables joined into one long line, and how nobody had needed to decide whose chairs were allowed to stand beside whose.

She remembered Mrs. Kade's terrible pickled onions.

For a while she could remember the taste of them.

Then the memory slipped away.

She turned the photograph over again.

**He found the table.**

She smiled.

Ines waited beside the door.

"Would you like me to put it with your things?"

"No," Sela said. "I'll keep it."

She placed it inside the book of poems Lina had once lent her.

***

The next review was scheduled for Thursday.

Sela attended with the same care she had brought to the first hearing. She wore the green sweater and carried a short statement prepared with Aron. It was only two pages. She had learned that offering all her records at once gave administrators an excuse to describe her as preoccupied.

The statement referred to the published authorization of the Department of Civic Weather, the corroborated financial records, the absence of threatening conduct, and Oren's clinical opinion.

It also referred to the public award citation honoring Irena Solvik's work in the southern stabilization period.

Sela asked a simple question.

"If the state now officially credits this program with changing our civic life, why does my file still treat my belief that the program existed as evidence against me?"

The reviewing officer looked at the document.

"That is not the issue before the panel."

"It was the issue used to justify bringing me here."

"We're reviewing your current capacity to comply with the requirements of the service."

"I have complied with the rules here. I've attended every meeting. I haven't threatened anyone."

"Your continued insistence on this subject is part of the concern."

Sela waited.

She could feel anger moving through her body. Her heart beat faster. She wanted to shout that the contradiction was visible on the two pages in front of him, that a person did not become ill because an official refused to read a record.

She did not shout.

"Please put my question in the minutes," she said.

The officer nodded to the clerk.

The clerk wrote something down.

Later the minutes recorded that the subject had continued to challenge the legitimacy of the assessment process and maintained a fixed interpretation of historical events.

They did not contain her question.

The placement was continued.

***

After the hearing, Aron sat with Sela in the visiting room.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be."

"I should have pushed harder on the contradiction."

"You did."

"I could appeal the wording of the minutes."

"You should."

He nodded.

His briefcase rested on the floor between them. There were more papers inside it than he could hold in his hands.

"Do you ever think about stopping?" she asked.

He looked up.

"Sometimes."

"Why don't you?"

"Because the record would then end with their version."

She considered this.

"Would that be so terrible?"

He looked at her for a long time.

"Yes," he said.

It was a small answer to a large question.

Sela accepted it.

***

In Merrow, Lev arrived home after visiting hours.

She was tired. The bus had been late and the road near Eastmere was under repair again. She placed her coat on the crooked hook, beneath Sela's scarf, and stood in the hallway with her keys in her hand.

The house smelled of soap and yesterday's bread.

There was no noise from the kitchen.

She opened the cupboard and took out two plates.

She had been doing it for years.

Some evenings she put the second one away before eating. Some evenings she left it on the table. Tonight she set it opposite her own and laid a fork beside it.

Then she ate her supper.

There was no lesson in the arrangement. It was simply how she had chosen to keep the house.

Outside, Rowan Way was dark.

The new streetlights had been installed under a northern-approved infrastructure grant. They worked reliably. The road had been resurfaced. The rainwater drain had been repaired.

All these things were good.

None of them made the country sovereign.

***

In Veyr, the official history was approved for distribution to schools.

A clerk checked the citations, verified the spelling of the award recipients' names, and noted that the annex describing uncertainty in causal attribution had been omitted from the public edition.

The omission was authorized.

The clerk stamped the file.

**COMPLETE.**

At Eastmere, Sela's case remained open.

There was a copy of Oren's first assessment in the record. There were the published documents about Civic Weather. There was the official citation celebrating the department's contribution to southern stabilization. There were Sela's questions, her lawyer's objections, and the handwriting of staff who had tried to record what she actually said.

None of it opened the door.

The woman who had helped her neighbors find books, organize suppers, and remember the names of those who stopped coming was still confined.

The psychologists who had directed the northern operation had received their medals.

The republic had ceased to exist.

On Rowan Way the land remained, and the houses remained, and the people who had survived continued living among the things they could no longer put back together.

In the official record, the work was complete.

In Sela's room, the radiator knocked.

She opened her book and looked once more at the photograph of a boy beside a small white table.

Then she waited for the next visit.

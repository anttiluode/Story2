# Chapter Twelve — Official Results

*Veyr, Northern Directorate. April.*

The Directorate's conference room had acquired a new carpet.

It was thick and dark blue, the sort of carpet designed to make footsteps sound respectful. The previous carpet had been removed after a visiting minister complained that one corner curled upward and might cause an accident. Nobody could remember the last time the ministry had replaced a staircase light in a building used by ordinary citizens.

Dr. Irena Solvik stepped onto the new carpet and found that her shoes made no sound at all.

At the far end of the room, a government technician was trying to make the projection screen show the correct file. Every time he succeeded, another official asked him to adjust the font.

The report was titled:

**CIVIC RESILIENCE IN THE SOUTHERN THEATER: INTERIM ASSESSMENT**

The word *resilience* had been chosen because it sounded like something a doctor might want to restore. In the Directorate it referred to Veyr's ability to withstand the policies of Liora and its allies. The fact that Liora's own resilience was diminishing was regarded as encouraging.

Irena had been given twelve minutes to present two years of work.

Niko Rahl had spent six weeks trying to ensure that the twelve minutes contained no claim the data could not support.

His draft had been seventeen pages long.

The final version was five.

***

“Show me the participation figures,” Irena said.

Niko handed her a printout.

On the left was a line measuring membership in local civic groups. On the right was a line showing the share of respondents who said they would hesitate to work closely with someone of opposing political beliefs.

Both lines had moved in troubling directions.

“Those are genuine,” Niko said. “As far as we can determine.”

“Then why the qualification?”

“Because the data doesn't say what caused the movement.”

“I'm aware.”

“The national housing shock began during the same period. So did the factory closures. Several of the surveys use different questions than earlier versions. And the platforms changed their recommendation systems independently. We should—”

“We have seven ministers in the room at ten.”

“Then we should be more careful, not less.”

Irena took off her glasses.

There was a time when she had welcomed his insistence on accuracy. It was the quality that had made him valuable. He had grown up inside Veyr's schools, read the prescribed histories, completed the required service, and emerged with a faith in numbers that the Directorate initially found useful.

Numbers did not have loyalties, he believed.

He was still young enough to mistake that for protection.

“The report says the operation contributed to a worsening environment,” Irena said.

“No. Page three says it *produced* the observed decline.”

She opened the report.

It did.

She had approved the sentence three days earlier after Director Kest wrote in the margin: **Our contribution must be legible to the Leader.**

“Change it to ‘is consistent with our strategic objectives,’” she said.

Niko looked at her.

“That is still ambiguous, but it's better.”

“Then make the change.”

He took the page.

Later, when the report went to the copying department, the old sentence remained.

Irena did not notice until after the presentation.

By then she no longer wanted to.

***

Director Kest entered the conference room with a medal on his jacket.

It was a small silver star, awarded for service in Tarven. Kest had never served in Tarven. He had written supply authorizations during the first months of the invasion and received the decoration for ensuring continuity of operations. He was proud of it because the public could see it.

“The Leader has a personal interest,” he told Irena.

“In the methods?”

“In the results.”

“What is he expecting?”

“Assurance.”

“About what?”

“That Liora will cease interfering in the northern theater.”

Irena looked at the map on the wall.

Tarven lay between Veyr and a line of mountains, its disputed cities marked with little flags. South of the gulf was Liora. It had no army capable of reaching Veyr's capital. It hosted refugees, shipped medical supplies to Tarven, and supported sanctions that inconvenienced the northern economy.

Veyr described all three activities as aggression.

“You know we cannot guarantee a political outcome,” Irena said.

Kest smiled.

“I know you can explain why you cannot guarantee it. I am asking you not to explain that today.”

She could have refused.

She had imagined refusing in the past, usually while walking home from the ministry, when refusal was a scene she could play without consequences. She would set down the report, tell Kest what the evidence did and did not establish, and leave with her professional dignity intact.

In the actual room, with the new carpet, the ministerial seals, and the knowledge that a refusal would be recorded as disloyalty rather than caution, she gathered the papers.

“Very well,” she said.

That was all.

***

The presentation was successful.

Irena showed images of civil unrest, distrust surveys, news stories about political antagonism, and the decline of membership in organizations that once brought Liorans together. She described the northern state's efforts in broad institutional language, never specifying a particular family, teacher, school, or patient.

The men around the table watched the colored lines. They asked few questions about how the figures had been collected.

One minister wanted to know whether Liora's parliamentary coalition would survive the next national election.

“Too early to say,” Irena answered.

Another asked whether Liora could still sustain its support for Tarven.

“Support remains substantial,” she said. “But domestic agreement on its cost is weakening.”

That was true.

The minister nodded as though it confirmed his entire understanding of the country.

Kest asked a final question.

“Are southern citizens now less capable of collective action?”

Irena considered the wording.

“Some kinds of collective action are becoming more difficult,” she said.

“Would you say their ability to act as one political community has declined?”

“Yes.”

“Then that is progress.”

The word settled over the table.

The minister responsible for civil protection smiled. The deputy chief of the Directorate made a note. The room moved on to a discussion of staffing and funding, as though the human consequences were a successful engineering trial.

Irena had been treating patients before some of those ministers could vote.

She knew what fear did to the body. She knew how people sought certainty when their ordinary lives became unpredictable. She had once told a soldier home from Tarven that nightmares were not evidence of cowardice, and that an injured mind deserved the same careful attention as an injured limb.

Now she had presented the signs of an injured society as a portfolio of achievements.

Nobody applauded at the end.

They approved her budget.

***

Niko was waiting outside when she emerged.

“How did it go?”

“Well.”

“Did they ask about uncertainty?”

“No.”

“Did you raise it?”

“Where it mattered.”

He held a copy of the presentation.

“Page three still has the old sentence.”

She took it.

There it was, in black ink beneath a tidy graph: **Program activity has produced measurable declines in cross-group confidence.**

The department did have evidence of attempts to interfere, and some signs consistent with influence. It did not have the controlled comparison the sentence implied. It certainly could not separate its own activity from every domestic cause of the decline.

Niko watched her read.

“We need to correct the record.”

“The minutes will reflect the language I used.”

“You didn't use that language in the presentation?”

“No.”

“Then why does it remain in the handout?”

She looked at him.

“Because the handout was printed yesterday.”

“That's not a reason to leave it uncorrected.”

It was the first time he had spoken to her in that tone.

A passing official slowed, then continued down the corridor. Doors along the seventh floor had a habit of being open just enough for a conversation to become public without anyone appearing to listen.

Irena lowered her voice.

“Don't do this here.”

“Where would you prefer?”

“My office. At four.”

He nodded.

At four, when he arrived, she told him the distribution had been too limited to warrant a formal correction.

He said the report would be used to justify more resources.

She said that decision had already been made.

He asked whether that made the wording less important.

She told him to take the weekend off.

***

In Merrow, the *Chronicle* published a correction to an error that had appeared in another outlet's coverage of Eva Maren's investigation.

The original story had named a Common Future volunteer as an officer of Tomorrow's Neighbors. The woman was not an officer; she had attended two public meetings and helped at a charity sale. The mistake had been repeated on Lantern, followed by photographs of her house and claims about her loyalty to the country.

Eva's correction included a source and a quotation from the charity's official records.

A reader wrote to thank her.

Six others accused her of covering for the volunteer.

The woman stopped answering the door to people she did not know.

Samuel Rees called Eva into his office again.

He had lost another advertiser.

“I've had a letter from our printer,” he said. “They're raising the cost.”

“Everyone is.”

“We can print four fewer pages or charge more.”

“If we charge more, fewer people will buy it.”

“I know how prices work, Eva.”

She sat down.

Samuel had been a patient editor. Lately patience required more strength than either of them possessed.

“I'm sorry,” she said.

He rubbed his face.

“Don't be. The story was right. We did what we were supposed to do.”

“Doesn't feel like it.”

“No.”

He put the letter in a folder marked **Operations**.

Once he had been proud of the newspaper's independence. Now he spent part of every morning calculating how long it could remain independent if it continued paying for electricity, printing, lawyers, wages, and a building whose roof had begun to leak.

There were no northern officers in his office.

The costs were real.

The consequences served Veyr all the same.

***

The second week of April brought a wave of protests across Liora.

Some were peaceful. At one gathering, parents demanded accountability from the schools. At another, workers protested proposed changes to employment protection. A third was organized around rising housing costs and attracted people who disagreed about nearly everything except their rent.

Then, in the capital, police used force against a crowd after a small group of protesters broke the windows of a government building. Several people were injured. The circumstances were disputed even after an official review found that some officers had acted improperly and some demonstrators had assaulted police.

The response fractured along lines that no longer matched the evidence.

One side treated every injury as proof of an authoritarian government. Another treated every protester as a potential rioter. People who had been present gave testimony that was ignored when it complicated either story.

At the railway depot, Tomas heard a colleague say that the country needed a strong hand.

“Strong enough for what?” Tomas asked.

“To make people stop acting like animals.”

“That's not what the law is for.”

“You sound like you want the streets to burn.”

“No. I want the law to matter.”

“Then you're on their side.”

Tomas thought of Joren in the supermarket car park. The same phrase had followed him into the depot, a place where men who knew precisely how railway signals worked could no longer agree on the meaning of a sentence.

He went outside and called Dain.

The phone rang three times.

His brother answered.

“Hello?”

Tomas had not prepared what to say. For a moment he heard traffic behind Dain and the rasp of someone working with metal. It was astonishing how familiar the sound was.

“There's a fault at the old west crossing,” Tomas said.

Dain laughed once.

“You called me about a railway crossing?”

“It's near your substation. Thought you might have heard whether the power line was affected.”

“Not that I know of.”

“Right.”

“Anything else?”

Tomas could have said he missed him.

He could have asked about fishing, or the burned peppers, or whether Dain still had their father's old radio. He could have admitted that the practical question was an excuse to hear his brother's voice.

Instead he said, “No. Just checking.”

“All right.”

“Thanks.”

The call ended.

He stood beside the depot wall and stared at the minutes of the call on his screen.

Thirty-six seconds.

It was the longest they had spoken in months.

***

In Veyr, Niko went to see his sister.

She lived in a small apartment with her husband and their twelve-year-old son, Pael, who had saved his birthday money for a bicycle. The bicycle was blue and too large. Pael loved it anyway.

Its chain had slipped off.

Niko knelt on the pavement with grease on his fingers while Pael watched.

“You have to keep it under tension,” Niko said.

“Is it broken?”

“No. It's just off.”

“Why?”

“Because the wheel moved a little and the chain stopped lining up.”

He adjusted the axle and tried the pedals.

“See? It's not difficult if you can work out what changed.”

Pael spun the wheel.

“Can you fix people like that?”

Niko looked up.

“What do you mean?”

“If they get angry all the time.”

His sister stood in the doorway holding a dish towel.

“Pael.”

The boy shrugged.

“I'm just asking.”

Niko wiped his hands on a rag.

“Not like a bicycle.”

“Why not?”

“Because people have to want help. And even then, the problem isn't always in one place.”

Pael considered this, then climbed onto the bicycle and rode toward the park.

His sister waited until the boy was out of earshot.

“You look terrible,” she said.

“I've been working.”

“On what?”

“You know I can't tell you.”

“I know. I also know you haven't slept properly in weeks.”

He looked down at the grease on his hands.

“My department measures changes in what people believe.”

“And?”

“Sometimes I think we're measuring what they've stopped doing for one another.”

She said nothing.

He had told her too much, or not nearly enough. In the Directorate these were not different categories.

“Pael still talks to the boy upstairs,” she said eventually. “His father and ours don't agree about the war.”

“That's good.”

“His father says things that frighten me.”

“Do you tell Pael not to talk to the boy?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because he's a child.”

She looked toward the park, where the bicycle moved between trees.

“Because it's not his war.”

Niko found that he could not answer.

***

Irena received the promotion letter on a Tuesday.

The title was **Deputy Commissioner for Civic Resilience**, a position that would place her above two other departments and give her access to the Directorate's strategic planning committee. It came with a larger salary, an official car, an invitation to a residential compound outside the capital, and the possibility of one of the honors awarded to civilians whose work supported national security.

She read the letter twice.

Her daughter, Elin, called at lunchtime.

“Are you coming for Leni's birthday?”

Elin's daughter, Leni, was turning six.

“Of course,” Irena said. “Sunday.”

“You said that last year.”

“And I came.”

“After she went to sleep.”

Irena was silent.

Elin sighed.

“It's just that she keeps asking whether you work in the hospital anymore.”

“Tell her I work for the government.”

“I do. She wants to know if you help people.”

Irena looked at the promotion letter on her desk.

“I do research.”

“That isn't what she asked.”

The call ended politely. Irena sat without moving for several minutes.

At four, Director Kest visited her office.

“Congratulations,” he said.

“Thank you.”

“The Leader is pleased.”

“Was he shown the full report?”

“He was shown what he needed.”

“I would like the annex on uncertainty restored to the next briefing.”

Kest lowered himself into the chair opposite her.

“Doctor. You have just been offered a position that most people in this building would sacrifice years to obtain. It is not wise to greet that offer with qualifications.”

“The qualifications are part of the science.”

“Then keep them in the science.”

He rose.

“We're conducting national security policy, not a university seminar.”

After he left, Irena opened a drawer and took out Niko's first handwritten memorandum. The one about people no longer trusting members of the other political camp to perform ordinary tasks.

*The risk is not that people disagree. The risk is that every institution becomes a test of belonging.*

She had once crossed the sentence out for being too literary.

Now the Directorate had awarded her a promotion because the thing described in the sentence was happening.

She put the paper back.

Then she signed the acceptance letter.

***

On Sunday she went to Elin's house early.

Leni was still awake. She had prepared a cake with sugar stars and wanted her grandmother to help light the candles. The frosting had cracked at one corner, and the girl insisted that only Irena could repair it.

Irena dipped a spoon into warm water and smoothed the edge.

“See?” she said.

Leni examined the cake.

“Like new.”

“Almost.”

The child smiled and ran to fetch a paper crown.

For an hour Irena was not a director, not a psychologist, not a functionary of the northern state's secret war. She was a grandmother with sugar on her fingers, helping a child make a cake look whole.

Then her phone rang.

Kest wanted revisions for a meeting about the southern parliament.

Irena stepped into the hall to answer.

Leni followed and stopped behind the door. She heard her grandmother's voice become formal.

“Yes, Director.”

“No, that risk has been accounted for.”

“Yes. The measures are proceeding.”

When Irena returned, the cake had been cut. The little girl looked disappointed, though she did not say why.

“Did I miss it?” Irena asked.

Elin handed her a plate.

“Only the candles.”

Irena sat down.

The family continued around her. Someone told a joke. Someone spilled a drink. Leni got icing on her sleeve. Nobody in the room mentioned Liora, or Tarven, or the people who were losing their ability to share a street.

At the Ministry of Continuity, her signed acceptance had already been filed.

On the bottom of the document, in a box marked **Basis for Appointment**, somebody had typed:

**Demonstrated strategic effect on southern civic cohesion.**

She had been promoted for making something disappear.

There was no space on the form to record what it had been.

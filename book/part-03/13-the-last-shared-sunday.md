# Chapter Thirteen — The Last Shared Sunday

*Merrow. Early May.*

Mrs. Kade died on a Thursday morning.

The cause was not political.

She was ninety-one years old. Her hip had never healed properly after the fall, her lungs had weakened through the winter, and her body had finally grown tired of keeping the complicated machinery of life in motion. She died in the care home with a nurse holding her hand, while the light through the window moved slowly across a photograph of a school she had once taught.

There was no conspiracy in it.

This mattered to Sela Orin, who had begun to see patterns everywhere and was frightened of the temptation to turn every sorrow into evidence of the same crime.

Mrs. Kade had lived long. She had been loved. She had also been difficult, stubborn, nosy, occasionally unkind, and extravagantly wrong about the proper temperature at which to serve tea.

She had taught children to read by making them laugh at words they were frightened of.

She was a person, and now she was dead.

Sela heard the news from Mrs. Kade's nephew, who called to ask whether the neighborhood would help arrange a memorial.

“Of course,” she said.

He sounded relieved.

“Auntie always talked about the table in the street.”

“We'll do it,” Sela said.

She did not yet know whether that promise was one she could keep.

***

Lev was home when Sela returned from the library.

Sela found her sitting at the kitchen table with a plate of toast she had forgotten to eat.

“Mrs. Kade died,” Sela said.

Lev closed her eyes.

“Oh, Sela.”

For a few minutes they sat in silence. They had both known the old woman for years. She had called Lev a troublemaker when Lev and Sela married, then arrived the following week with a cake and told them that nobody had asked her opinion about the marriage, which was a pity because she had many.

“What are we doing?” Lev asked.

“A service at the community hall. Her nephew wants something simple.”

“Good.”

“He asked us to help.”

Lev looked at Sela.

“Are you going to make a list?”

Sela almost smiled.

“Yes.”

“Of people to invite, I mean.”

“Yes.”

“Then I'm helping.”

It was such an ordinary offer that Sela wanted to cry.

She took out paper and began writing names. She used the old photograph from Rowan Way as a guide: the Kellers, the Ardens, Dain, Fen, the man from number eight, Joren Fenn because Mrs. Kade had once taught his daughter, the mayor because he had spent years being scolded by her in public.

Lev brought a second sheet.

“Food,” she said.

“You don't have to organize food.”

“It's a memorial. We're going to feed people.”

Sela nodded.

Together they made the lists.

It felt like a rehearsal of a skill they were forgetting.

***

The first disagreement came over the venue.

The community hall was municipal property. A local Renewal representative objected to the mayor appearing at a public event there because the mayor had recently endorsed a Common Future candidate. A Common Future organizer said the memorial should not be used to rehabilitate the mayor's record on social services.

Neither objection had come from anyone in Mrs. Kade's family.

Sela wrote a short message.

**This is a memorial for a woman who taught generations of this town to read. The mayor is attending as a former pupil, and there will be no political speeches.**

Someone replied that education was political.

Sela wrote:

**So is the price of bread. We're still going to serve sandwiches.**

The reply was shared widely. People praised her for standing up to extremism. Others accused her of mocking serious concerns. Sela wanted to say that she had not stood up to anything. She had asked people to stop using a funeral as a place to settle their arguments.

She put the phone down and called the hall caretaker.

“Is it available on Sunday?”

“Yes.”

“Can we have the tables?”

“Of course.”

The two words were so simple that for a moment she felt as though the town still existed in the old way.

***

At the Arden house, Mara told Ivo that Mrs. Kade had died.

He was sitting at his desk with a circuit board. He looked up.

“The lady who shouted at Fen's music?”

“Yes.”

“Who made the weird onions?”

“Yes.”

He considered this.

“Did she die because of the fall?”

“Not exactly. She was very old.”

“Was she scared?”

Mara hesitated.

“I don't know.”

“Did she know she was dying?”

“I think so.”

Ivo set the board aside. There were questions he no longer asked his parents because the answers led to arguments. Death was not one of them. Adults could disagree about the meaning of politics. They could not disagree that Mrs. Kade would never open her door again.

“Can I go to the memorial?” he asked.

“Of course.”

“Is Uncle Dain going?”

“I expect so.”

“Can Dad be there when he is?”

Mara looked toward the back room where Tomas had been sleeping.

“I think he can.”

“You always say you think.”

She had not known he was keeping count.

She went to find Tomas.

He was sitting on the bed, fixing a broken latch on a toolbox. The mattress was too narrow for his shoulders. She had meant to ask him to buy a proper one, then had never been able to decide whether that would be an act of kindness or an admission that he would be staying there.

“Mrs. Kade's service is Sunday,” she said.

“I know.”

“Ivo wants us to go together.”

“Of course.”

“Dain will probably be there.”

Tomas kept working.

“I assumed he would.”

“Can you two be in the same room?”

He looked up.

“I'm not going to start a fight at a funeral.”

“I didn't say you would.”

“You asked.”

She sat on the edge of the bed.

“I don't know what to say to you anymore.”

He rested the latch on his knee.

“Neither do I.”

For a moment neither moved.

The back room was smaller than their old bedroom, and the distance between them could have been crossed with one step.

She almost reached out.

Instead she asked whether the toolbox was fixed.

“Nearly.”

She stood and left.

***

Dain received the invitation while he was under a power transformer.

He read Sela's message at lunch and remembered a schoolroom that smelled of chalk dust and wet wool. Mrs. Kade had taught him to read when he was six. Tomas had been one class ahead. Their mother used to collect them together on Fridays, and sometimes the teacher would send them home with a book she thought too difficult for them.

When Dain was eight, he had refused to read aloud because another boy laughed at him. Mrs. Kade kept him after class and gave him a book about machines. She made him read the first page in a whisper, then the next page more loudly, until he realized that the words did not care whether anyone laughed.

He still had the book somewhere.

He went home after work and searched through a box of old things.

There were photographs of their mother, a broken wristwatch, a certificate from a school sports day, and a postcard Tomas had sent from a railway training course. At the bottom lay the book.

Its cover was bent. On the first page Mrs. Kade had written:

**To Dain. A difficult word is only a word you haven't learned yet.**

He sat on the floor and read the inscription twice.

Then he called Tomas.

The line rang. He had almost forgotten what it felt like to be the one making the call.

Tomas answered.

“Hello?”

“It's me.”

“I know.”

“Found something of Mrs. Kade's. Thought you might remember.”

Dain told him about the book.

Tomas laughed softly.

“She made me read a book about trains. Said it was the only thing I'd pay attention to.”

“Seems she was right.”

“Usually was.”

There was a silence.

“See you Sunday?” Dain asked.

“Yes.”

“All right.”

“Dain—”

“Yeah?”

Tomas looked at the photograph on the kitchen mantelpiece. He had been meaning to say something to his brother for so long that the words had grown heavy and unfamiliar.

“Bring the book,” he said.

“Of course.”

The call ended.

Dain remained seated on the floor.

He had heard his brother's voice without anger. It seemed like a miracle and, because it was so small, it also seemed ridiculous to call it one.

***

Sunday was bright and cold.

Sela and Lev arrived at the hall before ten. The caretaker had already put out twenty chairs. Sela asked for forty. Lev set out tea, bread, and a large pan of soup. Fen came with a small speaker, but left it switched off until someone asked for music.

The nephew arrived carrying a photograph of Mrs. Kade in her first teaching job.

She was young, unsmiling, and standing beside a blackboard. The woman in the photograph looked as though she had been born ready to tell somebody to stop slouching.

Sela put it beside a vase of flowers.

People began coming at eleven.

The Kellers arrived together. Ada was carrying a tray of sandwiches; Jonas looked nervous, but he shook hands with Lev and helped arrange chairs. The mayor came without his official badge. Two retired teachers brought stories written on paper. The nurse from the care home arrived with a small bunch of yellow flowers.

Joren Fenn appeared in an old dark jacket, accompanied by Mira and Tessa.

He had not brought a camera crew.

That was the first thing Eva Maren noticed.

She had come partly because Mrs. Kade had taught her mother, and partly because Sela had asked her to help serve tea. Eva saw Joren near the entrance and assumed a producer would appear behind him.

There was no producer.

Joren went to the nephew and spoke quietly. Then he sat at the back with his family.

Mira looked tired, but she was holding his hand.

A little later Dain came in with the old book.

Tomas stood near the front with Mara and Ivo.

The brothers saw one another across the room.

Nobody moved for several seconds.

Then Tomas raised a hand.

Dain raised his.

It was not reconciliation. It was only recognition.

For the first time in months it happened in front of people who had known them before politics had become a test of loyalty.

***

The service began with a reading by one of Mrs. Kade's former pupils.

He was a large man with a trembling voice. He told a story about being unable to read a sentence when he was seven, and the teacher who had waited until he could. He said the country owed more to patient women with chalk on their sleeves than to any minister who had ever stood at a podium.

People laughed.

The mayor spoke briefly, ignoring the prepared notes in his hand.

“She once told me,” he said, “that I should stop promising to fix the pothole and get somebody to fix it.”

More laughter.

“She's still right,” someone called.

The mayor nodded.

“Yes. She is.”

The room warmed.

A former student read a passage from a book Mrs. Kade had loved. It was about a traveler who lost his way and discovered that every road home was made by people he had once helped. Nobody argued about what the passage meant. People simply listened.

Then Dain came forward with the book.

He had not planned to speak. When he saw the photograph, however, and the names of all the people who had learned to read under the same stern gaze, he understood that bringing the book and saying nothing would leave something unfinished.

He opened it to the first page.

He read the inscription aloud.

**A difficult word is only a word you haven't learned yet.**

Then he looked at Tomas.

“My brother learned to read in her class too. He was always ahead of me. I hated that.”

Tomas smiled.

“Still do,” Dain said.

There was laughter.

“She never let me use him as an excuse. If he could do something, she told me, that was proof a person could learn it, not proof I was stupid.”

He closed the book.

“I owe her more than I can say.”

He returned to his seat.

For a moment the room was quiet in a way that belonged to grief rather than fear. People looked at the photograph and remembered a woman who had known them before they had decided who they were allowed to admire.

Ivo leaned toward Mara.

“Dad's crying,” he whispered.

Mara looked at Tomas. There were tears in his eyes.

“It's all right,” she said.

“I know.”

He took her hand for a moment.

***

After the service, people gathered at the long tables.

There were only four of them, and two were borrowed from the community hall. Sela had arranged the chairs so that nobody would have to sit next to someone they feared speaking to. She hated herself a little for doing it.

But people moved the chairs anyway.

The old teacher's funeral had brought together colleagues, former pupils, neighbors, people who had voted for competing movements, people who had stopped talking, and people who wanted only to eat a sandwich and remember somebody.

For perhaps twenty minutes, it seemed possible that grief would restore a common language.

Joren approached Lev with a cup of tea.

“I wanted to say I'm sorry about what happened outside the clinic,” he said.

Lev looked at him.

“Which part?”

“The interview. The people coming there afterward.”

“You didn't tell them to come.”

“No.”

“But you knew what they were saying.”

He lowered his eyes.

“Yes.”

“Some of my staff are afraid to work there.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He flinched.

She was not trying to humiliate him. She wanted him to understand that apology and consequence lived in different timeframes.

“I can't take back everything that happened,” he said.

“No.”

“Can I do anything now?”

Lev thought of the young nurse who had transferred to another district, the patients who delayed care, the staff who had learned to look at every stranger as a potential threat.

“You could tell your audience that a clinic is a place where people deserve help, regardless of what they believe,” she said.

He nodded.

Then he said, very quietly, “They won't like that.”

Lev looked at him for a long time.

“That's why it would matter.”

He returned to his table without promising anything.

***

Eva and Sela stood beside the tea urn.

“There's another document,” Eva said.

“What kind?”

“A payment record. The legal review confirmed it's authentic.”

“And?”

“It doesn't show Veyr. It shows Harborbridge had an undeclared role in another political event.”

“Do you think it connects them?”

“It connects money. That's enough for another story if we can establish the reporting obligations.”

Sela looked around the room.

“Does it matter?”

Eva set down her cup.

“Of course it matters.”

“I know. I mean—look at them. Mrs. Kade dies, and we can sit together for one afternoon. Tomorrow they'll go back to calling one another enemies.”

“Perhaps. But we can't stop asking what's true.”

Sela nodded.

They had been having versions of this conversation for months, and neither was sure whether the other understood the part that frightened her most.

Eva feared a country where evidence could not be shared.

Sela feared a country where evidence could be shared and nothing changed.

***

Tomas found Dain outside.

His brother was standing beside the hall's back door, holding a paper cup of tea. Beyond the car park, a line of trees showed the first pale green of spring.

“Good speech,” Tomas said.

“Thanks.”

“She'd have told you to speak up.”

“She'd have told me to stop slouching.”

They laughed.

For a while they talked about Mrs. Kade. They remembered her classroom, the smell of the old radiators, the day Tomas had been punished for bringing a small frog into a reading lesson. Dain had forgotten that story. Tomas remembered every detail.

Dain told him about the postcard he had found from the railway training course.

“I was proud of you,” he said.

Tomas looked at him.

“I know.”

“I don't think you do.”

The conversation changed, almost imperceptibly.

Tomas held his cup between both hands.

“You could have come around any time.”

“So could you.”

“I called.”

“About a crossing.”

“I wanted to hear from you.”

Dain was silent.

“Then why didn't you just say that?” he asked.

Tomas wanted to explain that the months of silence had become a thing with weight, that apologizing first felt like agreeing with everything his brother had said about public workers, the union, the country. It was a stupid feeling. He knew it was stupid. He could not quite stop having it.

“Because I was angry,” Tomas said.

Dain nodded.

“Me too.”

There it was.

Not forgiveness. Not agreement. But two men admitting, without an audience, that anger had been a choice they had kept making.

Dain looked down at his cup.

“I said things about your work. I shouldn't have.”

Tomas felt the relief arrive before he could defend against it.

“I said things about yours.”

They stood there, two brothers at the edge of an ordinary car park, close enough to touch.

Dain said, “Do you want to come fishing next weekend? Ivo's been asking.”

Tomas opened his mouth.

A voice called from the hall.

“Dain? There's someone who wants to meet you.”

It was Jonas Keller, standing with a local Renewal council candidate who had recently appeared on the Honest Hour. The candidate had recognized Dain from an old video about power outages. He wanted to ask whether Dain would speak at a meeting about public utilities.

Dain looked back at Tomas.

“Give me a minute,” he said.

Tomas felt the old instinct rise.

“Of course. Politics first.”

Dain's face tightened.

“That's not fair.”

“Isn't it?”

“I was just talking to you.”

“And now you're not.”

The words were out before Tomas understood he had said them.

Dain stared at him.

“I asked you to come fishing.”

“I know.”

“Then answer me.”

Tomas could have said yes. His brother was waiting for a simple answer. It was still possible.

Instead Tomas looked toward the candidate by the door.

“You should go. Your people need you.”

Dain lowered his cup.

“You're unbelievable.”

He walked away.

Tomas remained beneath the trees while the relief he had felt only minutes before turned back into familiar anger.

He wanted to chase after Dain. He imagined calling him by the childhood nickname he had not used in years. He imagined saying, *Don't go. I want to fish with you and the boy. I want to talk about something other than the country. I want my brother back.*

He did none of those things.

When he returned to the hall, Dain had already left.

***

At four, the tables were being cleared.

The mayor helped stack chairs. Joren carried rubbish bags outside. The Kellers washed dishes together. Fen turned on the speaker very softly and played an old song that most of the room knew.

For a few minutes nobody checked a phone.

Then a young man near the door received a notification about a confrontation in the capital. He showed it to a friend. Someone else said the clip was misleading. Another person recognized a banner and began an argument about who had thrown the first stone.

The words were quieter than they would have been in another room, but they were there, waiting beneath the surface.

Sela asked whether they could leave the argument until tomorrow.

Several people looked ashamed.

They did.

It was not much of a victory. It was enough for the remainder of a funeral.

***

That night Ivo stood in the hallway holding Mrs. Kade's memorial programme.

It showed the photograph from her first teaching job. Beneath it was a sentence her nephew had chosen:

**She taught us that a person could learn what they did not yet know.**

Ivo took it to the kitchen.

Tomas was sitting at the table, looking at his phone. Mara stood by the sink.

“Will we have the supper in the street this summer?” Ivo asked.

Mara glanced at Tomas.

“We can try,” she said.

“Do you think everybody will come?”

Neither parent answered.

Ivo looked at them, then at the photograph on the mantelpiece. He had grown used to adults making promises that meant something else.

“Why don't we just ask them?” he said.

Tomas looked up.

Mara wiped her hands.

It was a perfectly reasonable idea. They could knock on doors, borrow tables, make food, and let the arguments exist without allowing them to decide who deserved a chair.

They had done it before.

They would have to choose to do it again.

“Maybe,” Tomas said.

Ivo nodded and went upstairs.

In his room he placed the memorial programme beside the old photograph of the first supper. He looked at the faces and tried to remember the names of everyone who had been there.

Mrs. Kade was gone. Some families had moved away. Others remained on Rowan Way but might as well have moved to another country. His uncle lived only a short drive away, yet had become someone he was expected to see by arrangement.

He had never been told that the streets of a country could move while the buildings remained in place.

***

In the northern capital, Director Kest received a summary of developments in Liora.

The report mentioned growing antagonism around the forthcoming election, several unresolved financing inquiries, and an increasing tendency for local institutions to be interpreted through national political loyalties.

There was no reference to the funeral in Merrow. No one in Civic Weather knew that Mrs. Kade had died, or that two brothers had almost forgiven one another beside a car park.

The department's view remained broad, statistical, sometimes accurate, sometimes distorted by the desire to see what its superiors wished to see.

Kest marked the report **SATISFACTORY**.

Then he signed a requisition increasing the capacity of the southern operations liaison office.

Elsewhere in Veyr, an army officer asked for additional supplies for the fighting in Tarven and was told his request would have to wait.

Budgets expressed priorities more honestly than speeches.

***

On Rowan Way the last chairs were stored away.

Sela put the borrowed crockery in the kitchen. Lev rinsed a pot. They moved quietly, the way people do after a long day containing too much emotion.

“Did you see Tomas and Dain talking?” Lev asked.

“Yes.”

“Maybe that's something.”

“Maybe.”

Sela looked at the empty table.

She had wanted the funeral to prove that the old neighborhood was still there beneath the anger. For most of the day it had seemed true. People had worked together. They had mourned a woman they all remembered. They had set aside arguments for the sake of someone who could no longer ask them to.

The knowledge should have made her hopeful.

Instead it made her afraid.

They had not forgotten how to be kind.

They had not lost the ability.

They were beginning to require an exceptional reason to use it.

Sela turned off the kitchen light.

Outside, the houses stood where they always had. A curtain moved in one window. A car started at the corner. Somewhere a child laughed, and somewhere else a couple argued behind a closed door.

The last Sunday on which so many of the old neighbors would gather beneath one roof had ended.

No one knew it was the last.

There was no announcement, no decree, no formal closing of the street.

There was only a room that would not be booked again, and the memory of a woman who had once told a frightened child that a difficult word was only a word he had not yet learned.

Some of the words Liora needed had become very difficult.

People were beginning to prefer silence.

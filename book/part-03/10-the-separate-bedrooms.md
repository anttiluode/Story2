# Chapter Ten — The Separate Bedrooms

*Rowan Way, Merrow. January. The winter after the second supper.*

The room had been yellow when they moved in.

Not an attractive yellow. The previous owner had painted the walls a color that reminded Mara of butter left too long in the sun, and Tomas had promised they would repaint before bringing any furniture upstairs.

Seventeen years later, the yellow remained behind the wardrobe.

Most marriages contained a little geography like that. Places where intentions had hardened into furniture, where something meant to be temporary had stayed long enough to become part of the house.

In January, Mara found a damp patch on the wall behind the bed.

She discovered it while looking for a charging cable that had slipped between the mattress and the plaster. The mark was the size of her palm, dark at the edges. She touched it. Her finger came away cold.

“Tomas,” she called.

He was downstairs repairing a broken switch on the kitchen extractor, an ordinary job that had become an elaborate one because the replacement part was the wrong shape. He called back that he'd be up in a minute.

She waited four minutes. Then she photographed the patch and posted the picture in the neighborhood group, asking whether anyone had used a good roofer recently.

Seven people replied before Tomas arrived.

One suggested a company. Two told her landlords and governments had made decent housing impossible. A woman offered a useful explanation about condensation. Someone she didn't know wrote that the house looked neglected and her husband ought to be ashamed.

Mara deleted that reply.

Tomas came upstairs carrying a screwdriver.

“Where is it?”

She showed him. He ran a hand over the plaster and looked toward the window.

“Could be the gutter. I'll look when it's light.”

“I've already asked for recommendations.”

“Good. We may need somebody.”

He saw the phone in her hand.

“Did you post the bedroom?”

“Only the wall.”

“It's our bedroom.”

“It's a photograph of damp.”

“I know.”

“Then what's the problem?”

He was tired of saying that nothing was a problem when he thought something was a problem. It was a small privacy he had wanted, the freedom to discover a leak in his house without an audience. There were other freedoms she wanted. Both were reasonable, and the conversation had already begun to abandon reason.

“Forget it,” he said.

Mara looked at him as though he had closed a door in her face.

“That's what you always say.”

He left the screwdriver on the windowsill and went back downstairs.

There had been a time when they knew how to leave a quarrel unfinished without making it worse. They could make tea, go to bed, wake up, and discover that neither cared who had been right. Now every unfinished quarrel returned the next day with witnesses neither spouse had invited.

***

A month earlier the *Merrow Chronicle* had finally published Eva Maren's investigation into the Public Voice Foundation and Tomorrow's Neighbors.

The story was painstaking. It identified documented payments associated with organizations on opposing political sides, inconsistencies in public accounts, and the shared involvement of Harborbridge Civic Services. It distinguished what was established from what was still unknown. It did not accuse any named person of treason. It did not claim that foreign interference had been proved.

The article appeared on page three of the printed paper and on the website beneath a headline Samuel Rees had argued over for an hour:

**Questions Over Political Funding in Merrow**

Almost nobody read it that way.

The first national network to mention the investigation described it as evidence of corruption in Common Future. A Renewal commentator posted a screenshot with the lines about one grant highlighted. Common Future supporters responded by circulating the passages about Joren Fenn's programme, declaring that the Honest Hour had been bought and paid for.

The underlying article included both.

By evening, Eva had received hundreds of messages accusing her of protecting whichever organization the sender opposed. People who had read only a highlighted paragraph demanded that she explain why the other paragraph had been omitted.

Samuel called her into his office at ten the following morning.

“We've had three advertisers pause their contracts,” he said.

“Because the story's wrong?”

“Because they're frightened.”

“Do we correct anything?”

“Not unless something's wrong.”

He rubbed his eyes.

“There's a call from the Public Voice lawyers at noon. Then a call from Tomorrow's Neighbors at two. I'm expecting to hear from the king of the moon before supper.”

Liora had no king.

Eva laughed, and was surprised by how much she needed to.

“Maybe we should publish the documents separately,” she said.

“They're already linked.”

“I mean without any commentary. Just the records.”

“Then people will invent commentary.”

“They already have.”

Samuel looked at her with an expression she could not read. There was pride in it, perhaps, and grief for the idea that a newspaper could establish the common facts of a disagreement.

“You did your job,” he said.

“Did I?”

“You got it right.”

The sentence ought to have been enough. It was not.

***

Mara had shared the article twice.

The first time she wrote that both movements should answer questions about their funding. Alia Venn called to say she agreed completely, then suggested that Mara's wording was being used by dishonest people to imply Common Future was equivalent to Renewal.

“They're not equivalent,” Alia said. “You know that.”

“I said both organizations should explain their accounts.”

“That's perfectly fair in principle. But you have influence now. A sentence can be taken out of context.”

Mara was proud of the influence. She hadn't asked for it, exactly, but she had earned it through weeks of work that no one in the municipality had volunteered to do.

She revised the post.

The second time she wrote that the Honest Hour owed its audience an explanation and that accusations against Common Future were being used as a distraction.

Tomas read the revision while waiting for a train.

He sent her a message.

**Why did you change it? The first version was right.**

She wrote back: **I'm tired of people using my words against the schools.**

He answered: **The funding issue doesn't disappear because you don't like who repeats it.**

She read the message as an accusation that she had chosen dishonest friends. He meant it as a warning that she was abandoning a standard she had insisted upon when the council withheld money from her football club.

Both understood half the conversation. Each was more certain of the other half.

At home they said almost nothing about it. They ate supper with Ivo and asked about school. Tomas repaired the kitchen switch. Mara finished a report for the club.

At eleven, in bed, Tomas reached for her and she moved away without thinking.

“Sorry,” she said.

He lay on his back.

“Are you angry?”

“I'm tired.”

“Of me?”

She could have said no. She was tired of a hundred things: work, the football club, being recognized by strangers while failing to accomplish anything practical, the state of the country, an unpaid electricity bill, the damp patch upstairs, and the strange way she and Tomas now spoke as if defending positions before a committee.

“I don't know,” she said.

The answer seemed worse than yes.

He got out of bed.

“Where are you going?”

“Downstairs.”

“Tomas.”

“I can't sleep.”

He took a blanket and went to the sofa. Mara watched the empty doorway for a long time.

Then she opened her phone and began reading.

***

At the clinic, Lev had stopped opening Lantern.

The receptionist now kept a list of names associated with threatening or disruptive incidents. It was not a political list. It recorded behavior: shouting at staff, filming other patients without permission, refusing to leave after a consultation, trying to obtain private information.

The problem was that every incident became political afterward.

One patient complained that the clinic treated her unfairly because she supported Renewal. Another insisted he had been denied an appointment because he criticized Renewal. Lev reviewed both cases. The first patient had arrived forty minutes late; the second had been given the wrong date by a scheduling program. Both had legitimate reasons to be upset about aspects of their treatment. Neither case supported the political explanation being circulated online.

She drafted a statement about the errors. Her supervisor asked her to remove details that might expose the clinic to litigation. The legal office asked her to avoid words that could be interpreted as admitting systemic failure. By the time the statement was approved, it said almost nothing.

At home she told Sela.

“Maybe Eva could write about it,” Sela said.

Lev was washing a cup. She set it down carefully.

“I told you what happened because I wanted you to know what my day was like.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Sela looked up.

Lev wiped her hands.

“I don't want to be another source in your notebook.”

It was almost exactly what she had said months earlier. Sela felt the shame of recognizing a lesson she had failed to learn.

She pushed the notebook away.

“Tell me about the patient.”

Lev sat down. She told Sela about an elderly man who had brought a bag of pears because the staff had arranged transport for his wife. She described the young nurse who had been frightened after her photograph was circulated and had now begun working in another district. She mentioned a woman who came to the clinic simply because it was warm.

Sela listened. The stories were not evidence for a theory. They were Lev's life, which had become harder.

Before bed, Sela pulled a chair close to Lev's and leaned against her shoulder.

For an hour neither mentioned Liora.

***

Tomas spent six nights on the sofa.

Ivo counted them without intending to. He was old enough to understand that adults sometimes slept separately and too young to understand why the location of a pillow could feel like a verdict on his entire future.

On the seventh morning he came downstairs and found his father making coffee.

“Is it because of Uncle Dain?”

“No.”

“Is it because of Renewal?”

“No.”

“Is it because of me?”

Tomas turned so quickly that the spoon struck the side of the mug.

“No. Absolutely not.”

“Then why?”

He considered telling Ivo that marriage was complicated. That was true. It was also what grown-ups said when they didn't want to explain a thing they barely understood.

“Your mum and I are having trouble talking to each other.”

“You talk all the time.”

“That's the trouble.”

Ivo looked at the sofa, where the blanket was folded neatly and Tomas's pillow rested against the arm.

“Do you still love her?”

“Yes.”

“Does she love you?”

Tomas paused half a second too long.

“I think so.”

Ivo picked up his school bag and left without finishing breakfast.

The phrase followed him all day. *I think so.*

At school he was asked to write a short essay about the place he felt safest.

He wrote about the old fishing spot by the river.

He did not mention his house.

***

Mara asked Tomas to come upstairs on Friday.

There were no speeches. She had been thinking about the absurdity of keeping two people apart in a house they could barely afford to heat. She was lonely and ashamed of being lonely. She missed the smell of the oil on his railway jacket and the sound of his breathing when he fell asleep.

She had washed the sheets.

“It's freezing downstairs,” she said.

He looked at her, trying to understand whether this was an invitation or a complaint about the heating bill.

“I know.”

“You can sleep up here.”

He carried his pillow upstairs.

They lay beside each other without touching. A familiar ceiling looked strange in the silence. Tomas wanted to ask if she had missed him. Mara wanted him to reach for her, but feared that if he did she would move away again and destroy something neither could name.

After twenty minutes she said, “Do you remember the yellow paint?”

“What yellow paint?”

“Behind the wardrobe.”

He laughed softly.

“I promised to repaint it.”

“You did.”

“I even bought the paint.”

“Where is it?”

“Garage, probably. Hardened into a brick.”

She turned toward him.

“We were so certain about everything.”

“No, we weren't.”

“We were certain we wanted to be here.”

“That part I remember.”

For a moment the wall between them was only a damp patch and an old wardrobe. Mara reached across and found his hand.

Then his phone vibrated on the bedside table.

A message from a railway colleague: the transport minister had blamed union obstruction for delays after a serious signaling fault. The colleague had attached a clip of a Common Future spokesperson saying the time for compromise with Renewal was over.

Tomas watched it without sound.

Mara recognized the spokesperson. She had met her twice.

“That's not what she was talking about,” Mara said.

“How do you know?”

“Because I heard the whole speech.”

“Then you know what she said before the clip.”

“Yes.”

“Was it better?”

Mara withdrew her hand.

“We just had five minutes where we didn't do this.”

“I'm asking you a question.”

“It always starts with a question.”

He put the phone away.

She turned onto her side.

They had not chosen to stop loving each other. They had chosen, again and again, not to risk being misunderstood one more time. The choices accumulated until love had no easy way to cross the room.

At two in the morning, Tomas took his pillow and returned to the sofa.

When Mara woke, his side of the bed was cold. She told herself she had expected it.

***

The separate bedrooms became a household arrangement before anybody acknowledged it as one.

Tomas bought a cheap mattress for the small room at the back of the house. It had once been an office. Later it had held boxes of Ivo's baby clothes and a broken exercise bicycle. He moved the boxes into the garage and repaired the door that had never quite closed.

Mara helped him carry the mattress upstairs.

It was such an ordinary task that they almost forgot what it meant.

“Mind the banister,” she said.

“I've got it.”

“You'll scrape the wall.”

“Then we can paint it.”

They both laughed.

Afterward she stood in the doorway, surveying the room. A bed, a small lamp, a narrow window overlooking the hedge, and Tomas's old books on signaling systems.

“Do you want a blanket?” she asked.

“I'll bring one.”

“It's colder in here.”

“I know.”

Neither said the word *separation*. That was a word for people with lawyers and decisions and new addresses. They had a mortgage. Their son needed a home. Tomas had early shifts. Mara often worked late. Separate rooms made practical sense.

They told themselves so for weeks.

Ivo learned which door was his father's and which was his mother's. He stopped asking whether Tomas would come upstairs. On a family form from school, he checked the box labeled **Parents living together**.

It was true.

For some reason he felt as though he had lied.

***

One evening Mara found an old photograph in the drawer of the bedside table.

They were standing outside the house on the day they collected the keys. Tomas's hair was longer. Mara was wearing a red coat she had loved and later given away. Behind them the windows were open, although there had been no furniture and no curtains.

She took the photograph downstairs.

Tomas was asleep on the new mattress, still wearing his work clothes. The book he had been reading lay open against his chest.

Mara considered waking him. She could sit beside him, show him the photograph, ask whether they could begin again. There was no rule against such a thing. No one was keeping a score of who apologized first.

His phone lit up on the bedside table. A notification showed a news headline blaming Common Future for a railway incident she knew nothing about. She knew what conversation would follow if she asked.

She set the photograph beside his lamp, face down, and closed the door.

In the morning Tomas found it. He held it for a while, looking at the younger versions of themselves before the furniture, the children, and the words they would later use to wound each other.

He carried it into the kitchen intending to ask why Mara had left it.

She was already on a call, discussing a campaign meeting with Alia.

He placed the photograph by the bread bin. He had an early shift.

That night Mara moved it to the mantelpiece. Tomas noticed, but neither mentioned it.

They had become so careful not to start an argument that they no longer knew how to start a conversation.

At the end of February, a survey asked Liorans whether they believed their political opponents could be trusted around children, with money, or in positions of public responsibility. The results were alarming. People reported greater fear and less confidence in one another.

The report made national news.

At the Arden house, neither spouse read it aloud.

It had become possible to live inside the same building and belong to separate countries.

Neither of them had yet left home.

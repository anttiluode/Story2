# Chapter Eleven — Children of the Borderless War

*Merrow. March.*

The children understood the rules before the adults admitted there were rules.

They learned them by watching where their parents sat at football matches, which houses were no longer visited, and the sudden way conversation stopped when they came into a room. They learned to distinguish an ordinary silence from a dangerous one. They learned that some names could be said in one kitchen but not another, and that a joke told at school might be brought home as evidence of something unforgivable.

Nobody taught a class on any of this.

At Merrow District School, the children taught one another.

Ivo Arden had known Tessa Fenn since they were five. She had once bitten him for taking her blue crayon, and he had responded by putting all her yellow crayons under the radiator. Their mothers had apologized, the children had forgiven each other before lunch, and years later the story was still told whenever either of them became too certain that the other was unreasonable.

They were not best friends in the way young children understood friendship. By twelve they had separate circles, different interests, and an emerging embarrassment about being seen together. Tessa played goalkeeper and could tell whether a bicycle chain needed oil by listening to it. Ivo liked making things move when they weren't supposed to move: toy cars built from discarded motors, a model drawbridge operated by string, a small fan he had made from a hairdryer motor that sounded like a furious wasp.

But when a teacher assigned a science project requiring two people to build a moving model, they chose each other without discussion.

“Railway crossing,” Ivo said.

“Boring.”

“Drawbridge?”

“Better. Who gets to build it?”

“Both.”

“Who gets to say whether it works?”

“Physics.”

Tessa grinned.

They built the bridge out of thin wooden strips in the school workshop. Ivo handled the wiring, Tessa built the supports. The bridge opened beautifully when nobody touched it and collapsed when the teacher pressed the switch.

“Needs a stronger hinge,” Tessa said.

“Needs less teacher,” Ivo replied.

They laughed so hard the teacher sent them outside.

It was the last time that year either would remember laughing without wondering who might misunderstand it.

***

The school organized a Civic Day in the spring.

The idea was older than the current conflict. Each year students researched a public question, presented different points of view, and practiced disagreeing without insulting one another. Previous subjects had included transport, river pollution, school lunches, and whether the town should build a new swimming pool.

This year the subject was **What Does a Country Owe Its Citizens?**

The teachers had chosen it because the national debate had become unavoidable. They hoped that giving students a quieter room and better rules might restore the habit of listening.

Parents reacted as though the school had announced a plan to train soldiers.

A Renewal parents' group accused the teachers of encouraging children to accept state control. A Common Future group accused the school of creating false equivalence between public service and deliberate cruelty. Several parents who belonged to neither group asked for the event to be canceled because they did not want their children placed between angry adults.

The school head, Ms. Pellin, refused to cancel it.

She had been teaching for twenty-six years and possessed a voice that could bring two hundred children to silence without rising above conversation. She had never been frightened of a difficult meeting. She was becoming frightened of the things meetings no longer seemed able to accomplish.

“Children need to learn to argue better than we do,” she told the parents.

Some cheered. Others accused her of insulting families.

The school asked students to present pairs of competing proposals and explain the strengths and weaknesses of each. Every claim had to be supported by a source. No student was asked to disclose a parent's voting preference.

Ivo and Tessa chose the financing of public transport.

That ought to have been safe.

They knew the subject. Ivo's father worked on railway signals. Tessa's father had spent months complaining about roadworks and the cost of keeping a business open. There were genuine disagreements, but they were the kind two children could investigate.

They collected published budgets, asked a station manager about safety inspections, and spoke with a bus driver whose route was being reduced. Tessa interviewed Joren about the cost of delivering parts when traffic was diverted. Ivo asked Tomas to explain why a cheaper signaling contract could become expensive if inspections were missed.

Each parent gave them useful information.

Joren also gave them a recording of a recent Honest Hour episode.

“People ought to hear what happens to a town when nobody listens,” he said.

Tomas gave Ivo a set of maintenance figures.

“Don't use anything you can't explain,” he said.

That night Ivo watched the Honest Hour clip. He recognized Joren's voice: the mechanic who once fixed his mother's car without charging for labor, the father who brought oranges to matches. In the recording Joren called railway managers and union representatives a protected caste that used safety as an excuse for waste.

Ivo stopped the video.

He showed it to Tessa the next morning.

“Your dad's talking about mine.”

“He's talking about a system.”

“He says people like my dad don't work.”

“That's not what he means.”

“Then why say it?”

Tessa watched the clip again. She knew her father was often less careful with words than he ought to be. She knew he had been lied about too. The first feeling made her uneasy. The second gave her something to hold.

“Your dad thinks people like mine are selfish,” she said.

“My dad doesn't even talk to Uncle Dain now. That doesn't mean he hates mechanics.”

“Uncle Dain's a mechanic.”

“He's an electrician.”

“You know what I mean.”

Neither did, precisely.

The school bell rang. They left the argument unfinished and went to class.

***

A new student named Sami sat beside Ivo in mathematics.

Sami had come from Tarven, the country north of Liora where the war had been fought for years. He understood the language well enough to follow most lessons and spoke slowly when he wanted to be certain of a sentence. He was very good at fractions. He disliked the sound of sudden bells.

Ivo had learned not to ask questions about the war unless Sami volunteered an answer.

One afternoon Sami showed him a picture of a house.

“Was it yours?” Ivo asked.

“Yes.”

“It looks nice.”

“It was.”

There was a silence.

“My grandfather made the stairs,” Sami said. “They weren't very straight.”

Ivo smiled. He knew about crooked woodwork. His father could repair almost anything, but the cupboard under their stairs had never closed properly.

“Do you have a picture of inside?”

Sami looked for one. There was a table with a blue cloth, a bowl of fruit, and a woman whose hand reached into the edge of the picture.

“My grandmother,” he said. “She hated photographs.”

“Mine did too.”

“What happened to your grandmother?”

“She died a long time ago. What happened to yours?”

Sami put the phone away.

“I don't know.”

Ivo understood the answer was not an invitation to ask more.

For the rest of the lesson they worked on fractions. Sami finished his worksheet first and quietly helped Ivo with the last question.

That evening Ivo told Tomas about the photograph.

Tomas listened, then asked whether the school was doing enough to help newly arrived children.

“I think so,” Ivo said. “Why?”

“I want them to.”

Ivo knew people online were arguing about refugees. In his mother's feed he had seen stories about people refusing to help them. In clips shared at school he had seen arguments that the government was forgetting its own citizens. His father had opinions, his mother had others, and the people speaking most loudly about Tarven often seemed to know the least about Sami.

“Could I invite him for supper?” Ivo asked.

“Of course.”

He asked Mara too.

“Of course,” she said.

It was a small thing to discover that his parents could still give the same answer.

***

On the morning of Civic Day, someone put a photograph of Tessa's father in the student group chat.

Joren was standing onstage beneath a Renewal banner. The image had been cropped so that a sentence above his head appeared to identify all his supporters as enemies of the nation. Beneath it a student wrote that Tessa's team should not be allowed to represent the school.

Tessa saw the message on the bus.

By the time she reached the gates there were dozens of replies.

Some defended her. Some joined the accusation. Several students mocked the sender. Many reacted with symbols and moved on. What might once have been a foolish comment made in front of five friends had been transformed into a public test of loyalty.

Tessa found Ivo by the bicycle stands.

“Did you say anything?”

“About what?”

She showed him.

He read the messages.

“I didn't see it.”

“Would you have said something?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn't you?”

“I just told you.”

“You're always online.”

“Not before school.”

She stared at him.

It was a bad morning for truth. The platform had made it possible for her to know how many people had seen the photograph and impossible to know who had read it with any care.

“Your mum shared that thing about my dad,” she said.

“My mum didn't post this.”

“Same people.”

“That's not fair.”

“Neither is this.”

They carried the unfinished argument into the workshop, where their bridge stood between them on a bench.

The hinge had finally been repaired.

It opened and closed perfectly.

***

The first two Civic Day presentations went well.

A group of younger students debated whether school meals should be free. They disagreed about cost, argued about what the numbers meant, and ended by proposing a trial. The teachers congratulated them.

Then a video appeared online.

It showed Ms. Pellin telling a parent at an earlier meeting that children needed to learn to argue better than adults. The clip stopped before the rest of her explanation. A caption said the headteacher believed Lioran parents were unfit to teach their own children.

A few parents arrived at the school gates. They had been told that children were being pressured to declare political allegiances. Others came to defend the teachers. A local channel sent a camera.

Inside, the students began receiving messages from adults.

**Tell them what they are teaching you.**

**Do not let the school shame your family.**

**Show them you're not afraid.**

Teachers asked everyone to put their phones away.

Some did.

Others hid them beneath their desks.

Ivo and Tessa were scheduled to present at eleven. At ten forty-five, Tessa said she wanted to remove the railway figures from the display because they made her father look dishonest.

“They're the figures,” Ivo said.

“I know they're figures.”

“Then what's wrong with them?”

“You chose the ones that make your dad right.”

“We chose the ones in the public report.”

“Maybe the report's wrong.”

“Then find a better one.”

“You're being like him.”

“Like who?”

“Your father. Always acting as if facts belong to you.”

Ivo felt something go hard inside him.

“Your dad gets paid to make people angry.”

She went pale.

“Take that back.”

He could have said he didn't know who paid Joren, only that a foundation supported the programme. He could have acknowledged that the accusation was too broad. He had the words. He understood the distinction because he had heard Tomas make it for years.

Instead he said, “Everyone knows.”

Tessa put down the display board.

“Then do it yourself.”

She walked out.

Ivo stood by the bridge, looking at the small motor and the line of wires they had arranged together. They had been friends for seven years. Now he was alone with a working model he no longer wanted to show anyone.

The teacher found him at the bench.

“Where's Tessa?”

“She quit.”

“Did something happen?”

“No.”

It was a lie so familiar to the adults around him that he barely noticed he had told it.

***

The Civic Day ended early.

Outside the gates, two groups of parents were shouting. Most stood at a distance, worried and embarrassed, trying to collect their children without being dragged into the confrontation. One father tried to separate two men who had shoved one another. He was struck in the mouth by accident, or perhaps not. The video that circulated afterward did not show the beginning.

The next day some people insisted a teacher had encouraged violence. Others claimed the entire incident had been arranged to make one political movement look bad. The school published a statement that cited the injuries, the police call, and the canceled event.

A small newspaper later corrected several of the more dramatic claims.

The correction was not shared nearly as widely.

Ivo did not read any of it.

He was thinking about Tessa.

He opened their old message thread. There were photographs of the bridge when it was only a pile of wood. A recording of the motor making the noise of an angry wasp. A joke about a teacher who had sat on a tube of glue. A picture of Tessa holding two crayons, one blue and one yellow, because their mothers had told them the story again at her birthday party.

He typed: **I shouldn't have said that about your dad.**

He looked at the words.

Then he thought of how she had said his father acted as if facts belonged to him. He wanted her to apologize too. He imagined sending the message and receiving no reply, or worse, receiving a screenshot of it in the group chat.

He erased the sentence.

He had watched Tomas and Dain do this for more than a year.

He did not know he was repeating them.

***

At home that evening, Mara and Tomas stood together in the kitchen, reading the message from the school.

They were united in anger at what had happened to Ivo. Then they began arguing about the adults who had gathered outside the gates.

Mara had seen footage of a man carrying a Renewal banner.

Tomas had seen footage of a woman in Common Future colors shouting at the teachers.

Neither knew which footage showed which part of the confrontation.

“Can we not do this tonight?” Mara asked.

“I'm trying to understand what happened.”

“Then listen to what Ivo said.”

Tomas turned to their son.

Ivo was sitting at the table with his school bag on the floor. He had come home prepared to explain the argument with Tessa. Instead he had spent half an hour listening to adults explain their own.

“I didn't say anything,” he whispered.

Mara crouched beside him.

“What's wrong?”

He shook his head.

She put a hand on his shoulder. He moved away. She felt the gesture like a wound.

“Ivo.”

He stood, went upstairs, and shut his bedroom door.

For once neither parent followed him immediately. They were both frightened that if they asked the wrong question, he would stop talking altogether.

***

The next day Sami sat beside Ivo at lunch.

The room was loud, full of students discussing who had done what. Some of the accounts were confident and mutually impossible.

“You and Tessa aren't speaking?” Sami asked.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Our parents.”

Sami considered this.

“Did your parents tell you not to?”

“No.”

“Then why?”

Ivo pulled at the label on his drink.

“It's complicated.”

Sami nodded. He knew what complicated meant.

“In Tarven we had two streets that didn't talk,” he said. “I never knew why. My aunt said it began with a man selling a field. My grandfather said it was politics. My mother said it was older than both of them.”

“What happened?”

“The houses burned during the war. Now the people from both streets live in the same building somewhere else.”

“Do they talk now?”

“I don't know.”

It was the most honest answer Ivo had heard all week.

They finished lunch. Sami showed him a drawing of a circuit he had been working on. Ivo said the switch was in the wrong place. They argued about it, got a pencil, and drew a better one.

When the bell rang, Sami asked whether he was coming over to work on the model later.

“Which model?”

“The drawbridge.”

“It's finished.”

“Then make it better.”

Ivo thought of Tessa.

“I don't think I can.”

Sami looked surprised.

“You built it. Of course you can.”

There were kinds of damage that could be repaired with a stronger hinge.

There were other kinds that could not.

***

Tessa went home and found Joren recording an episode in the garage.

He was speaking into a microphone while a producer sat behind a laptop. The subject was Civic Day. His voice was a little louder than his ordinary speaking voice and carried the rhythm he had developed when people began applauding him.

“Parents have every right to ask what is happening in their children's classrooms,” he said. “When teachers decide the country belongs to one point of view, those parents have a duty to stand up.”

The producer signaled approval.

Tessa waited until the recording stopped.

“Dad.”

Joren removed the headphones.

“What's wrong?”

She told him about the photograph in the student group and the argument with Ivo. He listened with growing anger, which frightened her more than indifference would have.

“Give me the names,” he said.

“No.”

“Somebody's bullying my daughter.”

“I know.”

“I can make sure this stops.”

“That's exactly what I don't want.”

He looked at her, bewildered.

“I can use the programme. These people need to learn—”

“Dad, please.”

The producer closed his laptop and quietly left the room.

Tessa stood beside the workbench where her father had repaired her first bicycle.

“I used to be your daughter at school,” she said. “Now I'm your argument.”

He looked as if she had struck him.

For a long time neither spoke.

Then he said, “I'm sorry.”

She was too angry to be comforted by the words.

“Then stop talking about it on the show.”

Joren looked toward the microphone.

He had already recorded forty minutes.

He could ask the producer not to publish it. He might lose the payment for the episode. There would be another sponsor to satisfy, another argument about free expression, another audience demanding an answer.

“I'll think about it,” he said.

Tessa's face changed. Not into rage. Into the careful emptiness of a child learning that a parent's promise had conditions.

She walked out.

Joren sat alone beside the microphone until his phone rang.

***

The school closed the workshop for repairs the following week.

Somebody had knocked over several cabinets during the disorder after Civic Day. The damage wasn't severe, but the teachers had to inspect the equipment and restore order before children could use it again.

The drawbridge remained on a shelf, its supports carefully labeled, the little motor disconnected.

Ivo asked Ms. Pellin whether he and Tessa would still receive marks for their project.

“You both did the work,” she said. “You will both receive the marks.”

“But we didn't present.”

“No.”

“Does that mean it failed?”

The headteacher looked at him.

“I don't think that is a question about marks.”

He said nothing.

She brought the model down and set it on the desk. She connected the battery. The bridge rose smoothly, paused, and lowered.

“That's good engineering,” she said.

Ivo watched it move.

“We argued about the hinge.”

“Did you?”

“Loads. She wanted a steel one. I wanted wood.”

“And?”

“She was right.”

Ms. Pellin smiled.

“Does she know?”

Ivo stared at the bridge.

“No.”

“Then perhaps that's one thing you could tell her.”

He took out his phone, opened the old message thread, and found her name.

This time he typed only four words.

**You were right about the hinge.**

He sent them before he could change his mind.

The message showed as delivered.

He waited.

An hour passed. The school day ended. At home the phone remained silent.

He set the phone beside his bed and watched the screen go dark.

Outside, Merrow's streetlights came on, one after another. Through the houses, families sat before their separate screens, receiving separate accounts of the same school day.

At midnight, Tessa read the message.

She wanted to answer. She almost did.

Then she saw another message in the student group, a photograph of her father beneath an accusation that had already been disputed. Someone had tagged Ivo in the replies.

She locked the phone.

The next morning she passed him in the corridor and looked away.

The bridge would open perfectly whenever anyone pressed the switch.

Neither child touched it again.

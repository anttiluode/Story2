# Chapter Fourteen — The Election Nobody Could Lose

*Republic of Liora. Autumn, after Mrs. Kade's funeral.*

On election morning Tomas Arden discovered that the kitchen clock was seventeen minutes slow.

There had been a power interruption in the night, too brief for most of the house to notice. The oven's digital display flashed midnight. The old clock above the sink continued to tick, but its hands no longer agreed with the time on Tomas's phone.

He corrected it, made coffee, and read the instructions for voting that had come through the letterbox.

Mara was already awake.

She stood at the counter in the sweater she wore when she expected to be photographed. She had been volunteering for Common Future's district campaign for six weeks. There were posters in the boot of her car and a folded banner behind the front door, waiting to be returned to the campaign office.

"You're going early?" Tomas asked.

"Polling station first. Then I'm helping with transport."

"Transport to the polling station?"

"Older people, anyone who can't get there."

He looked up from the instructions.

"That's good."

For a moment she seemed pleased. It had become rare for one of them to say something uncomplicated about the other's work.

"You?"

"After my shift."

"You'll make it?"

"Yes."

He wanted to ask whether she'd be home for supper. She wanted to ask whom he intended to vote for. Neither asked.

Ivo came downstairs carrying a school exercise book. At thirteen he had begun moving through the house as though the adults were temporary obstacles between him and the things he needed.

"Are we having the country tonight?" he asked.

Tomas frowned.

"What?"

"The results. They keep saying it's the most important election. Are we having everyone over to watch?"

In earlier years the Ardens had invited neighbors to watch election returns. There had been tea, bad jokes, argument, and one memorable evening when Mrs. Kade fell asleep before the first constituency was called and woke at dawn furious that the country had made its choice without consulting her.

Mara and Tomas looked at one another.

"No," Mara said.

"Why?"

"People are busy."

Ivo knew that was not the reason. He had learned that a true sentence could be used to hide a more difficult truth.

He took bread from the cupboard.

"Okay."

His mother watched him spread butter on a slice so thinly that it tore.

"Want me to do that?"

"I've got it."

He sounded like Tomas, which made her love him and resent the morning in the same breath.

***

Liora's electoral system had been designed by people who distrusted absolute power.

The republic elected a parliament through a mix of local representatives and proportional seats. Governments usually required coalitions. They were messy, occasionally short-lived, and often infuriating. The system had survived wars, recessions, and the disgrace of a prime minister who had used public money to renovate a holiday cottage.

The National Electoral Commission was independent by law. Polling places were supervised by officials nominated through several bodies. Ballot boxes were sealed in public. Parties could observe the count. Courts could order recounts.

The system was not flawless. No human institution was.

But for decades, a losing party had been able to say something vital to its supporters: *We lost this election. We will contest the next one.*

This year, both major movements had spent months telling their members that defeat would mean the republic had already been stolen.

Renewal's leader, **Garran Holt**, stood at rallies before enormous screens showing factories, shuttered shops, and unfinished housing estates. He promised to restore competence, responsibility, and the dignity of work. Some of his proposed policies had real support among people who were tired of watching local services deteriorate. Others were slogans broad enough to mean whatever the audience most wanted.

Common Future's national leader, **Nera Valen**, had been a labor lawyer before entering parliament. She was careful with details and could explain precisely why a budget measure would harm a clinic. She also had a growing habit of describing people who disagreed with her as though they were knowingly voting to harm their own neighbors.

Both leaders had political skill.

Both were surrounded by people who told them that moderation would be interpreted as weakness.

On the final night of the campaign, Holt told his supporters that the people of Liora would be heard *whether the old establishment liked it or not*.

In a rally across the capital, Valen told hers that the country faced a choice *between democracy and those who had stopped believing in it*.

The next morning, the newspapers printed these statements side by side.

Each movement read the other's quotation as a threat and its own as a warning.

***

Sela Orin volunteered as a polling-place assistant.

She had done it before. The work was dull, precise, and satisfying. She checked identification where the law required it, directed voters to booths, and made sure people who needed help were given it without anyone learning how they voted.

At eight in the morning, an elderly man arrived carrying the wrong identification card. Sela helped him find the correct procedure. He thanked her.

At ten, a young woman asked whether her vote would be public. Sela explained the secrecy of the ballot.

At noon, two people wearing rival movement badges argued about a poster outside the legal boundary. The presiding officer asked them both to remove the badges while inside. They complied.

At one, a man began filming Sela's desk.

She asked him to stop because his camera might capture private voter information.

He refused.

A police officer came and explained the rules. The man put away his phone, then went outside and told an audience online that the state had silenced him for documenting the election.

By three, the video had been viewed thousands of times.

Sela was visible in it for four seconds.

A message appeared in the library account she managed.

**WE KNOW WHAT YOU ARE DOING.**

She showed it to the presiding officer. He told her to save it, and she did.

She continued her shift.

At six a father arrived with two small children. One child was sleepy, the other wanted to know why he couldn't put a drawing in the ballot box. The father explained that the box was for choosing the government.

The child asked, "Can I choose a good one?"

The father laughed.

"That's what we're all trying to do."

Sela thought it was the only sentence she had heard all day that belonged to everyone.

***

In Merrow, the railway had been running with reduced staffing for months.

Tomas had finished his shift at seven and reached the polling station with time to spare. The queue stretched halfway around the building. He recognized three people from the depot. One nodded. One looked away. The third asked if he knew whether the election would be fair.

"Why wouldn't it be?" Tomas asked.

"People are saying boxes are going missing."

"Where?"

"All over."

"Have they reported it?"

"That's the thing. They wouldn't tell us if they had."

Tomas glanced toward the door, where officials were recording the arrival of each voter.

He thought of the maintenance systems he worked on. A fault could be hidden. A record could be falsified. The possibility of such things was why inspection and independent checks existed. But a person who had decided the inspection was dishonest before looking at it had made himself impossible to reassure.

"Ask the commission," Tomas said.

The man laughed bitterly.

"Exactly."

Tomas went inside and voted.

He had never told Mara which party he supported. She had never asked. In their early marriage, that privacy had been a kind of mutual respect. Now the secrecy felt like another wall.

He emerged into the dark and stood beneath the school canopy while rain ran from the gutter.

He wondered whether to call Dain.

His brother would be voting somewhere across town. They might have chosen differently; they might not. Tomas no longer knew enough about Dain to guess.

He put his phone away and went home.

***

By ten that night, the televised results were too close for anyone to call.

Renewal had gained heavily in former industrial districts. Common Future had held large cities and improved in places where younger renters and public-sector workers had organized. The smaller parties—the Rural Alliance, the Civic Moderates, the Free Coast—held seats that might decide the government.

In one constituency the count was suspended because a sealed container had arrived with a damaged exterior tag. Officials photographed it, notified party observers, and secured the ballots while a judge considered the proper procedure.

The judge ordered a supervised count with additional witnesses.

The ballots were counted.

The result changed nothing outside that constituency.

By midnight, however, the photograph of the damaged tag had become proof of a stolen election to thousands of people who had not heard the judge's decision.

A different clip showed a party observer shouting at election officials. It ended before the observer apologized for misreading a form.

The clips traveled farther than the explanations.

At Common Future's Merrow office, Mara watched the numbers with Alia Venn and a room full of exhausted volunteers.

Someone opened a bottle of sparkling cider. Someone else said it was too soon.

"We're not losing," Alia said.

The sentence was greeted with applause.

In another building across town, Dain watched Holt's national broadcast with colleagues from the power company. People cheered when Renewal took a district and cursed when it lost one. Dain had stopped wearing the party badge to work, but he still found its promises attractive: fewer pointless contracts, better apprenticeships, somebody willing to ask why the machinery of ordinary life cost so much and worked so badly.

His phone vibrated with a message from Ivo.

**Do you know who won yet?**

Dain typed: **Nobody. Not yet.**

He watched the words, then added:

**How's school?**

Ivo replied a few minutes later.

**Fine.**

Dain knew enough about the family to hear the distance inside that word.

***

At four in the morning the result was clear enough to announce.

Renewal had won the largest share of votes and the most seats of any single party. It did not have a majority. Common Future and two smaller parties together held just enough seats to form a government, provided their coalition negotiations succeeded.

The Electoral Commission's preliminary figures were consistent across the independently observed counts. Challenges in several districts were being reviewed. None of the substantiated counting errors identified so far would change the broad distribution of seats.

The law was not ambiguous about who could govern: whoever secured the confidence of parliament.

The country was not willing to hear it.

Holt appeared before cameras at dawn.

"The people chose change," he said. "If the establishment assembles a government of losers to keep that change from happening, the people will remember."

It was not an explicit call to disobey the constitution.

It was heard as one by some supporters and as a necessary defense of democracy by others.

At noon Valen spoke.

"The constitutional process is clear," she said. "We will not permit threats to overrule lawful democratic government."

She was right about the process.

Then she added, "Those who reject the verdict of our institutions reject the republic itself."

The statement made no distinction between people questioning a particular counting decision, people peacefully advocating electoral reform, and people threatening violence.

It made for an effective clip.

Renewal's accounts circulated the second sentence without the first. Common Future's accounts circulated Holt's warning without the explanation about coalition negotiations.

The two speeches became a conversation between edited shadows.

***

The legal challenges proceeded.

A court found an administrative error in one district and ordered a limited recount. It corrected several hundred votes. Another challenge was dismissed because the evidence did not support the allegation. A third identified unlawful campaign activity by a local group and referred it for investigation without invalidating ballots cast by uninvolved voters.

The commission published its decisions and the underlying procedures.

Several officials were praised for their diligence by people who agreed with the results.

Others were threatened by strangers who did not.

At the library Sela read the official judgments and placed a copy in a public information folder. She did not endorse a party. She told visitors that they could examine the rulings themselves.

A young man came to the desk.

"Do you believe the election was legitimate?"

"Based on the official count and the reviewed challenges, yes."

"So you're with them."

"I'm with the records."

"That's the same thing."

She watched him leave.

The remarkable feature of the exchange was not his anger. It was the speed with which a procedure had become a person.

***

Parliament met six weeks later.

The negotiations had been bitter. Common Future had promised concessions on housing and public employment. The Civic Moderates insisted on new limits for government borrowing. The Free Coast demanded powers over regional transport. Nobody got everything they wanted.

They signed an agreement.

Nera Valen was elected prime minister by the narrowest workable parliamentary majority.

It was lawful.

It was also fragile, and everyone knew it.

Across Liora people who supported the coalition celebrated. Others protested peacefully, demanding new elections or reform of the electoral system. A smaller group tried to force its way into the parliamentary grounds and was turned back by police. Several officers and demonstrators were hurt. Investigations began.

For three days, the news followed the confrontation.

No one spent much time discussing the government's actual program.

At the railway, Tomas heard that the coalition would preserve some inspection funding but delay equipment upgrades. At the power company, Dain learned that promised procurement reform had been traded away in negotiations.

Both men had reasons to criticize the agreement.

Neither could find a public argument that remained about the agreement for more than a minute.

***

Ivo watched the parliamentary vote at home.

Tomas sat on one end of the sofa. Mara sat in an armchair. They had each returned late from work and were too tired to argue openly.

When the speaker announced the new government, Mara let out a breath.

"At least it's settled."

Tomas looked at her.

"Is it?"

"The vote's done."

"The government barely has the numbers."

"That's still a government."

"I didn't say it wasn't."

She waited, expecting another qualification, another lecture about rules she already knew.

Instead he asked, "Do you think they can get the sports hall open?"

Mara stared at the television.

"I don't know."

It was the answer she might have given a year earlier without embarrassment.

Ivo sat between them with his knees pulled up.

"Who lost?" he asked.

"Renewal," Mara said.

"Not exactly," Tomas said at the same time.

Ivo sighed.

He had begun to dread a sentence spoken by two adults at once. It always meant a conflict about words that appeared simple.

"What happens to the people who lost?" he asked.

"They become the opposition," Tomas said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means they still represent the people who voted for them. They challenge the government. They propose laws. They try again next time."

Ivo considered this.

"Then why are they saying the country is over?"

Mara looked at Tomas. He looked back.

Neither had a good answer.

On the screen, supporters of the new coalition waved flags outside parliament. Across the square, opponents waved the same flags and shouted that the government had betrayed them.

Ivo had never realized a country's flag could become two different things depending on who was holding it.

***

In the northern capital, Dr. Irena Solvik reviewed the election analysis with Director Kest.

The department had predicted heightened distrust, a prolonged dispute over legitimacy, and difficulty forming a government. It had not correctly predicted the distribution of seats.

Kest examined the written assessment.

"A coalition of losers," he said, quoting Holt's phrase. "Useful."

"Politically effective," Irena agreed.

"Did our work contribute?"

"To the general environment, plausibly. We cannot assign a specific electoral outcome to the program."

Kest smiled.

"Doctor, I asked whether it contributed."

She knew what he wanted to hear.

"Yes, Director."

He marked the page.

Niko Rahl, sitting at the other end of the table, said nothing.

He had prepared a separate analysis showing that the strongest predictor of several district results was the local economy. He had also found that some Veyr-linked activity coincided with rising distrust but could not be isolated as the cause. His report remained in the appendix.

Kest did not open it.

"Can the new government govern?" he asked.

Irena thought of the coalition agreement, the legal rulings, the protests, and the still-functioning courts. The republic had not collapsed. It remained capable of decisions. A hundred ordinary offices had opened that morning, and people inside them had worked.

"With difficulty," she said.

"Then we continue."

***

That evening Samuel Rees published an editorial in the *Merrow Chronicle*.

It was not long. He had cut the newspaper to fewer pages and could not afford sentiment.

He wrote that people were entitled to dislike the result, to challenge mistakes through lawful procedures, to demand political change, and to protest a government they opposed. None of those things made them enemies of the republic.

He wrote that the courts had reviewed the allegations before them, that the count had produced a valid parliament, and that the coalition had won the votes required to govern.

He wrote that he disliked some of the coalition's proposals and some of the opposition's rhetoric.

Then he wrote the sentence he had been trying to find since election night:

**A government can be legally chosen and still fail to earn trust. A country survives only if losing an argument remains different from losing the right to belong.**

The editorial drew praise from readers who liked the first half and anger from readers who read the second half as an attack.

Several subscriptions were canceled.

One elderly woman sent a handwritten note enclosing enough money to pay for a year's delivery.

Samuel placed the note in his desk.

It would not save the newspaper.

He was grateful for it anyway.

***

By December, Liora had a government and a parliament, a functioning court system, a lawful election result, and an opposition with more voters than any single governing party.

The constitution was intact.

In the southern capital, a contractor repaired a broken streetlamp outside the parliamentary building. Across the river, protesters prepared another demonstration. In Merrow, the sports hall remained closed. Tomas went to work before dawn. Mara tried to arrange a new fundraising meeting. Dain inspected a transformer that had been neglected for eighteen months.

Their lives continued.

But there was a new sentence in the language of Liora, spoken by people on both sides, sometimes under their breath and sometimes into a microphone.

**If we lose again, there will be no country left.**

It was an astonishing thing to believe about an election.

It became an ordinary thing to say.

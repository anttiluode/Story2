# Chapter Fifteen — A Republic of Small Rooms

*Merrow. Winter into spring.*

The first room to close was the one above the bakery.

Common Future had rented it for eighteen months. The stairs smelled of yeast, the radiators clicked, and the old wooden chairs were too narrow for anyone wearing a thick coat. Mara Arden had first come there to talk about bus fares, and a lawyer had given her a number that helped her football club challenge a municipal decision.

Now the landlord was ending the arrangement.

Alia Venn held up the letter at the last meeting.

"They say it's about insurance," she said.

The room muttered.

A man near the window suggested the landlord had been threatened. Someone else said Renewal supporters had been gathering signatures against political meetings in local businesses. A woman insisted the bakery had received anonymous calls.

Mara asked whether anyone had spoken to the landlord.

"I have," Alia said. "He says the insurer raised the premium."

"Did it?"

"He wouldn't show me the paperwork."

"He's not obliged to."

"Do you believe him?"

"I don't know him well enough to believe or disbelieve him."

The room went quiet.

Mara recognized the sensation: a single ordinary question could make her seem disloyal to people who had once welcomed her.

Alia softened her voice.

"Nobody's asking you to choose sides, Mara."

That was exactly what the room had been asking her to do for a year.

Outside, the baker was loading trays into a van. Mara waited until he finished, then asked whether the closure would affect his business.

"Already has," he said. "Half my customers think I'm one of them. The other half think I'm the other lot."

"Who raised your insurance?"

"The insurer."

"Why?"

He pointed to the cracked plaster above the back door.

"The wiring upstairs needs replacing. And somebody broke a window last month. It isn't all politics."

"Would you have kept renting the room if the wiring was fine?"

He wiped flour from his hands.

"Maybe. But I don't want the fighting either."

There were two explanations, both true.

The building needed repairs. The town had made a meeting room too expensive to maintain.

Mara thanked him and went home with a box of unsold leaflets.

***

The next room was at the railway depot.

A month after the election, management announced another delay in replacing a signaling control unit. The supplier had increased its prices. A government procurement review had frozen several contracts, partly because the new coalition had promised to examine waste and partly because the previous administration had signed agreements no one wanted to defend.

Tomas agreed that some contracts should be examined.

He did not agree that the inspection schedule could be cut while everyone argued.

The union called a meeting. There were forty-two members on the roster; twenty-seven attended. The chairman warned that management was hiding risk behind accounting language. A younger worker said the union had become a mouthpiece for Common Future and could no longer be trusted to represent people who voted differently.

"How does your vote change whether a signal is safe?" Tomas asked.

"It changes whether anyone listens to me," the worker replied.

The chairman said the union represented all members.

Someone laughed.

Then a veteran technician, who had voted for Renewal and served on the union committee for years, stood and said he would resign rather than sit through another meeting where safety was treated as a question of identity.

He walked out.

Tomas watched him go.

The man was one of the few people in the room who knew the old west crossing better than Tomas did.

A week later he resigned.

His skills did not disappear. He took them to a private contractor serving industrial sites. The railway lost a man it could not replace quickly.

When a fault occurred at the west crossing that spring, the remaining technicians worked through the night to keep the line operating under restrictions.

Nobody was injured.

The depot manager called that a success.

Tomas called it luck supported by exhausted workers.

***

The third room was the district clinic.

Lev Orin arrived for a morning shift to find two nurses in the car park arguing over a patient's transfer.

The hospital thirty kilometers away had space in an observation ward, but the shared transport arrangement had been suspended pending a dispute between public and private providers. The ambulance service could still handle emergencies. Nonemergency transfers were supposed to be coordinated through a new system that was not yet operational.

This was not a secret plot.

It was a badly managed transition that might have happened in a less frightened country. In Liora it became another proof of moral treachery.

A nurse said the new system was a gift to private contractors.

Another said the old system had wasted money and endangered patients.

Both had evidence for parts of their claims.

Neither had the authority to move the patient.

Lev called the receiving hospital, then the transport desk, then a colleague who had a vehicle approved for medical transfers. By noon they found a lawful way to bring the patient across.

The patient was seventy-six, recovering from pneumonia, and too weak to be angry.

"Am I in trouble?" he asked as they helped him into the vehicle.

"No," Lev said.

"Then why has everyone been shouting?"

She had no answer that would reassure him.

He watched her face and patted her sleeve.

"Never mind," he said. "I know you're trying."

The kindness nearly undid her.

At the end of the shift a supervisor asked Lev to help draft a public statement. The clinic was being accused of refusing treatment to people who supported Renewal. That accusation was false. It was also true that the clinic had longer delays, fewer staff, and a scheduling system with serious faults.

Lev wrote a clear account.

The legal office removed the admission about the faulty system.

The communications office added a sentence praising the government.

A senior official replaced the entire statement with three paragraphs about the clinic's commitment to equal treatment.

Lev refused to sign it.

"Why?" her supervisor asked.

"Because it answers a question nobody asked."

"The accusation is serious."

"So is the fact that people are waiting too long for care."

"We can't solve every problem in one statement."

"No. But we could tell the truth about two of them."

The supervisor sent the statement without her name.

It was shared online by people defending the clinic and mocked by people who wanted the clinic destroyed.

The waiting list did not become shorter.

***

The fourth room belonged to the Merrow Repair Circle.

For thirty years, volunteers had opened a municipal workshop two Saturdays a month to mend household appliances, bicycles, lamps, radios, and furniture. The work was not glamorous. It kept appliances out of rubbish heaps and helped families who could not afford replacements.

Dain had repaired electrical devices there since he was seventeen.

Tomas had occasionally helped with motors and control switches.

Sela had brought people from the library who wanted to learn simple repairs. Mrs. Kade used to arrive with an object too old to fix and insist that the volunteers try anyway.

The Repair Circle had never required members to agree about anything outside the room.

After the election, a disagreement began over a proposed sponsorship from a company whose director was prominent in Renewal. Some members thought the money was welcome. Others believed accepting it would turn the Circle into a political advertisement.

The sponsorship was modest. It would pay for insulation and replacement tools.

The debate lasted six weeks.

An amended proposal would have accepted the funds without allowing logos or speeches. It nearly passed. Then someone circulated an old photograph of the director at a rally, and the argument began again.

The town council offered to mediate.

The volunteers refused because they no longer trusted the council to be neutral.

The lease expired while they argued.

On the final Saturday Dain came to collect his tools.

There were a few broken lamps on the shelves, a bicycle with no back wheel, and a radio whose owner had died before it could be repaired. A handwritten sign above the workbench said:

**IF IT IS BROKEN, TRY TO UNDERSTAND IT BEFORE THROWING IT AWAY.**

Dain had painted the sign with Tomas when they were teenagers.

He took it down.

A younger volunteer asked whether the Circle might reopen somewhere else.

"Maybe," Dain said.

"Where?"

Dain looked around at the empty room.

The volunteers could afford a few tools, but not a building. The council could afford a building, but no longer had the trust required to allocate it without an accusation. The sponsor could afford the repairs, but its money had become a flag.

"I don't know," he said.

He carried the sign home.

***

Sela's attendance book had changed.

At first she had recorded the names of missing volunteers. Then she began adding the activities they could no longer carry out. The summer clothing drive was now run by two separate groups that would not share vehicles. The river cleanup happened in rival sections, with volunteers collecting rubbish only on their own side of a footbridge. A neighborhood watch group had split over whether its information meetings should include representatives of the police.

There were still hundreds of acts of kindness every day. People fed relatives, brought medicine, checked on elderly neighbors, and helped strangers whose cars broke down. Sela refused to write a story in which the entire country had become cruel.

What had weakened was the machinery that turned those acts into something larger.

A grandmother could help a child cross the road.

She could not, alone, repair a school transport system.

A nurse could comfort a frightened patient.

She could not, alone, organize an ambulance network.

Dain could fix a kettle.

He could not give the whole town a workshop if no one trusted anyone else with the keys.

Sela had once taught history. She knew that governments were not made entirely of laws, and nations were not made entirely of flags. They depended on countless boring agreements: who would collect rubbish, maintain the roads, check the fire alarms, keep a record that a person had been born or had died.

Those agreements could survive disagreement about policy.

They could not survive indefinitely if every custodian was suspected of serving an enemy.

She wrote that observation in her notebook.

Then she crossed out the word *enemy*.

She had begun to distrust any word that made people sound simpler than they were.

***

In early March a transformer failed near the western housing estate.

The failure was ordinary in its origin. The unit was old. A replacement had been requested, delayed, reviewed, and delayed again. Nobody knew precisely when it would fail, and neither the technicians nor the managers could have guaranteed its remaining life.

When it failed, power disappeared from three streets and a small block of sheltered housing.

Dain was on the response team.

He arrived at midnight with two colleagues. The transformer had to be isolated and a temporary supply arranged. A technician from the railway was called because a nearby signaling cabinet might have been affected.

It was Tomas.

The brothers met under a portable work light, wearing reflective jackets and carrying tools.

"Evening," Dain said.

"Looks that way."

They examined the site. Dain explained the fault. Tomas checked the signaling cabinet and confirmed that the railway equipment had switched to a safe restricted mode.

For two hours they worked without mentioning the election.

They disagreed about a cable route. Tomas showed the diagram. Dain pointed to a change made during an earlier repair. They checked the actual equipment and found that both drawings were partly outdated.

"Well," Dain said, "that's encouraging."

Tomas laughed.

It was the old laugh.

For a while, there were only two men trying to make something work.

The temporary supply was restored shortly before four. Lights came on in the sheltered-housing block. A resident opened a window and called down to thank the crew.

At dawn someone posted a photograph of the repair team.

Tomas and Dain were visible beside the service vehicle.

The caption said that public-sector incompetence had nearly frozen pensioners in their beds.

A different account used the same photograph to accuse private contractors of sabotaging public infrastructure.

Neither knew who employed the people in the picture.

By lunchtime the photograph was an exhibit in a political argument.

Dain called Tomas.

"Have you seen this?"

"Yes."

"They're blaming us for fixing it."

"Of course they are."

"I ought to say something."

"Don't. You'll just make it worse."

Dain hesitated.

"I'm getting tired of being told when I can speak."

Tomas could hear the old grievance approaching.

"I'm talking about that specific photograph," he said.

"And I'm talking about my job."

The call ended badly.

It did not erase the work they had done together during the night. The lights were still on. The residents still had heat.

But the next time the brothers met, the first thing they remembered was the call rather than the repair.

***

At school, Ivo and Sami were given permission to use the workshop again.

The old drawbridge was still on a shelf. Its motor had been disconnected after the Civic Day dispute, and someone had removed the battery pack.

Ivo looked at it, then at Sami.

"Leave it," he said.

"Why?"

"It isn't ours."

"It is partly yours."

"Not anymore."

They began making a small generator from salvaged parts. Sami had found a damaged motor and wanted to turn it into a hand-cranked charger. The teacher approved the project because it involved useful physics and almost no public controversy.

They worked well together.

Sami was patient where Ivo was impatient. Ivo improvised where Sami insisted on measurements. They argued about every wire. They were beginning to trust the argument.

One afternoon Tessa passed the open workshop door.

She saw the generator on the bench and stopped.

"That bearing's going to seize," she said.

Ivo looked up.

"It's fine."

"Not if you run it at that speed."

Sami examined the bearing.

"She's right."

Ivo's face grew hot.

"Nobody asked."

Tessa's expression closed.

"Suit yourself."

She walked away.

Sami waited until she was out of sight.

"She was right," he said again.

"I know."

"Then why did you say that?"

Ivo stared at the motor.

Because he had been waiting for a reply to his message about the hinge for months. Because he had watched her turn away in corridors. Because apologizing had once made him feel small and he did not want to experience it again.

"I don't know," he said.

Sami took a screwdriver from the tray.

"That's okay. But we need a different bearing."

They continued working.

The generator turned.

It made enough electricity to light one small bulb.

They switched off the room lights and watched the bulb glow in the darkness.

***

The *Merrow Chronicle* closed its second-floor office in April.

The paper did not shut down. Samuel Rees negotiated a cheaper printing contract and moved the remaining staff into the ground-floor newsroom. Two reporters left. A photographer began working three days a week. The investigative budget, never large, was reduced to almost nothing.

Eva helped carry boxes downstairs.

In one was a stack of old editions. The headlines described road accidents, football victories, marriages, elections, and floods. There were notices of school plays and photographs of ribbon-cutting ceremonies. The old paper had been far from perfect. It had missed scandals and printed errors. It had also given residents a place where their town appeared as one town.

Samuel looked at the stack.

"We used to argue about which story deserved page one," he said.

"We still do."

"Now we argue about whether people will believe a headline if they don't like the answer."

Eva set down the box.

"We've always had angry readers."

"Yes. But they used to be angry about what we wrote."

He picked up a paper from fifteen years earlier.

"Now they can be angry about what somebody says we wrote."

He put it back.

Eva had continued investigating Harborbridge Civic Services. Its public filings were incomplete but not inherently unlawful. The two funding cases remained documented. Several organizations had refused to answer questions. Her attempts to trace the ultimate ownership reached a gap in the records.

She had begun to suspect that another source would be required.

Samuel knew it.

"Don't let the need for a bigger story make you careless," he said.

"I won't."

"People want a single explanation."

"I know."

"Maybe there isn't one."

"Maybe there are several, and one of them has a name."

Samuel studied her.

"Then find the name."

***

At the end of April, a meeting of neighborhood association leaders was held in the municipal building.

Sela attended because the library still coordinated volunteer services for elderly residents. The room contained people who had once helped one another without checking affiliations. Now several groups refused to share sign-in sheets or contact lists. Some cited privacy. Others cited threats. Some had legitimate concerns; others distrusted the person asking for the information.

A woman running a clothing drive said she could no longer guarantee that volunteers from different districts would work safely together.

A man organizing road maintenance said he had received anonymous messages accusing him of favoring neighborhoods that voted for the coalition.

A retired teacher asked whether they could at least maintain one shared emergency contact system.

The chair proposed creating a neutral coordination group.

"Who appoints it?" somebody asked.

A second person demanded to know who would audit it.

A third said that if the mayor appointed anyone, the group would not be neutral.

The meeting lasted three hours.

They agreed to meet again.

Afterward, Sela stood outside beneath the rain and watched people hurry to their cars.

She thought of the failed summer supper, the funeral that had briefly restored the old feeling of a town, and the hundreds of little decisions made since then to keep people separate.

A republic was supposed to contain disagreements large enough to require institutions.

Here was a room full of people who wanted an institution.

They could no longer agree on who would hold the keys.

***

At home Lev found Sela sitting beside the closed attendance book.

"How bad?" she asked.

Sela shook her head.

"Not bad. Ordinary."

"That's not what your face says."

"I think the ordinary part is becoming the worst part."

Lev made tea.

They sat at the kitchen table while rain moved against the glass. There were no cameras, no banners, no strangers judging what they said. The power was on. The pipes worked. Their house was warm.

Sela placed her hand over Lev's.

"I'm sorry I've spent so much time counting what we've lost."

Lev looked at her.

"Then count something else."

"What?"

"What hasn't gone yet."

Sela opened the notebook to a clean page.

She wrote down the transformer repair. Two brothers had restored power together. The nurse who had sat with the frightened patient. The teachers who reopened the workshop. Sami and Ivo, making a bulb light in the dark.

She paused.

Those things mattered.

They were not enough to replace the institutions that were failing around them. But if she recorded only failure, the record itself would become dishonest.

She continued writing.

Above the page she wrote:

**What still works.**

In the northern capital, a statistical model displayed a downward line showing participation in cross-group civic activities.

The model could not see a boy turning a generator by hand.

It could not see two women sitting at a kitchen table.

It could not see the lights coming back on in sheltered housing.

It was still telling a story.

It was simply not the whole one.

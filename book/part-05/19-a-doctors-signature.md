# Chapter Nineteen — A Doctor's Signature

*Merrow. January.*

The first person Oren Pell saw that morning was a man who believed his dead brother was standing in the doorway.

The man was frightened. He had not slept for days, and his speech ran ahead of itself in bursts that were difficult to follow. He was not a danger because he believed something strange. He was a patient in distress, and Oren wanted to know what was happening to him.

They talked for forty minutes.

Oren asked about sleep, medication, the brother, and the man's own understanding of his experience. He listened when the man said that sometimes he knew the doorway was empty and sometimes he did not. He asked permission before examining him. Together with the ward team he made a plan for care, including close follow-up and assessment of possible medical causes.

At the end the man said, "I know it sounds mad."

Oren shook his head.

"It sounds frightening. That's why we're taking it seriously."

The man went out looking a little less afraid.

Oren recorded what he had observed and what remained uncertain. He wrote about the patient's own wishes. He did not pretend the consultation had solved everything.

Then a new file arrived on his desk.

It was marked:

**PUBLIC DISORDER ACT — PRIORITY ASSESSMENT**

The person named on the first page was Sela Orin.

***

Oren knew the name.

Not personally, though he had seen her in the hospital café with Lev. He knew her from Eva Maren's reporting. She was a librarian who had been recording the decline of volunteer associations and public trust in Merrow. Some of her observations had been quoted in the *Chronicle*. She had helped people examine published documents concerning the Northern Directorate's covert program.

The referral described her differently.

It said she displayed a persistent belief that foreign authorities had orchestrated the destruction of her community. It claimed she had shown increasing preoccupation with this idea, had repeatedly challenged municipal staff, and was unable to accept reassurance from authorized sources.

The file included excerpts from a council meeting, complaints from members of the public, and a report that she had attempted to circulate controversial political documents in a municipal library.

A line in the summary caught Oren's eye.

**Patient maintains belief in an entity styled "Department of Civic Weather" despite official rejection of this narrative.**

He opened the *Merrow Chronicle* website.

The report containing that name was there, along with the authorization document, the corroborating financial records, the methodological qualifications, and the Northern Directorate's denial. The evidence was not perfect. It did not need to be perfect to establish that Sela's belief was grounded in something other than an invented entity.

He read the referral again.

It did not mention the published documents.

It did not mention the government's own announced inquiry.

It did not say she had threatened anyone.

At the bottom was a request for an urgent risk opinion, with the possibility of temporary secure placement if she were judged unable to cooperate with the administrative process.

Oren picked up the telephone.

"Who authorized this referral?"

A voice from the emergency coordination office gave him a case number.

"I can read the number," Oren said. "I asked who authorized it."

The person on the line said the referral had been approved by the municipal public-order liaison.

"Has she been seen by a clinician?"

"That's why we're contacting you."

"Has anyone alleged a medical emergency?"

"There are concerns about escalating beliefs and failure to comply with official instructions."

"Which instructions?"

A pause.

"Doctor, the details are in the file."

He looked at the file.

That was the problem.

***

Sela was asked to attend the hospital for a voluntary interview.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday. It described the appointment as an opportunity to clarify concerns and provide her perspective. It did not mention the possibility of secure placement in the first paragraph. That appeared later, under a heading about administrative options if assessment could not be completed.

Lev read the letter twice.

"What is this?"

Sela took it from her.

"I think it's about the library board."

"Why is the hospital involved?"

"Because of the new law."

Lev sat down.

The clinic had spent weeks receiving guidance about public-order referrals. She knew how easily a person could be pushed from an administrative problem into a medical category if nobody insisted on the distinction.

"We need a lawyer," she said.

Sela was still looking at the appointment details.

"I haven't done anything."

"I know."

"Then why would I need one?"

Lev placed her hand over the letter.

"Because someone is asking whether you can be made to stay somewhere against your will."

Sela stared at her.

They had survived long hours, threats against the clinic, and years of political arguments. She had known that people might mock her work, perhaps even accuse her of wrongdoing. She had not imagined the state would ask a doctor to decide whether the evidence she kept was a symptom.

She picked up her notebook.

Lev stopped her.

"Not everything."

"What?"

"Bring what proves the facts. Not every book you've written."

Sela looked wounded.

"I'm trying to make sure they understand."

"I know. But you don't have to put your whole life on trial to answer one false accusation."

They found a solicitor willing to attend.

His name was Aron Vale, a quiet man who had spent years representing patients and families in disputes over care. He did not assume the hospital was acting in bad faith. He had known good clinicians, including Oren Pell, and had seen the practical difficulty of making rapid decisions when somebody genuinely needed help.

He also recognized that the letter mixed two different questions.

"Did she threaten anyone?" he asked.

"No," Lev said.

"Did she violate a court order?"

"No."

"Has anyone described symptoms apart from the allegations she made?"

Sela handed him the referral summary.

He read it.

"Then we'll start there," he said.

***

The interview took place in a room with a small window.

Sela arrived wearing a blue coat and carrying a folder. Lev sat beside her. Aron took a chair near the door. Oren entered with no white coat and introduced himself without mentioning the new law.

For several minutes he asked ordinary clinical questions.

Was she sleeping? Eating? Working? Did she feel safe at home? Had she been frightened by the anonymous messages? Had she considered harming herself or anyone else? Had she received treatment for any mental-health condition that would be relevant to the current assessment?

Sela answered.

She slept badly sometimes, especially after threatening messages. She was worried about the library and her marriage, though she and Lev remained close. She did not wish to harm anyone. She had no reason to believe that a stranger controlled her thoughts or that she was being sent secret personal messages.

She believed, on the basis of published records, that a foreign government had tried to weaken civic cooperation in Liora.

"I know the documents do not prove everything," she said. "Eva's article was careful about that."

Oren nodded.

"Tell me what you think they prove."

She opened the folder and summarized the authorization, the financial connection through Harborbridge, and the limits of the internal analyst's conclusions. Oren asked where the material had come from. Sela named the publication and the outside research organization. She did not claim to possess secret evidence unavailable to anyone else.

"What would make you change your mind?" he asked.

"About which part?"

"The northern department."

"If the documents were shown to be forgeries, I'd have to reconsider. But the independent checks and matching records would need explaining."

"And about whether the program caused the difficulties in your neighborhood?"

"I don't know how much it caused. I never said I did."

Oren looked down at the referral.

The municipal summary described her as incapable of revising a fixed belief.

Here was a woman articulating uncertainty, differentiating evidence from inference, and naming circumstances in which she would revise her conclusion.

"Why do you keep recording who comes to the library?" he asked.

Sela smiled sadly.

"Because I used to teach history. Because I like the people who come there. Because if the reading group stops meeting, I want to know whether it can be brought back."

It was the longest answer she had given.

Then she added:

"Mrs. Kade taught half the town to read. We buried her in May. Everyone came to her funeral. For one day we remembered how to talk."

Her voice broke.

Oren waited.

She wiped her face and continued.

"I think about that day because it tells me we still know how."

There was no clinical basis in the conversation for declaring her incapable of recognizing reality.

Oren closed the folder.

"I have enough for my opinion," he said.

***

His report was brief.

It recorded that Sela had attended voluntarily and cooperated. It stated that she understood the difference between documented facts and unresolved hypotheses, showed no evidence in the interview of a psychotic disorder, and had expressed no intent to harm anyone. It acknowledged that a single assessment could not exclude every possible condition, as no honest clinical encounter could.

Then Oren wrote the conclusion.

**Political dissent, distrust of public institutions, and the citation of verifiable records are not psychiatric symptoms in themselves. The available information provides no clinical justification for involuntary psychiatric detention.**

He signed it.

The signature took less than a second.

He had spent the entire morning reaching the conclusion.

***

The emergency coordination office called an hour later.

An administrator asked whether he had considered the history of escalating behavior.

"Yes."

"Did you examine the municipal risk summary?"

"Yes."

"Then perhaps you don't understand the public-order context."

"Public order is not a psychiatric diagnosis."

The administrator's voice remained polite.

"We're asking for clinical expertise."

"I gave it."

"Your report could leave the municipal authority exposed if the subject continues to cause disruption."

"Then the authority should address disruption through lawful, proportionate means. It should not ask me to invent an illness."

The call ended.

Oren looked at the clock. It ran four minutes fast, as it always had. He had once refused to correct it because he believed a physician should arrive early rather than ask patients to wait.

That evening he received notice that his role on the Public Disorder Act assessment panel was being reviewed.

Two days later he was removed from the panel pending a professional standards inquiry into his refusal to consider all available risk information.

His medical license was not revoked. He continued treating patients on his regular ward for the time being.

The letter said the decision was administrative and did not imply misconduct.

His colleagues understood what it meant.

***

Sela and Lev went home after the interview.

They bought bread on the way.

The bakery was quieter than before. The upstairs room where Common Future used to meet had been closed for months, and the baker had finally repaired the wiring. A small sign said the space was now available for private hire.

"Maybe we could have the supper there," Sela said.

Lev looked at her.

"What supper?"

"The street supper. If people won't come to Rowan Way, perhaps they'll come somewhere else."

Lev laughed gently.

"You're impossible."

"I've been called worse."

She bought a loaf and a packet of biscuits. They walked home in cold sunshine.

For two evenings they believed the matter was over.

Sela put the legal letter in a drawer.

She returned to work at the library. She helped a child find a book about bridges, showed an elderly man how to use the online train timetable, and repaired a torn cover with adhesive tape.

On Friday a municipal official called to tell her the hospital assessment had not resolved the public-order referral.

"But the doctor said there was no basis for detention," Sela replied.

"That was one opinion."

"He's a psychiatrist."

"The review remains open."

"On what grounds?"

The official said she would receive further notice.

Sela called Aron Vale.

He was not surprised.

"I'll request the full record," he said.

"Can they simply ignore a medical opinion?"

"They shouldn't. That doesn't mean they won't attempt another route."

She looked toward the library shelves.

"How is this lawful?"

"That is the question we're going to put in writing."

***

The second opinion came from Dr. Marek Voss.

He was a consultant psychiatrist contracted to provide emergency assessments when local services lacked capacity. He had worked in hospitals for years and had treated many patients with care. He was not a cartoon villain. He was a man accustomed to short appointments, incomplete records, and institutions that rewarded quick closure.

In recent months his workload had increased. He had been praised for resolving difficult referrals promptly. He had come to believe that his role was to identify risk efficiently and leave disagreements about public policy to other authorities.

He reviewed the municipal file before speaking to Sela.

The file now included Oren's report, but the conclusion had been placed after several pages of administrative concerns. A covering note suggested that Oren had failed to give sufficient weight to the repeated complaints and the subject's refusal to accept official reassurance.

Voss read the note first.

When Sela appeared for the follow-up interview, accompanied by Lev and Aron, he asked why she believed her town was being targeted.

"I don't believe we were individually targeted," she said. "I believe a northern department tried to weaken civic trust. There are documents."

"Why does this concern you so intensely?"

"Because it happened where I live."

"Have you considered that continuing to raise the matter may be harming your community?"

"Of course. I also think hiding the documents would be wrong."

"Do you accept the authority of the municipal board?"

"To administer the library, within the law. Not to decide which documented facts I'm allowed to discuss."

He made a note.

Aron asked to see what had been recorded.

Voss said the report would be available through the normal process.

Sela offered the newspaper and the records again. Voss glanced at the first page.

"These are political materials," he said.

"They're the basis of the claim in your referral," Aron answered.

Voss looked at him.

"My task is not to determine foreign policy."

"No," Aron said. "Your task is to determine whether there is a clinical basis for the decision the authority is seeking."

The doctor did not reply.

He was under time pressure, and the administrative office had already told him that the case was considered high priority. He had not been asked to decide whether Sela was a political opponent, but the file made her disagreements with officials impossible to separate from the description of her mental state.

He should have separated them anyway.

***

That night Voss prepared his opinion.

He did not write that Sela was violent; she had made no threat.

He did not write that she was unable to care for herself; there was no evidence of that.

Instead he described a pattern of persistent preoccupation with perceived institutional hostility, limited acceptance of official reassurance, and behavior that might impair her ability to comply with emergency public-order directions.

The language was cautious enough that he could later defend individual phrases as observations.

The conclusion was not cautious.

He recommended temporary secure evaluation under the Public Disorder Act, citing a need to clarify risk in a controlled environment.

Oren had refused to translate dissent into illness.

Voss signed a document that allowed the authorities to do it without stating the transformation plainly.

His pen moved across the paper.

He went home.

He slept.

***

Lev read the second report at the kitchen table.

She worked in medicine. She knew what a rushed assessment looked like. She knew how often clinicians had to make decisions with incomplete information. She also knew that the report's central concern was Sela's refusal to stop referring to a documented foreign operation.

"There's no clinical basis," she said.

Aron nodded.

"We're appealing."

"Before they do anything?"

"We'll request urgent review."

"Can they hold her meanwhile?"

He hesitated.

"Under the temporary provisions, they may try."

Sela was sitting beside them.

She had not spoken for several minutes.

Lev turned toward her.

"We'll fight this."

"I know."

"Don't say that as if it's already happened."

Sela looked at the notebook on the shelf.

"I keep thinking I should have done more."

"More what?"

"Written less. Spoken better. Made them understand."

Lev took her hands.

"You answered every question. You showed them the documents. You didn't do anything wrong."

The words did not make the danger smaller. They gave Sela something to hold while the danger remained.

Outside, Rowan Way was quiet. Someone had left a snow shovel beside the wall, though there had been no snow for days.

Sela remembered the first summer supper, the long table and the lights in the windows. She remembered asking whether people would come if she invited them.

She had believed that a town was something people made by showing up.

Now she was waiting for a decision about whether she would be permitted to remain in it.

***

Oren received a copy of the second opinion through the review process.

He read it once, then again.

He could see the shape of the error. Not a single spectacular falsehood, but a series of small substitutions: disagreement described as rigidity, justified worry described as preoccupation, refusal to accept an official account described as inability to accept reality.

A patient might genuinely experience distress during public conflict. Distress deserved care.

It did not authorize an institution to punish the patient for the contents of a political belief.

He wrote a formal objection and sent it to the hospital's medical director and the independent patient-rights office.

The medical director called him.

"Oren, this is becoming difficult."

"It should be difficult."

"Your position is under review."

"Then review it."

"Please think about your patients."

"I am."

He ended the call.

Then he went to the ward, where the man who had once believed his brother stood in the doorway was waiting to discuss the progress of his treatment.

They talked about sleep, fear, and the small improvements of the previous week. The man said the doorway still frightened him sometimes, but that he was beginning to feel more secure.

Oren listened.

The contrast was almost unbearable.

Here was a person with a genuine clinical problem receiving care, and a librarian with verifiable documents being sent toward detention under the language of care.

He returned to his office and placed the second opinion beside his own.

Two signatures.

One medical profession.

Two entirely different uses of its authority.

The following morning Sela's case was transferred to a regional facility.

The notice described the move as protective.

It did not say from whom.

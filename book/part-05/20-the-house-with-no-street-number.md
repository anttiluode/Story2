# Chapter Twenty — The House With No Street Number

*The Eastmere Regional Evaluation Centre. February.*

There was no street number on the gate.

Lev had checked the address three times before leaving Merrow. The transfer notice named the Eastmere Regional Evaluation Centre and gave a road, a district, and a postal code. It did not identify the entrance. The navigation system directed her to a closed service road behind a grain warehouse, where a man in a reflective jacket told her she had taken the wrong turn.

"People do that all the time," he said.

"Which way should I go?"

He pointed toward a narrow lane beside the old waterworks.

"There's a gate. Doesn't have a number. You'll know it."

Lev drove through February rain, past a football pitch with broken fencing and a stand of trees whose branches reached over the road. The buildings appeared behind high hedges: low, pale, anonymous, the kind of place that could have been a school, a warehouse, or a government records office.

The gate was blue.

A sign said **REGIONAL CARE SERVICES**.

There was no address.

At reception a woman behind glass asked for identification, then explained that Lev's name had not yet been added to the visitor list.

"I'm her wife," Lev said.

"I understand."

"She's been here three days."

"I understand."

"Then let me see her."

The receptionist looked tired. She was not responsible for the transfer, the law, or the entries in the computer system. She called someone, waited, called again, and finally said that the visit could be authorized for twenty minutes.

Lev thanked her because she had helped.

Then she sat on a plastic chair and tried not to imagine Sela sitting somewhere else in the same building, waiting for a door to open.

***

The transfer had happened early on Monday.

Two officials had arrived at Rowan Way with an order. One wore ordinary clothes and carried a folder. The other wore the dark jacket of a regional escort service. They did not shout. They did not handcuff Sela in the kitchen. They explained that she was required to attend a temporary secure evaluation and that the order authorized transport even if she did not consent.

Aron Vale arrived before they left.

He read the order, asked for the authorizing decision, and recorded his objection. He told Sela that the urgent review request had been filed, and that he would challenge the detention.

"Am I allowed to take my notebook?" she asked.

The official said personal materials would be screened at the facility.

"Can I take a book?"

"Within the facility rules."

Sela went upstairs to pack.

Lev followed her.

They stood in their bedroom, where the sheets were warm from the night before. The room was full of ordinary things: a half-used tube of hand cream, a receipt tucked beneath a lamp, a blue sweater hanging from the back of a chair.

Sela opened the wardrobe and reached for the sweater.

"Take the green one," Lev said. "It's warmer."

Sela looked at her.

"That's what you're worried about?"

"No."

Lev began to cry.

She had held herself together through interviews, letters, appeals, and the fear of losing her wife's freedom. Now the choice of a sweater broke the last of her composure.

Sela put down the blue one.

"All right," she said.

They packed a small bag.

At the front door, Sela paused beside the coat hook.

Mrs. Kade had given them the hook years earlier, saying every proper household required a place where people could stop pretending they were visitors. It was crooked and had been repaired twice.

Sela took her coat from it.

"Keep the door locked," she told Lev.

Lev almost laughed.

That was what Sela always said when she left for the library.

"I will."

They kissed.

Then Sela went with the officials.

The car drove out of Rowan Way without lights or sirens.

At the end of the street, Ivo Arden was leaving for school. He saw the vehicle but did not recognize the woman sitting in the back.

He would learn about it at lunchtime.

***

Eastmere had once been a rehabilitation center for people recovering after severe injuries.

Its newer wing served patients who genuinely needed psychiatric assessment and care. Some had experienced frightening illnesses or crises; others were recovering with the help of staff who worked long shifts and knew their patients well.

The regional government had designated a smaller wing for people referred under the Public Disorder Act.

The wings shared a building and some services.

Their purposes were not the same.

Sela understood the distinction from the first afternoon.

A nurse named Ines showed her the dining room, the visiting area, and the rules for contacting a lawyer. Ines spoke plainly and did not treat Sela as a child.

"You can request a review of your records," she said.

"I already have a lawyer."

"Good. Tell him anything you believe is incorrect."

"May I keep my notebook?"

"It has to be checked first."

"For what?"

Ines hesitated.

"Personal information, material the facility considers unsafe, things like that."

"It's a list of people who used to come to a reading group."

"Then I hope you get it back."

Sela did not.

The notebook was placed in a property bag with her phone, keys, and several letters. A receipt described its contents as **assorted written materials**.

Sela asked for a more precise description.

The staff member said she could submit a correction request.

She did.

Three days later the request was acknowledged.

No correction appeared in the inventory.

***

The first night she slept badly.

There were lights beneath the door and footsteps in the corridor. A woman in a nearby room was crying after a difficult telephone call. Somewhere a trolley rattled across the floor. Sela knew these were ordinary hospital sounds, yet each arrived as proof that she was not at home.

She had no clock.

In the morning she asked what time it was.

"Six thirty," a nurse said.

"Could I have paper?"

"Of course."

She was given three sheets and a pencil.

On the first she wrote the date.

On the second she wrote everything she remembered about the transfer order.

On the third she began a list.

**Things that are true even if nobody here believes them.**

She wrote that Mrs. Kade had died in May. That Lev was her wife. That the library had held public records. That the *Chronicle* had published documents showing a covert northern influence program. That she had not threatened anyone.

Then she stopped.

It frightened her to feel compelled to write those facts down.

She had spent years teaching history and maintaining records because people forgot things. Now she was writing to reassure herself that memory still belonged to her.

She folded the pages and put them beneath her pillow.

***

The first formal review was conducted by a panel that included an administrator, a clinician, and an appointed legal officer.

Sela's lawyer joined remotely after a delay caused by a scheduling failure. The hearing lasted less than an hour.

The administrator asked whether Sela accepted that her repeated public statements had caused concern in Merrow.

"Concern to whom?" Aron asked.

"To members of the community and public officials."

Sela answered that she knew people had complained. She disputed that disagreement with a published report or with a municipal decision amounted to a clinical risk.

The panel asked whether she would agree to stop discussing the northern program while her case remained under review.

Aron objected.

"Is that a medical recommendation, a legal condition, or a request that she refrain from lawful speech?"

The administrator said it was a question designed to assess cooperation.

Sela looked toward the screen.

"I can agree not to harass anyone," she said. "I already don't. I can agree to keep records private when they contain personal information. I can't agree to say I don't know what the published documents show."

A note was made.

Later, when she obtained a copy of the hearing summary, she saw that her response had been condensed to:

**Subject unwilling to modify public claims despite ongoing community concern.**

She asked for the full transcript.

There wasn't one.

The panel extended the evaluation pending a further risk review.

The decision said that no final diagnosis had been made.

It did not explain why the absence of a diagnosis did not weigh more heavily in favor of release.

***

Lev's first visit lasted sixteen minutes.

A member of staff brought Sela into the room. She wore the green sweater and looked exhausted but otherwise like herself. Her hair was tied back with a plain band, and she was carrying the pencil and three sheets of paper.

Lev stood so quickly her chair scraped the floor.

They embraced.

The staff member looked away.

"Are they treating you well?" Lev asked.

"Most of the staff are kind."

"Are you getting your things?"

"No. They say they're reviewing them."

"I've brought the book."

"Which one?"

"The one you were reading."

Lev showed her the cover. It was a history of ordinary life in Liora, a book of old photographs and household objects. Sela had been reading it before the transfer.

The staff member said the book would need to be checked.

Sela nodded as though this were a small inconvenience rather than another fragment of life held at a counter.

"How's the house?" she asked.

"Quiet."

"The library?"

"I haven't been."

"The flowers by the window?"

"Alive."

Sela smiled.

"Good."

Lev wanted to tell her about the appeals. Aron had requested an urgent judicial review; the court had asked the government for a response. The hospital's medical director had been contacted. Oren Pell had filed a formal objection. The case was not forgotten.

She told Sela all of it.

Sela listened carefully.

"Do you think they'll let me out?"

Lev looked into her face.

"Yes," she said.

She believed release was right and had reason to hope the appeal would succeed. She could not know when.

Sela heard the uncertainty beneath the answer and did not challenge it.

Instead she asked Lev to remember to pay the electricity bill.

Lev laughed and cried at the same time.

***

At the Arden house, Ivo learned where Sela had gone.

Mara told him because she believed he deserved an explanation. She said Sela had been taken for an evaluation under the emergency law and that Lev and a lawyer were trying to bring her home.

"Is she ill?" Ivo asked.

"We don't know of a reason to think so," Mara said.

"Then why is she in a hospital?"

"Because the authorities made a decision that people are challenging."

He looked at Tomas.

"Can they do that?"

"Not just because they disagree with her," Tomas said.

"But they did."

Tomas stared at the table.

"That's what the lawyers are trying to establish."

Ivo was angry in a way his parents had rarely seen. His childhood had been full of adults explaining why things couldn't be done. Here was a thing that had been done which every adult he trusted seemed to think was wrong.

"She's the one who kept the books," he said.

"Yes."

"She taught me how to find the old town maps."

"Yes."

"She helped us with the football club."

Mara nodded.

Ivo looked toward the window. Across the street Sela's curtains were drawn. The house was still there, warm and tidy, with a little tree beside the front door.

"Then go get her," he said.

Tomas rose from the table and went to the kitchen sink.

He was ashamed of himself for having no answer that a boy could use.

***

Other cases began appearing in the legal aid office.

Not all involved the same circumstances. Some concerned people with genuine psychiatric needs who were being poorly served by the emergency system. Others involved demonstrators accused of violence who had legal charges pending. A small number resembled Sela's case: citizens who had persisted in challenging official accounts and whose refusal to withdraw their claims had been characterized as a possible medical problem.

A municipal records officer had alleged irregularities in the handling of an election-related contract. A preliminary audit later confirmed that several documents had been misfiled. The officer nevertheless faced an emergency risk review after repeatedly appearing at council meetings and refusing to stop asking questions.

A school administrator had publicly corrected a false account of the Civic Day disorder. She was suspended after complaints that her statements had inflamed tensions. A referral for welfare assessment followed when she refused to retract her correction.

Aron Vale represented one of the cases and consulted colleagues about the others.

He was careful not to claim every referral was identical or every person was innocent of every alleged act. Each required evidence and a fair process. But the recurring feature disturbed him: an institution's inability to answer a factual challenge was appearing inside medical or safety paperwork as a reason to question the challenger.

He tried to arrange a joint legal review.

The lawyers agreed on the importance of the cases.

They could not agree on a public statement.

Some feared that association with one political movement would make the other movement abandon the clients. Others feared their own funding would be withdrawn. A few believed the cases were being exaggerated for partisan gain.

They issued separate statements instead.

The irony was not lost on Aron.

It did not help him get Sela released.

***

At Eastmere, Sela befriended a woman named Lina who was recovering from a severe depressive episode.

Lina had come to the hospital voluntarily. She had been frightened of being alone after a period of illness, and she wanted treatment. She was getting it, although staffing shortages meant she sometimes waited longer than she should.

One afternoon they sat beside a window watching rain move across the courtyard.

"Why are you here?" Lina asked.

Sela considered how to answer.

"An authority thinks I need an evaluation."

"Do you?"

"I agreed to speak to doctors. One of them said there was no reason to hold me. Another recommended further assessment."

Lina nodded.

"Doctors disagree sometimes."

"They do."

"Is one of them wrong?"

"I think the one who recommended detention is wrong about my beliefs."

Lina looked toward the window.

"People were wrong about me for years," she said. "They thought I was lazy."

Sela waited.

"I was ill," Lina continued. "I needed help. I wish somebody had believed that sooner."

Sela reached for her hand.

"I believe you."

They sat together.

There was no contradiction between Lina deserving treatment and Sela deserving freedom. Both had been denied the simple dignity of being understood on their own evidence.

In the evening Lina lent Sela a book of poems from the ward library.

Sela read it slowly because there was nothing else she could control about the length of the day.

***

The district court held a review of Sela's detention after repeated requests.

The judge considered the emergency law, the clinical reports, and the administrative record. Oren Pell's objection was included. So was the second opinion recommending continued evaluation.

Aron argued that the state had not identified a medical emergency or a specific risk justifying confinement. He showed the public records Sela had cited and explained that the referral's central factual premise was false.

The government lawyer did not deny that the northern program had been documented. Instead she argued that the court was reviewing a temporary administrative risk decision, not the truth of a foreign intelligence allegation.

The judge expressed concern about the lack of clear clinical grounds.

She ordered further information from the regional authority and directed that the detention be reconsidered within a defined period.

She did not order immediate release.

The decision was reported as a partial victory for Sela.

At Eastmere it meant another week, then another.

***

Lev stood outside the blue gate on the day the review was announced.

A reporter had asked whether she felt vindicated.

Lev looked at the camera.

"My wife was taken from our home because she would not agree to deny public evidence," she said. "A doctor examined her and found no clinical basis for detention. She is still inside."

"Do you believe the government has acted maliciously?"

"I believe people with power have made decisions they should have to justify."

"Are you saying the whole medical system is corrupt?"

"No. I work in medicine. I see people doing difficult, necessary work every day. This is why the abuse is so serious. It uses the authority of care to do something care is not for."

She stepped away from the camera.

The clip that appeared online contained the sentence about decisions and justification.

One account used it to accuse the government of imprisoning opponents. Another called her a doctor spreading panic about the care system.

Neither showed her full answer.

She had begun to understand why Eva Maren looked tired all the time.

***

That night Sela asked Ines whether the window in the corridor could be opened.

"No," Ines said. "Building policy."

"Does anyone ever open it?"

"Maintenance, sometimes."

"It smells like rain outside."

"It does."

Ines stood with her for a moment.

"You have a visitor tomorrow."

"Lev?"

"Yes."

Sela smiled.

When the nurse had gone, she returned to her room and unfolded the three sheets of paper. They were creased and covered with small handwriting.

She had begun a second list beneath the first.

**People who have been kind to me here.**

Ines. Lina. The porter who found an extra blanket. A kitchen worker who remembered that Sela disliked sugar in her tea.

The list mattered.

It did not make the detention lawful.

She thought of the old neighborhood supper and the way people had once placed chairs outside without asking who deserved them.

Then she looked at the door.

It was closed.

For the first time in her life she could not decide when she was allowed to go home.

***

The facility's official name was printed on every page of her record.

**Eastmere Regional Evaluation Centre.**

In the section labeled **Reason for Continued Placement**, the latest entry read:

**Persistent externalizing narrative; limited insight into destabilizing effects of public statements; further structured observation indicated.**

The file did not say that the northern department had existed.

It did not say that the documents were authentic.

It did not say that the municipality had never identified a threat made by Sela.

It described the evidence she cited as a *narrative*.

On the first page of the appeal, her lawyer had written:

**The facts remain facts even when their repetition is inconvenient.**

Those two documents lived in the same building.

Only one could open the door.

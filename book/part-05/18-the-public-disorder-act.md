# Chapter Eighteen — The Public Disorder Act

*Liora. The autumn after the disclosure.*

The man bleeding on the clinic floor had come to have his blood pressure checked.

His name was Ansel Dorr. He was sixty-eight, had worked most of his life repairing lifts, and had not attended a political demonstration in forty years. He had been sitting outside the examination room when an argument began at reception.

A visitor was demanding access to a patient's medical record. The receptionist explained that she could not release it. He said the refusal was proof that the clinic concealed evidence of political discrimination. Two people with him began filming.

Ansel stood up to ask them to stop shouting.

One of the visitors pushed past him. Ansel lost his balance, struck a chair, and fell.

There was no great explosion of violence. A cup spilled. Somebody called for help. A young nurse pressed a towel against Ansel's forehead while Lev Orin examined him. The people filming continued for several seconds before realizing what had happened.

Ansel needed seven stitches.

The police took statements. The man who had pushed past him was questioned. A prosecutor later brought a charge based on the circumstances of the fall.

The incident was real. The injury was real.

That evening, however, the photographs of the blood on the tiles circulated with four mutually incompatible explanations. One said a Renewal mob had attacked the clinic. Another blamed an anti-Renewal agitator. A third alleged that staff had injured a patient and invented a protest. A fourth claimed the photographs were old.

Lev watched them pass across a colleague's phone.

"We have the whole incident on our cameras," the receptionist said.

"Then release it," somebody replied.

"We can't release patients' images."

"Of course you can't."

The camera footage, which was preserved for the investigation, was treated as proof of concealment because it was not distributed to strangers.

Lev went to the treatment room. Ansel was sitting up with a dressing over his eyebrow.

"Am I allowed to go home?" he asked.

"Yes. Someone will explain the aftercare."

"Good. I've got a dog."

He looked at the blood on his sleeve.

"All I wanted was for them to leave the girl at the desk alone."

Lev took his hand.

"I know."

She could not tell him that by morning his injury would be cited in parliament by people who had never met him.

***

The Public Disorder Act began as a response to violence that nobody needed to invent.

Over the preceding months, election officials had been threatened, clinics had been harassed, and public meetings had ended in fights. A magistrate had received an envelope containing a photograph of her children. A volunteer delivering food to elderly residents had been assaulted after being mistaken for an organizer of a political rally.

The police needed resources. Prosecutors needed time. Several local agencies needed clearer procedures for dealing with threats.

The government's first bill contained ordinary provisions that might have helped.

Then the Ministry of Interior added sections on coordinated disorder, interference with essential services, and the temporary assessment of people alleged to pose a continuing danger to public safety.

The definitions were broad.

A committee lawyer wrote that no provision should permit detention merely because a person distrusted the government, held an unpopular belief, or made an allegation that officials considered embarrassing.

The Justice Minister agreed in public.

In private, senior officials argued about people they called *persistent destabilizers*: citizens who repeatedly challenged public explanations, refused to withdraw accusations, or brought documents to meetings where the subject was supposed to be something else.

Not all of those people were acting responsibly. Some had threatened staff or harassed families. Some were simply rude.

Others had evidence.

The draft used a single administrative phrase for all three kinds of conduct.

***

Prime Minister Nera Valen read the bill at her kitchen table.

She had been a labor lawyer before entering politics. In her office she could speak for an hour about constitutional standards, administrative appeal, and the distinction between suspected wrongdoing and proof. At home she read the proposed powers through the eyes of someone who remembered defending workers against decisions made by people who were absolutely certain they were protecting the public.

Her husband put a cup of tea beside the papers.

"Are you going to pass it?" he asked.

"I don't know if I have the votes."

"That wasn't what I asked."

She looked up.

He had known her long enough to recognize the difference between a political answer and an honest one.

"I think we need parts of it," she said.

"And the rest?"

"Some of it needs rewriting."

"Then rewrite it."

"We don't have unlimited time."

Ansel Dorr's photograph had appeared in every national bulletin. The Interior Minister wanted the bill introduced before the next demonstrations. Two members of her coalition had threatened to withdraw support unless the government acted. The opposition was demanding resignations over failures to protect the clinics.

Valen was being pressed from every direction by people with real reasons to want safety.

She was also becoming frightened of losing the government.

She had spent the election telling the country that the rule of law mattered. Now the survival of her administration depended on a law whose outer limits she could not defend.

She marked the medical-assessment section with a pencil.

**Clarify the threshold. Require independent clinical judgment.**

Then she went to bed.

When the final draft returned, those words had been acknowledged in a covering letter but not resolved in the text.

***

At the *Merrow Chronicle*, Eva Maren began reading the bill with Samuel Rees.

The newspaper had printed the northern-file investigation and survived, though not comfortably. The circulation had increased for a brief period. Advertisers had returned slowly. Legal fees had not disappeared. The second floor remained empty.

Samuel took out a ruler and placed it across the proposed law.

"Read that sentence again."

Eva read it aloud.

The law permitted short-term protective detention in designated circumstances, subject to an administrative risk assessment and subsequent review, where conduct was judged to present an ongoing threat to civic stability or essential public services.

"Which conduct?" Samuel asked.

"That's what I want to know."

"Threatening a nurse?"

"Potentially."

"Publishing documents that officials say frighten people?"

"The wording doesn't exclude it."

He looked at the next paragraph.

"And why is medical assessment here?"

"The ministry says it's to distinguish people in crisis from people acting deliberately."

"Does it say how?"

"Not in a way a court could test easily."

Samuel folded the draft.

He had spent decades covering legislation. Bills were often messy, and many frightening provisions were narrowed before they became law. He did not believe every government proposal was a plan for tyranny.

But he recognized a familiar administrative danger: a procedure designed to solve several different problems without admitting they were different.

"Find somebody who works in psychiatry," he said. "Not somebody who wants a television argument. Somebody who knows what an assessment actually means."

Eva wrote the instruction down.

***

Dr. Oren Pell worked at Merrow District Hospital.

He was fifty-three, broad-shouldered, meticulous, and inclined to wear the same wool jacket until its cuffs began to shine. His office contained a crooked houseplant, a clock that ran four minutes fast, and a bookshelf with more novels than medical texts.

He had trained in psychiatry because, as he once told a student, medicine became dishonest when it pretended that people's inner lives could be reduced to a blood test. He understood both the reality of severe mental illness and the danger of using psychiatric vocabulary as a moral judgment.

He was not a saint.

He could be abrupt with colleagues. He disliked administrative meetings and sometimes dismissed problems that required more patience than he possessed. He had once apologized to a patient three days late because his pride got in the way.

But he was exact about clinical records.

Eva met him in the hospital café.

"Can an unpopular belief be evidence of a psychiatric disorder?" she asked.

"Not by itself."

"What if officials think the belief is harmful?"

"Still not by itself."

"What if a person insists on something most people reject?"

"People insist on all kinds of things. The task is to assess the person and the evidence, not to take a vote."

She showed him the section of the bill.

He read it twice.

"This creates an awful temptation," he said.

"For whom?"

"For everyone who wants a difficult person to become somebody else's responsibility."

He was silent for a moment.

"Psychiatric wards are already full of people who need help. Some are acutely ill. Some are frightened. Some have nowhere else to go. Those are clinical problems. If this becomes a convenient place to put people who embarrass the state, the patients suffer too."

"Could doctors refuse?"

"They should refuse any unsupported clinical conclusion."

"Would the law protect them?"

He looked at the draft.

"I don't see a clear answer."

***

In Rowan Way, Sela Orin read Eva's interview in the *Chronicle*.

The headline was modest: **Doctors Question Medical Clause in Public Disorder Bill**.

Oren had been quoted accurately. He explained why psychiatric evaluation could not substitute for legal proof, and why dissent did not constitute an illness. The newspaper described legitimate uses of emergency mental-health care and the safeguards that should protect patients.

Sela cut the article out and placed it in her notebook.

Lev found her doing it.

"You're keeping everything," Lev said.

"Only what we might need."

"That's becoming a lot of things."

Sela looked at the pages spread across the table: attendance lists, public financial records, the ministerial file reported by Eva, printed corrections, committee minutes, and the new bill.

"I don't want anybody to say we didn't have the evidence."

Lev sat down.

"I believe you."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Sela glanced at her.

Lev was tired. Her clinic had lost staff, her colleagues were being threatened, and she had spent the day helping an elderly patient whose treatment had been delayed by a dispute about transport. She had no strength left for an argument about whether she loved her wife enough to understand her work.

Sela put down the scissors.

"Yes," she said. "I do."

Lev reached across the table.

"Then can we have supper without a document between us?"

They ate in the kitchen.

For an hour Sela did not open the notebook.

***

The parliamentary debate lasted two nights.

Renewal demanded stronger safeguards for political speech and criticized the government for the law's vague definitions. Some of its members nevertheless wanted broad powers to remove people they described as violent agitators. Common Future defended protections for essential services and argued that the state had waited too long to respond to harassment. Some of its members warned that the government was creating a tool any future administration could use against them.

The smaller coalition parties tried to narrow the detention provisions.

An amendment would have required a judicial order before any detention unrelated to an immediate medical emergency. It failed by a handful of votes after ministers said the courts could not respond quickly enough to every crisis.

Another amendment made the temporary review process more explicit. It passed.

A third required a public report on detentions every six months. It passed too, but the report could omit names and case details on grounds of privacy and security.

The final law contained safeguards. It also contained exceptions large enough to swallow them.

Valen voted for it.

When the result was announced, the governing benches rose in applause. Several opposition members joined them. Others stood in silent protest.

Outside parliament, protesters held signs calling the bill either a necessary defense of democracy or the beginning of a police state.

Both groups contained people who wanted the country to be safe.

Some had begun to forget that safety was not a thing a government could promise by removing everyone who made it uncomfortable.

***

The first detentions involved people accused of threats and violence.

Some were later charged with ordinary crimes. Others were released after review. Civil rights lawyers challenged the use of medical assessments in several cases, and the courts issued conflicting early rulings while the new law was being interpreted.

The system did not become abusive all at once.

That made it easier to defend.

When an administrator made a questionable decision, officials pointed to the many cases where intervention had prevented harm. When a doctor refused an assessment, someone else was asked to review the file. When a person appealed, the appeal went into a queue already too long for the promised deadline to feel meaningful.

The number of people affected was still small compared with the population.

That made it easier for everyone else to call the problem exceptional.

***

Sela spoke at a municipal library board meeting in November.

She had been asked to explain why several public records concerning foreign financing and the Civic Weather disclosure were being requested so frequently. The board wanted a consistent process for allowing access to documents that attracted protests.

She arrived with a folder of published records and a short written proposal.

"These are public documents," she said. "We can keep them available without endorsing every interpretation people make of them."

A board member asked whether her continued attention to the northern program was itself contributing to fear.

"I hope not," she said. "I try to distinguish what the records show from what they don't."

"Do you believe the operation is still active?"

"I don't know its current scope."

"Then why do you keep referring to it?"

"Because the documented program existed, and people have asked to examine the evidence."

A second member said that residents were beginning to regard the library as a center of political agitation.

"Residents come here to read," Sela said.

"Some are frightened by what you're presenting."

"Then we can help them read the material carefully."

The chairman sighed.

"Sela, you're making this difficult."

She looked at the people around the table.

"I thought it was supposed to be difficult."

The minutes later recorded that she had repeatedly rejected the board's concerns about civic stability.

They did not record her proposal in full.

The board postponed a decision.

The next morning an anonymous complaint alleged that Sela was encouraging distrust of public institutions through obsessive and destabilizing claims about foreign influence.

The complaint quoted lines from the *Chronicle* without naming the newspaper. It also quoted a sentence Sela had never said.

She asked to see the source.

The municipal office declined to identify the complainant.

***

In December, the Interior Ministry issued guidance on applying the Public Disorder Act.

The document emphasized that dissent was lawful and that political disagreement alone could not justify detention. It also encouraged agencies to refer persistent, escalating conduct for multidisciplinary review when officials believed ordinary administrative measures were insufficient.

The contradiction was not apparent in the preface.

It appeared in the way the same words could mean different things to different readers.

A clinician might see a patient who needed help.

A public official might see a critic who would not go away.

A frightened administrator might see an opportunity to transfer responsibility.

The law had created a common doorway for all three.

***

Eva called Oren Pell again.

"Have you been asked to do assessments under the new act?"

"Yes."

"How many?"

"I can't discuss patients or cases."

"Are you worried?"

He looked at the crooked houseplant on his desk.

"Yes."

"Can you say why?"

"Because I have begun receiving requests that describe a person's disagreement with officials in more detail than any symptoms."

Eva waited.

"That's not a diagnosis," he said.

"Will you put that on the record?"

He was quiet.

He had a job. The hospital depended on his work. There were patients he had seen for years who needed continuity of care. He did not want to become a symbol in a political fight.

Then he thought of the bill that had become law, and of the patient who would eventually sit before him carrying a file assembled by strangers.

"Yes," he said.

***

At Rowan Way, the winter holidays approached.

Mara and Tomas had managed several evenings without arguing. They still slept in separate rooms, and neither had proposed changing the arrangement. Ivo was taller and quieter. He had begun spending more afternoons with Sami working on a generator that could light a bulb by hand.

Dain sent Ivo a message asking whether he wanted to go fishing when the river thawed.

Ivo replied: **Maybe Dad could come too.**

Dain read it, then left the phone on the table.

He had not learned how to answer without making a promise he might fail to keep.

At the Orins' house, Sela wrapped a book for Lev and placed it beneath a small tree. Lev wrapped a wool scarf for Sela.

They still believed they would spend the winter together.

Sela's notebook rested on the kitchen shelf.

Beside it lay the newspaper clipping of Dr. Oren Pell saying that a belief was not an illness merely because officials disliked it.

In the municipal office, her name had been entered into a list of unresolved public-order referrals.

No one had told her.

The entry did not say she had committed a crime.

It said her case required assessment.

For the first time in her life, an ordinary word had begun to threaten the shape of her future.

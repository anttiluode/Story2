THE CENTRAL STORY

How a country disappears without a conventional invasion

The Distance Between Houses is a six-part political thriller set in the fictional Republic of Liora, a southern democracy, and the authoritarian Northern Directorate of Veyr. Veyr is already waging war in neighboring Tarven and elsewhere. Liora opposes that aggression and supports its victims. Rather than attack Liora directly, Veyr launches a covert program to undermine the relationships that allow its people to disagree and still govern themselves.

The operation is administered by the Ministry of Continuity's Department of Civic Weather, led by psychologist Dr. Irena Solvik, overseen by Director Kest, and examined with increasing alarm by analyst Niko Rahl. They study not merely elections or opinions but a society's capacity to cooperate across disagreement: who attends neighborhood meals, trusts neighbors, volunteers, or shares institutions with political opponents.

They do not control every platform, create every grievance, or determine every individual's choices. They fund and exploit existing conflicts, commercial incentives, opportunists and civic weaknesses. In Liora, ordinary people continue making real decisions, some generous and some damaging. In Veyr, official success reports increasingly claim more control than the evidence can support.

Both the human cost and the bureaucratic reward unfold side by side. The story begins with six shared folding tables on a residential street. Six years later Liora has ceased to exist as a sovereign republic; the officials who helped weaken it receive medals; one woman who documented the operation remains confined in a psychiatric facility.

PART I · CHAPTERS 01–05

The Summer of Open Doors

On Rowan Way in the city of Merrow, neighbors gather for their annual summer supper. They borrow food and folding tables, argue without separating into camps, help one another through a brief power failure, and look after children who move freely between houses. Mara Arden organizes youth sport; her husband Tomas maintains railway signals; Tomas's brother Dain works with municipal power systems. Their son Ivo grows up with the assumption that relatives, teachers and neighbors can be trusted, even when they are irritating.

Elsewhere, Irena receives her assignment to study and weaken Liora's civic cohesion. The contrast is immediate: she examines the same neighborhood as rows of measurable relationships, while its inhabitants know it as a place where someone brings a lantern when the lights fail. Niko worries that numbers cannot capture everything that makes the community function.

At home, a legitimate dispute about school sports and resources draws Mara toward increasingly aggressive online campaigns. A disagreement between Tomas and Dain hardens into a silence neither brother wants to repair. Librarian and former history teacher Sela Orin begins noticing what is disappearing: volunteers, reading-group members, attendance at local gatherings. Journalist Eva Maren starts looking for patterns that can be documented rather than merely suspected. The first damage is neither a dramatic riot nor a coup. It is people quietly withdrawing from one another.

PART II · CHAPTERS 06–09

The Names We Give Our Enemies

Two rival political movements, Renewal and Common Future, gain strength. Their supporters have real grievances about jobs, services, fairness and government. Yet membership starts to change the meaning of ordinary encounters: a familiar neighbor is now someone who belongs to the wrong camp. Mara discovers the intoxicating satisfaction of public recognition, while Tomas and Dain find political language to justify avoiding each other.

Sela tries to restore the annual supper. The second table is smaller, with chairs missing and people privately worrying about who else might attend. The community retains fleeting capacities for affection and practical help, but hesitation is replacing habit.

Mechanic Joren Fenn, father of Ivo's friend Tessa, starts a public commentary program called The Honest Hour. His complaints begin in genuine economic insecurity. A growing audience and outside support reward the more combustible version of his voice. His wife Mira sees that what once made him feel heard is becoming a livelihood he cannot easily risk losing.

Eva follows financial records to Harborbridge Civic Services and payments connected with organizations across opposing political lines. She can substantiate opaque funding but not yet prove the Northern Directorate is ultimately behind it. Her editor Samuel Rees understands the danger of publishing ahead of the evidence. The paper's restraint, however, cannot stop the public from turning limited findings into absolute claims.

PART III · CHAPTERS 10–13

The Feed Between Us

Polarization enters the architecture of the Arden home. Mara and Tomas remain married, but Tomas moves into a separate bedroom. They are no longer simply debating policy; each hears the other's words as evidence of allegiance. Ivo learns to tell which topics are safe and which can make a room go silent.

At school, Ivo and Tessa build a working model drawbridge together. Their engineering succeeds; their friendship does not survive the political meanings adults attach to their families. A school Civic Day collapses into factional conflict. Their classmate Sami, whose family fled the war in Tarven, offers a reminder of what war means beyond partisan performance, but the damage between the children persists.

In Veyr, Irena presents statistics showing real declines in social trust. She and her superiors blur an essential distinction: they know Liora is fragmenting, but they cannot demonstrate that their operation caused every observed change. Niko asks for more honest reporting. Instead of acknowledging uncertainty, the ministry rewards its preferred narrative. Irena accepts promotion to Deputy Commissioner for Civic Resilience.

When elderly Mrs. Kade dies of natural causes, her funeral brings neighbors together one last time. Their shared affection has not vanished. Tomas and Dain nearly reconcile, and for a moment the old Rowan Way seems possible. Yet neither brother makes the final effort. The reunion becomes a memory rather than a new beginning: the last shared Sunday.

PART IV · CHAPTERS 14–17

The Country That Hated Itself

At the national election, Garran Holt's Renewal wins the largest individual party tally, but not a majority. Nera Valen's Common Future forms a legally valid coalition government with other parties. The ballot count and constitutional process withstand formal challenges. Nevertheless, a growing share of Liorans no longer accepts that legitimate institutions can deliver a legitimate outcome when their preferred side loses.

Shared organizations shrink or close. A political meeting room above a bakery is lost. The railway struggles with staffing and coordination; clinic work becomes harder; a repair circle loses its premises; the local newspaper faces financial pressure. Tomas and Dain briefly cooperate to restore power during an actual emergency, but even a photograph of their useful work becomes raw material for factional interpretation. Practical solidarity survives in episodes, then is overwhelmed by the stories told about it.

In Veyr, Niko gathers and signs records showing that the Ministry of Continuity authorized the covert Southern Liaison Program and supported a relationship involving Harborbridge. The evidence reaches an independent research organization and then Eva, who carefully corroborates what it proves. The documents establish a deliberate effort to weaken cross-group cooperation and substantiate relevant funding links. They do not show that every domestic politician or media figure knowingly worked for Veyr, nor that the election itself was rigged.

When Eva and Samuel publish the verified investigation in the Merrow Chronicle, both major factions distribute selective fragments to accuse the other of being the true traitor. The country finally learns that an outside actor has tried to divide it, but knowledge arrives inside the very structure of distrust that actor exploited. Sela's display of evidence is removed from the library under institutional pressure; government officials begin considering new public-order powers. Even the truth no longer gives Liora a shared place to stand.

PART V · CHAPTERS 18–21

The Diagnosis

A real violent incident at a clinic increases demands for safety and order. Liora passes the Public Disorder Act, which includes dangerously ambiguous provisions for psychiatric risk assessment. Prime Minister Nera Valen recognizes troubling wording but votes for the legislation. The law is sold as a response to genuine danger; its provisions can also reach people who have committed no violence.

Sela has continued collecting public records and questioning official statements. An anonymous complaint portrays her evidence-based concerns about foreign interference as obsessive and destabilizing. Psychiatrist Dr. Oren Pell conducts a careful voluntary assessment and finds no clinical basis for detaining her. He distinguishes verifiable political beliefs from symptoms of a mental disorder and signs his refusal. For that professional judgment, he loses an emergency-panel appointment.

A second psychiatrist, Dr. Marek Voss, accepts a much more administrative framing and signs a recommendation for secure evaluation. Sela is taken from her home and held at the Eastmere Regional Evaluation Centre, behind a blue gate without a street number. In the official record, her reliance on published documents is recast as a troubling fixation; her insistence that the evidence exists is made to sound like further evidence against her.

Lev Orin, Sela's wife and a physician, struggles through visiting restrictions, assessments and appeals with lawyer Aron Vale. Sela befriends Lina, a patient with genuine psychiatric care needs, and receives decency from nurse Ines. The distinction matters: the novel does not demonize psychiatric illness or treatment. It shows state and medical authority being misused against a person for whom the record has not established grounds for involuntary confinement.

Lev refuses to retract her criticism of Sela's case and loses her supervisory role at the clinic pending review, while continuing her work and legal efforts. Oren preserves his objections and continues treating patients. The Chronicle suspends its print edition under financial and administrative strain, though its investigation remains true and its online reporting continues. Across the sea, Irena signs another sanitizing report. Sela is still inside Eastmere, and Lev still sets a place for her at home.

PART VI · CHAPTERS 22–24

The Last Country

Three years later, Liora's political system can barely act as a single state. Rival authorities dispute public services, emergency supplies and legal decisions. Northern leverage grows as domestic institutions lose the ability to coordinate. A proposed Compact of Civic Administration promises stability in exchange for profound concessions.

Parliament narrowly approves the Compact amid legal objections that the highest court cannot effectively enforce. The country's borders, currency, foreign policy and security functions pass to a northern-chaired Joint Continuity Council. Its sovereign legislature is suspended and its flag lowered. The Republic of Liora does not merely suffer a symbolic defeat: it ceases to exist as an independent country. Its territory is thereafter administered as the Southern Administrative Territory.

In Veyr, the state publicly honors those associated with the campaign. Irena, fellow psychologists Talin Vecht and Rysa Fal, and Director Kest receive medals for what official language describes as peaceful stabilization. Niko, whose concerns and records helped expose the program, is marginalized and erased from the celebratory story. Irena understands that the narrative of success conceals both the limits of her measurements and the human cost, but still does not meaningfully challenge it.

Back on Rowan Way, Ivo is now seventeen. His parents Mara and Tomas live apart; Tomas and Dain have not reconciled. Joren's former broadcasts still circulate even after he has closed the show, carrying arguments separated from their original context. Ivo must fill out the new authorities' identity papers to continue ordinary life. He does so without accepting that the conquest was right.

Ivo and Tessa exchange a few ordinary words — not a miraculous restoration of everything lost. Lev continues keeping a place for Sela. The six shared tables are gone. Ivo briefly sets out the small repaired white table, then folds it away. He brings an old supper photograph to Tomas, and father and son drink tea in a room with space for people who will not arrive.

EPILOGUE · ENTRY 25

The Official Record

In the following winter, Sela remains at Eastmere. She has aged, but she has not withdrawn the distinction between evidence and the official descriptions of her. At another review, compliance with a distorted version of the record matters more than the underlying facts. Aron Vale continues pursuing her case; Lev continues visits and brings clothes and books when allowed.

The state's victorious archive celebrates the northern psychologists and declares the campaign complete. Another branch of the post-republic administration continues to confine a woman for repeatedly citing public evidence of that same campaign. Sela keeps a photograph of Ivo beside the little white table and waits for the next visit. No release, reversal of annexation, or grand reconciliation rescues the ending.

The official record calls the work complete. The people who survived it are still living with what was done.

WHO IS WHO

The characters and their places in the story

Sela Orin

Librarian and former history teacher; meticulously documents the loss of civic trust and published evidence of interference. Wrongfully confined at Eastmere.

Lev Orin

Sela's wife, a district-clinic physician. Defends Sela, challenges the detention and pays a professional price for refusing a false retraction.

Mara Arden

Sport organizer, Tomas's wife and Ivo's mother. A genuine grievance draws her into a movement where recognition can displace relationships.

Tomas & Dain Arden

Brothers whose practical competence and lingering affection cannot overcome their escalating political estrangement. Tomas is Ivo's father.

Ivo Arden

The boy who watches adults break their friendships and institutions; later a seventeen-year-old facing the identity papers of a country that replaced his own.

Tessa Fenn & Joren Fenn

Tessa is Ivo's childhood friend and fellow builder. Her father Joren is the mechanic whose popular program, The Honest Hour, rewards outrage.

Eva Maren & Samuel Rees

The journalist and editor of the Merrow Chronicle. Their investigation verifies the outside program but cannot persuade a divided public to use the facts together.

Irena Solvik

Psychologist directing Veyr's Civic Weather operation. She earns promotion and a medal, despite knowing that claimed outcomes exceed the evidence.

Niko Rahl & Director Kest

Niko is the careful northern analyst who becomes a whistleblower. Kest is the senior official who favors flattering certainty over warnings.

Oren Pell, Marek Voss & Aron Vale

Oren refuses to justify Sela's detention, Voss signs the second recommendation, and lawyer Aron repeatedly contests her confinement.

Nera Valen & Garran Holt

Leaders of Common Future and Renewal. Their constitutional contest becomes a crisis of public trust. Valen later supports the ambiguous emergency law.

Mrs. Kade, Lina & Ines

Mrs. Kade represents the neighborhood's shared history. Lina is Sela's fellow patient with real care needs; Ines is a humane nurse at Eastmere.

WHAT THE TITLE MEANS

The distance is not geography

The novel's central image is a set of folding tables. At the beginning, people can move between houses, argue, eat together and trust someone they disagree with to keep the lights on. At the end, the houses are still standing and the people still know how to repair things, but the social connections that made a neighborhood — and a republic — possible have been hollowed out.

The operation succeeds partly because it takes advantage of real wounds, real incentives and real choices. It is not an explanation that absolves everyone in Liora, nor a claim that a sufficiently powerful foreign operator can dictate every outcome. The northern bureaucrats also falsify the neatness of their own success. But the institutional injury is real: when trusted methods of disagreeing disappear, even a documented warning can be made into another weapon of division.

The tragedy of Sela and Lev gives the national collapse a particular human form. The woman who tried to preserve records is detained through false descriptions in a record; her wife cannot bring her home merely by being right. Irena receives an official honor for outcomes her staff cannot honestly attribute. The story refuses to exchange one family's suffering for an uncomplicated promise that everything will eventually be repaired.

This is fiction. Its warning concerns the fragility of shared civic life and the misuse of power — not the illegitimacy of political disagreement or of psychiatric healthcare.
